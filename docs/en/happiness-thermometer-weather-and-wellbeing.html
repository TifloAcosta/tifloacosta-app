The Happiness Thermometer: Why Some Love Heat, Others Cold, and Some Cheer When It Rains

One of those medical studies without a white coat

By Tony Acosta · TifloAcosta

23 October 2026

Hello, hello, lovely people.

Today I woke up with one of those questions that arrive uninvited, settle in your head and refuse to leave until you investigate. And all because of the most ordinary conversation imaginable: talking about the weather.

You know how it goes.

Two people meet. One says the heat is unbearable and the other declares it a glorious day. A third person wishes winter would hurry up, and somebody else insists that cold weather makes life miserable.

If it starts raining, the conversation ends up sounding like a residents' meeting about maintenance charges.

Joking aside, it got me thinking.

Why do some people adore the heat while others find it torture? Why does rain bring peace to some people and gloom to others? Why does one person love the wind while another gets into such a mood that it seems safer to keep your distance?

Is it taste? Habit? Personality? Or is there something in our bodies that predisposes us to prefer particular weather conditions?

And since curiosity, fortunately, is not yet taxable, I started investigating.

It turns out there is far more science here than you might expect. And a few surprises.

First things first: we don't all have the same thermostat

The human body is an extraordinarily complicated piece of machinery. We all need to keep our internal temperature within fairly narrow limits, around 37 degrees Celsius, though normal temperature varies between people and throughout the day.

To manage this we have a sort of biological thermostat in the brain, in a region called the hypothalamus.

Don't worry about the name. Think of a maintenance manager who works around the clock without charging overtime.

When it's hot, this manager turns on the cooling system. We sweat, blood flow close to the skin increases and the body tries to release excess heat.

When it's cold, the manager does the opposite: conserves heat, reduces its loss and, when necessary, makes us shiver.

So far, we work much alike.

But here comes the first big difference: we don't all perceive temperature with the same intensity or need exactly the same conditions to feel comfortable.

And I'm not making that up.

A scientific review published in 2019 identified factors including body composition, metabolism, adaptation to local climate and even the feeling that we can control the temperature around us.

Let me translate.

Two people can sit in the same living room with the thermometer showing 23 degrees. One is perfectly happy; the other is seriously thinking about putting on a jacket.

Neither necessarily has a problem.

Their bodies simply interpret the same environment differently.

So if someone says they're cold while you're sweating, don't immediately accuse them of exaggerating.

They may genuinely be cold.

Now, if they insist on turning the heating high enough to ripen a whole crop of Canary Islands bananas in minutes, that's a problem of domestic coexistence.

Metabolism has a say, too

Our bodies generate heat constantly.

Even when we sit doing practically nothing, we burn energy to keep the heart, brain, lungs and everything else running.

That energy activity is called metabolism.

And not everyone produces or retains heat in exactly the same way.

Muscle mass, body fat, physical activity and other factors can change our responses to temperature.

Medical conditions may alter those responses as well.

An underactive thyroid, for instance, can make someone unusually sensitive to the cold. An overactive thyroid may cause heat intolerance.

That doesn't mean summer lovers all have overenthusiastic thyroids or winter lovers urgently need blood tests.

That would be like diagnosing a disease because somebody prefers croquettes to lentils.

But when intolerance of heat or cold appears unexpectedly, especially alongside fatigue, weight changes, palpitations or other symptoms, it may deserve medical investigation.

So our first conclusion is that the body's physical workings may contribute to weather preferences.

The most interesting part, however, is still ahead.

The brain has opinions about the weather, too

We've talked about the body, but the brain doesn't just receive information about whether it's hot or cold.

It interprets what those sensations mean to us.

And that brings us to something fascinating: emotions.

Imagine two people listening to rain.

One remembers a wonderful childhood afternoon at home with family, raindrops against the windows and a sense of complete safety.

The other remembers having to walk miles to school in the rain, arriving drenched and spending the morning in wet shoes.

Could they develop different feelings about rain?

Of course.

The brain connects sensory experiences with memories and emotions. So it is reasonable to think that some weather preferences may be shaped by personal history.

But it would be scientifically wrong to claim that everyone who loves rain had a wonderful childhood, or that everyone who hates it must carry a childhood trauma.

Human preferences are much more complex.

Still, the same event can stir up quite different emotions depending on our experience.

Rain doesn't come with an emotion built in at the factory.

We supply that ourselves.

The experiment that showed we're not all alike

Now we come to one of the most curious studies I found.

In 2011, researchers published a study in the journal Emotion asking how the weather affects mood.

They tracked 497 adolescents, linking thirty days of daily mood reports with actual weather conditions. Then they looked for the same patterns in the adolescents' mothers.

And they discovered something worthy of a family gathering.

Some people felt better when it was warm and sunny.

Others felt worse under those exact conditions.

Some were especially unhappy on rainy days.

And some people's moods barely changed with the weather.

The researchers described four patterns: summer lovers, summer haters, rain haters and those largely unaffected.

Marvellous!

Science had just confirmed that one neighbour may stand on the balcony proclaiming, 'What a beautiful day!' while the next-door neighbour looks at exactly the same sky and thinks, 'What dreadful weather!'

Both may be entirely sincere.

The researchers also found some similarities between mothers and their children.

That suggests possible family influences, but doesn't prove that a love of sunshine or rain is directly inherited through our genes.

In other words, if a whole family adores the rain, we can't declare that they've inherited an umbrella chromosome.

Biology, shared habits and several circumstances together may all matter.

The main finding is that the weather doesn't affect everyone's emotions in the same way.

That was precisely the answer I was looking for when this conversation began.

Rain: wonderful for some, dreadful for others

Some people feel wonderful as soon as it starts to rain.

They enjoy the sound of raindrops, the smell of wet earth, the cooler air and the invitation to stay peacefully at home.

For others, rain means discomfort, restrictions, less light or obstacles to daily activities.

And don't forget that we often react not simply to the rain but to everything that comes with it.

Someone who loves reading by a window as the rain falls may associate it with calm and rest.

A person who works outdoors will probably have a much less romantic opinion.

Talk to that person about the poetry of raindrops on the window and they may tell you exactly where to put the poem.

Some people also enjoy the steady sound of rainfall because it helps mask other environmental noises.

But that doesn't make rain a medical treatment or mean the sound relaxes everybody.

Context matters.

A great deal.

Sunlight and the chemistry inside us

Here medicine has quite a bit to say.

Natural light helps regulate our biological clock.

That internal clock organises sleep, wakefulness and many other processes that repeat roughly every twenty-four hours.

Light, particularly in the morning, helps synchronise it.

Melatonin, a hormone associated with sleep regulation, is involved in this system.

Scientists also study serotonin, a chemical messenger involved, among other things, in regulating mood.

I'm not going to present serotonin as happiness juice, because the brain isn't that simple.

But seasonal changes in light can affect biological rhythms and some people's well-being.

There is even a recognised medical condition called seasonal affective disorder.

It is a form of depression that follows a recurring seasonal pattern.

The better-known form occurs in the darker months, but a less common pattern appears in spring or summer.

And that is particularly interesting.

We can't even claim that summer is automatically the season of happiness.

For some people, quite the opposite may be true.

Naturally, preferring cloudy days doesn't mean you have depression, and enjoying winter isn't a psychological disorder.

A preference and an illness are different things.

The important distinction is whether seasonal changes cause persistent symptoms that seriously affect daily life.

Heat can even change our temperament

I'm sure you know the situation.

It's unbearably hot. You've spent the night tossing and turning, and in the morning somebody asks a perfectly innocent question.

You answer as though a tax inspector has just delivered a very unpleasant letter.

Science has something to say here, too.

High temperatures can make sleeping harder.

A scientific review published in 2024 found that higher environmental temperatures are generally associated with poorer and shorter sleep.

And after a bad night's sleep, regulating emotions, concentrating and tolerating irritations become more difficult.

Extreme heat also puts serious physical strain on the body.

That's why enjoying warm weather isn't the same as enduring dangerous temperatures.

Liking heat doesn't protect you from heatstroke.

The body doesn't grant immunity in exchange for enthusiasm.

Although some studies link high temperatures to aggressive behaviour or mental-health problems, these associations are complicated. It would be wrong to claim that people who live in hot places are inherently more aggressive.

What we know is that excessive heat can impair rest and well-being.

And that may help explain why some people feel physically and emotionally better when temperatures fall.

Wind: the guest who never asks permission to come in

Wind is particularly curious.

Some people love feeling air on their faces, hearing the trees move or experiencing that sense of coolness and motion.

Others can't stand it.

There are several factors at work.

Wind changes our thermal sensation because, under certain conditions, it helps the body lose heat.

So we may feel much colder when it's windy, even with the same reading on the thermometer.

Wind can also lift dust, cause noise and discomfort and complicate everyday activities.

And there's another interesting medical angle.

Some people with migraine appear especially sensitive to particular weather changes.

A scientific review published in 2025 found associations between migraine attacks and factors such as temperature and atmospheric pressure.

That doesn't mean every gust of wind causes headaches or that everyone with migraine responds the same way.

Studies don't always agree, either.

But there is evidence that some people are sensitive to specific atmospheric changes.

If headaches repeatedly occur under certain weather conditions, discussing that pattern with a health professional may be worthwhile.

Quite a different matter is the belief that a particular wind automatically makes an entire population irritable.

We should be cautious.

There is no solid scientific proof that a particular wind necessarily causes bad moods in everyone exposed to it.

So don't make wind your universal excuse.

If somebody has behaved like an ogre for three days, we can't always blame the weather system.

What about people who seem unaffected?

They belong in this story too.

One of the most interesting results of weather research is that, across large groups, the average effect of weather on mood is often quite small.

A 2008 study in Emotion analysed data from 1,233 people.

Researchers found some links between weather conditions and emotions, but overall weather explained only a small fraction of day-to-day differences in mood.

Still, there were important differences between individuals.

Let me put that simply.

Imagine ten people in a room.

Five are delighted because it's raining, three are miserable and the other two couldn't care less because they're thinking about lunch.

If we calculate the average mood, rain may appear to have changed very little.

But that doesn't mean it didn't affect certain people strongly.

It means their responses differ and can cancel one another out statistically.

That's why what happens on average across a population isn't the same as what a particular person experiences.

Medicine and psychology don't only investigate what we share.

They also study what makes us different.

Just as well, otherwise we'd all be fighting over one supposedly perfect air-conditioning setting.

Are we born preferring a climate, or do we learn to prefer it?

This was another question I wanted to resolve.

And here I have to admit that science doesn't yet have a definitive answer.

Biological characteristics affect how well we tolerate temperature.

We also know that the body can partially acclimatise to particular environments.

Regular exposure to heat, for example, can trigger physiological adjustments that improve our ability to cope with it.

But getting used to a climate doesn't necessarily mean falling in love with it.

You can spend years in a rainy city and still hate umbrellas.

Personal history, habits, daily activities and emotional associations also matter.

Someone who links summer with holidays, family gatherings and freedom may feel differently from someone who associates it with sleepless nights, working under a blazing sun and being unable to rest.

The same happens with winter.

To one person it means cold and restrictions.

To another it means tranquillity, rest and well-being.

Our preferences may therefore emerge from a combination of physical, psychological and environmental influences.

There is no single known cause, nor any medical test that can determine which climate will make each person happy.

And expecting to find one may be far too simplistic.

The big question: can a particular climate make us happier?

Now we reach the heart of the matter.

Tolerating cold, enjoying rain or disliking wind is one thing.

But can those things affect our happiness?

The most reasonable answer, judging by available research, is that weather can influence well-being, though not in a universal or inevitable way.

Someone who suffers badly in the heat, sleeps poorly during hot months and avoids activities when temperatures rise might feel better in a cooler climate.

Someone who enjoys plenty of daylight, outdoor activities and mild or warm conditions may prefer an environment that makes those experiences easier.

But human happiness depends on many more things.

Relationships, health, finances, security, opportunities and satisfaction with life matter enormously.

There is no serious study proving that everybody would be happier under the sun, nor that we should all move somewhere with three hundred rainy days a year.

Pleasant weather can contribute to well-being.

But by itself it is unlikely to compensate for a life full of serious difficulties.

And enjoying a weather event doesn't mean wanting to live permanently under its effects.

Some people adore listening to a thunderstorm while sitting comfortably at home.

Having to work outside in it for eight hours would be quite another story.

Even the most passionate love affair with rain can turn cold when water starts leaking through the ceiling.

So what do we do with all this?

For a start, understand something simple that we don't always put into practice.

Other people's weather preferences need not match our own.

Someone who feels wonderful on a grey, rainy day isn't necessarily exaggerating or suffering from a gloomy personality.

Someone who says they need sunshine to feel comfortable isn't necessarily inventing a need.

And someone who can't stand wind may be describing very real physical discomfort.

Our bodies sense environmental conditions, our brains interpret them and our own experiences help give them meaning.

It's not a question of one person being right and another wrong.

We are different.

And that diversity is part of our biology too.

One precaution is important, however: preferring certain weather is perfectly normal; intense, persistent or newly developed symptoms related to heat, cold or particular seasons deserve medical assessment.

Not everything can be explained away by saying somebody is cold-natured, loves heat or has an unusual personality.

And not everything we feel when the weather changes can scientifically be blamed on the weather.

Our little final diagnosis, without a prescription or a white coat

After reviewing the research, I think that apparently trivial conversation had far more substance than it seemed.

Science confirms that individuals differ in how they experience weather and in the influence it may have on their moods.

Temperature regulation, physical characteristics, environmental adaptation, biological rhythms and psychological factors can all play a role.

We also know that certain illnesses can change how we tolerate heat, cold and other conditions.

And there is no single climate that is perfect for everyone.

But we still don't know every cause behind our preferences.

We therefore cannot claim that a gene makes us rain lovers, a hormone forces us to adore winter or a particular personality condemns us to hate wind.

Reality is far more interesting.

Human beings carry more than a thermometer in their bodies.

We also carry our personal histories, brains full of memories and our own ways of interpreting the world.

All of it matters when we look up at the sky, feel the breeze or listen to rain.

So there you are.

Next time someone declares, 'What a wonderful day!' while you think the weather is an absolute disaster, don't argue too much.

You may both have perfectly good reasons for feeling that way.

And if the argument happens at home because one person wants the windows open and the other wants them shut, remember that science can explain a great deal, but it has yet to find a truly effective treatment for domestic wars over the air-conditioning remote.

Well, lovely people, what started as a chat about the weather has taken us through biology, medicine, psychology and the corners of our memories.

All to discover that a seemingly silly question can have a fascinating scientific explanation.

There is one thing I'm still curious about.

If we asked a hundred people which weather conditions genuinely make them happy, what would they say?

I'm convinced some answers would leave even the weather presenter speechless.

And what about you? Heat, cold, rain, wind, or are you one of those who just want everyone to leave the thermometer alone?

Sending you all a hug.
