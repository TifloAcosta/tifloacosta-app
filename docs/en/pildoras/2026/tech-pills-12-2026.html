Samsung changes TalkBack in One UI 9: less fragmentation and an important development for blind Android users

We begin with Android and accessibility. This is not the flashiest technology story in the world, but it is the kind of change that can make a real difference in the daily life of someone who uses a phone with a screen reader.

Samsung is testing One UI 9, and one of the changes seen in the beta is especially relevant for blind users: Samsung appears to be leaving behind its own version of TalkBack and aligning with Google’s TalkBack. It does not arrive wrapped in artificial intelligence or in promises that your phone will understand you better than your own family. But it could have practical consequences. And in accessibility, practical matters more than spectacle.

What TalkBack is

TalkBack is the main screen reader for Android. It allows a blind person to use a phone through speech, gestures, sounds, vibration, keyboard input and braille. The screen reader describes what is on the screen and lets the user move through buttons, text fields, menus, apps, notifications and settings. On iPhone, the equivalent is VoiceOver.

This does not mean that every Android phone behaves in the same way. That is where part of the problem begins. Apple controls the operating system, the hardware, updates and VoiceOver, which usually gives a more consistent experience, although not a perfect one. Android works differently. Google develops Android and TalkBack, but each manufacturer adapts Android in its own way. Samsung has One UI, Xiaomi has HyperOS, and so on. That variety can be useful, but for a blind person it can also become a complication. Something that works one way on one phone may work differently on another, and if the screen reader itself is not exactly the same, the situation becomes even messier.

What Samsung had been doing until now

Samsung maintained its own version of TalkBack. It was not a completely separate screen reader. It was based on Google’s TalkBack, but Samsung adapted and distributed it inside its Galaxy phones. At one time this made sense, because Samsung has done significant work in accessibility and has added its own useful features over the years. But maintaining a separate version had a clear problem: it did not always move at the same pace as Google’s version.

That could mean that a Pixel user received a new TalkBack feature earlier, while a Samsung user had to wait for Samsung’s update cycle. And when we are talking about a screen reader, an update is not decoration. A new feature can make typing easier. A fix can solve a problem with certain apps. For someone who does not use a screen reader, that may sound secondary. For someone who depends on it, it is noticed immediately.

What may change with One UI 9

According to what has been reported by Accessible Android and observed in the beta, Samsung is replacing its own TalkBack version with Google’s version. If this remains in the final release, the most important practical change would be that TalkBack on Samsung phones could be updated through Google Play when Google publishes new versions, instead of depending on Samsung-specific packages or full One UI updates.

Put simply: the screen reader on Samsung phones would be more aligned with the common Android TalkBack experience. This could reduce differences, speed up access to new features and avoid forcing some users to look for workarounds to install Google’s version. A blind user should not have to perform strange technical gymnastics just to keep the screen reader updated on a modern phone. Setting up a phone, voices, permissions and notifications is already enough of an obstacle course without adding accessible plumbing to the job.

Why this could be good news

The first advantage is less fragmentation. In accessibility, fragmentation hurts more than in other areas. If one guide works for one model but not for another, training becomes harder. If an update reaches some phones and not others, support becomes harder. With this change, Samsung seems to be removing one layer of difference in an important area.

The second advantage is that new features may arrive sooner. If Google improves TalkBack and Samsung uses that version directly, the path should be cleaner, although technology always keeps a small clause hidden somewhere waiting to spoil the party.

The third advantage affects training. For those of us who teach assistive technology, explaining that Samsung’s TalkBack is not exactly the same as Google’s TalkBack, that some options may be missing, that one feature depends on One UI and another on Google Play, adds unnecessary weight for beginners. A more common base makes things easier. It does not make them perfect, but it helps.

The fourth advantage affects people who change phones. If someone moves from a Samsung phone to another Android device, TalkBack should behave more similarly. That matters, especially for users who do not want to live inside their phone settings as if they were solving a detective case.

What may not change

This change will not fix every accessibility problem on Android. There will still be differences between brands, models and versions. There will still be badly labelled apps. There will still be screens where TalkBack says “button” and the user has to guess whether that button continues, deletes or buys something. TalkBack can read what an app correctly exposes to the system, but if the app is poorly designed, it cannot magically turn it into an accessible app. The responsibility is not only on Google or Samsung. It is also on developers.

In the beta, users also noticed that the option to stop speech by moving the phone near the proximity sensor does not appear. Accessible Android points out that this option was also missing from Samsung’s TalkBack version and relates it to the fact that current models no longer use a dedicated physical proximity sensor. For many users this will not be decisive, although for others it may be a small annoyance depending on their habits.

A beta is not the final version

We are talking about a test version. It may change, improve, gain adjustments or break things, because betas have that lovely habit of reminding us that technology never misses a chance to humble us a little. What remains to be seen is whether the change reaches the final version of One UI 9, which models receive it, whether it starts only with the Galaxy S26 family or expands to more devices, and what happens to phones that remain on older versions.

In short: the news is interesting, but it should not be treated as if every Samsung phone will change tomorrow. We will need to hear from users who test it in everyday use. That is where accessibility proves itself. Not in a press release. Not in a video with dramatic music. In real life.

TalkBack, Jieshuo and the wider picture

TalkBack is the main reference point on Android and has improved a lot over the years: gesture navigation, touch exploration, braille support, an on-screen braille keyboard and customization options. It is not perfect, but it is far from the early days when Android was a much harder option for many blind users.

There is also Jieshuo, also known as Commentary Screen Reader or CSR, an alternative screen reader appreciated by advanced users who want speed and deep customization. For a beginner, TalkBack is usually the most logical path. For an advanced user, Jieshuo can be an interesting alternative. There is no universal winner. Each tool has its audience.

Samsung and Android compared with iPhone

VoiceOver is deeply integrated into Apple’s system. That gives consistency. Someone who learns VoiceOver on one iPhone will usually find a fairly stable experience on another. Android follows a different philosophy, with more freedom, more device variety, more price ranges and more customization. That can be very good, but it also means that the experience of a blind person may vary a lot depending on the device.

That is why choosing an Android phone for a blind person should not be based only on camera, battery or price. Updates, stability, accessibility and real user experience also matter. Otherwise the phone may have a spectacular camera and fast charging, while the blind user finds built-in apps that do not work properly with TalkBack. A luxury flashlight in a room with no door.

Conclusion

Samsung appears ready to adopt Google’s TalkBack more directly in One UI 9. If the change reaches the final version, it could be a practical improvement for many blind Android users: a more aligned TalkBack, faster updates, fewer differences between devices and more useful training materials.

It is not a revolution. It does not fix every problem and it does not guarantee that every app will be accessible. But it may help reduce fragmentation in a key piece of the system: the screen reader, which is the main door into the phone for many blind people. If that door updates better and behaves more consistently across devices, users benefit.

Seen with caution, this is a step in the right direction. Fewer separate versions, fewer delays, less confusion. It may not sound huge until we remember that, for someone using a phone with a screen reader, consistency is not a minor detail. It is part of access. And if a major company decides to simplify instead of adding another layer to the maze, we are not going to complain. That would be far too rare to waste.

iPhone and Android start talking to each other: Quick Share moves closer to AirDrop

We continue with another border that is starting to move: sharing files between iPhone and Android without building a small domestic nuclear plant just to send a photo or a document.

For years, this has been one of those absurd little scenes of modern life. You have two expensive phones, spectacular cameras, artificial intelligence everywhere and processors capable of things that recently sounded like science fiction. But you want to send a photo from one phone to the other, and suddenly we are back in the Stone Age: WhatsApp, Telegram, email, cloud links, cables, patience and a few words best not typed in a family publication.

Well, this may finally be changing in an important way. Google is expanding compatibility between Quick Share, Android’s nearby file sharing system, and AirDrop, Apple’s equivalent. Some Android phones can already appear as a destination when an iPhone has AirDrop open, and the aim is for this possibility to reach many more models during 2026.

We are not talking about a strange app, a trick for advanced users or a solution with more steps than a medieval recipe. The idea is much simpler: select a file, choose share, the nearby device appears and the transfer is accepted. This is how it should always have worked, but large technology companies sometimes discover the word interoperability only when a regulator places a chair, a table and a rulebook in front of them.

What exactly is happening

Until now, Android had Quick Share and Apple had AirDrop, each living inside its own garden. If you were using Apple devices, AirDrop worked very well. If you were using compatible Android phones, Quick Share also did the job. The problem arrived when crossing the border.

Google had already started this compatibility on some Pixel models, and it has then been seen moving beyond that. The important point now is that the expansion points to many more Android devices during 2026. Brands mentioned include Samsung, OPPO, OnePlus, Vivo, Xiaomi and Honor. That does not mean every phone from those brands will receive it at the same time, nor that every older model will automatically be included. On Android, as we know, updates depend on the manufacturer, the model, the system version and, sometimes, the alignment of the planets. But the direction is clear.

How it will work with iPhone

The most direct route will use Quick Share on Android and AirDrop on the iPhone. For the transfer to work, the iPhone must be visible for AirDrop receiving. The Android user selects the file, opens the share menu, chooses Quick Share and, if everything is supported, should be able to see the iPhone as a nearby device. The iPhone user then accepts the transfer, just as with a normal AirDrop transfer. This should also work with iPad or Mac when they are configured to receive.

The QR code alternative

Not every Android phone will have direct AirDrop compatibility, at least at first. For those cases, Quick Share will allow users to generate a one-time QR code. The other person scans it and accesses the file through a secure Google page. This is no longer a direct local transfer: Google’s cloud becomes the middleman. The files will be end-to-end encrypted and available for 24 hours.

It is not as elegant as having the two phones see each other directly, but it can solve many everyday cases. For sending photos, a PDF, an audio file or a document to someone using another system, it may be enough and much cleaner than the usual circus of forwarding things through messaging apps.

Why this also matters for blind users

When a feature reduces steps, screens, intermediate apps and unnecessary decisions, it usually also reduces friction with a screen reader. Until now, sharing files between iPhone and Android could force users through messaging apps, contact searches, compression settings, checks about whether a file was sent as an image or as a document, links and menus that are not always well labelled.

If the operating system allows sharing more directly, the experience can become simpler for everyone. In accessibility, that matters more than it may seem. Sometimes accessibility is not a major feature announced with heroic music. Sometimes it is simply removing three awkward steps from a daily task.

The role of Europe

The Digital Markets Act is pushing large platforms to open their systems and improve interoperability. This affects many areas: switching between iPhone and Android, data transfer, eSIM, passwords, messaging and photos. There is no need to build a monument to every form produced in Brussels, but in this particular case regulatory pressure has helped. This is not happening because large technology companies suddenly woke up overwhelmed by generosity. It is happening because there is clear pressure to prevent ecosystems from behaving like castles with drawbridges.

What should not be exaggerated

This is good news, but the rollout will be gradual, depend on specific models and may differ between direct AirDrop compatibility and the QR code option. It will also need to be tested with screen readers: VoiceOver on iPhone and TalkBack on Android. A feature existing does not always mean it is perfectly polished for blind users on day one. That would be lovely. So would every app labelling its buttons properly, and here we still are, excavating through “button, button, button” like interface archaeologists.

Conclusion

Quick Share and AirDrop compatibility is an important step toward something that should be normal: an iPhone and an Android phone exchanging files without a small home engineering project. Google has made a move, AirDrop is beginning to fit into this interoperability, Europe has pushed from the regulatory side, and users may benefit.

For those of us who use assistive technology, it will need to be checked in real use: whether the menus are clear, whether VoiceOver announces the devices properly, whether TalkBack allows the transfer to be completed without barriers. But the direction is good. After so many years of AirDrop on one side and Quick Share on the other, the fact that both ecosystems are starting to speak to each other is positive. And when a barrier falls, even a small one, people who use technology to work, communicate and move through the world notice it.

Ray-Ban Display opens up to other apps: an interesting opportunity for blind users, but still with many conditions

Now we move to Meta’s smart glasses. There is promise here, there are possibilities, and, as usual, there is also a good amount of fine print waiting in the corner pretending it has done nothing wrong.

Meta has taken an important step with its Ray-Ban Display glasses, smart glasses with a small display built into the lens. The company is opening the door for outside developers to create applications for them or adapt existing iPhone and Android apps. Until now, these glasses mostly lived inside what Meta wanted to offer. Now they are starting to become a platform. And when a device moves from being a closed product to allowing third-party apps, things change.

They do not always change for the better, because this also opens the door to useless apps, weekend experiments and unlabelled buttons, a plague humanity seems determined to preserve. But it also creates a real opportunity for practical tools. And if we are talking about blind people, this deserves attention.

What Meta has announced

Developers will be able to create experiences for Ray-Ban Display in two ways. The first is by extending mobile applications that already exist on iPhone or Android. An app already on the phone could add a specific part to be displayed or controlled through the glasses. The second is through web applications using familiar technologies such as HTML, CSS and JavaScript. There is no need to invent a new language or force developers through a secret ceremony with robes and passwords. This lowers the barrier to entry and may encourage ideas designed with accessibility in mind.

The display is not everything

For many people with some usable vision, the built-in display may be useful: directions, captions, messages or reminders without taking out the phone. But for a totally blind person, a display in the lens does not help much if the information remains only visual. Smart glasses having a display does not automatically make them accessible. If an app shows information in the lens but does not speak it, send it through audio or allow it to be controlled with clear gestures, its usefulness will be limited. Accessibility is not about placing information in front of someone’s eyes. It is about delivering that information through a channel the person can actually use.

Why the Neural Band may matter

One of the most interesting elements of Ray-Ban Display is the Meta Neural Band, a wristband that allows the glasses to be controlled through small hand and finger movements. For a blind person, having to find a tiny physical button or depend only on voice commands can be inconvenient. Voice is not always practical: there may be noise, private conversations or places where one does not want to talk to a pair of glasses as if negotiating with a technological goblin.

If the wrist gestures are reliable, easy to learn and discreet, they could allow quick actions: accept a reading, repeat an answer, pause a description or cancel an action. There is real potential. But, as always, execution is the key. If the gestures are confusing or the system response is unclear, the device may be more impressive in the promotional video than useful in the street.

What kinds of apps could make sense for blind users

Opening the platform to third parties could be very interesting if apps appear that are designed around real needs: visual recognition to describe a scene, read text, identify products, detect colors, recognize banknotes or explain a restaurant menu. More specialized navigation tools could also be useful, not only saying “turn right” but adding context: where the building entrance is, whether there are stairs, whether the access point is on the left or right. That would be a meaningful improvement over “somewhere around here,” the beloved unit of measurement used by maps when they want to ruin your afternoon.

Public transport, reading documents and labels, captions, conversation translation, discreet alerts and message summaries are other areas where these glasses could be genuinely useful, provided the information is delivered through audio and not only as text in the lens.

The difference between a curiosity and a useful tool

A useful app should not simply say “we have added compatibility with glasses.” That is not enough. The important questions are whether the apps are designed with blind people in mind. What information does the person receive? How is it delivered? Can it be used without looking at the screen? Are the controls clear? Is there feedback through voice or vibration? Does it work while walking? Does it respect privacy? Does it avoid forcing the user to take out the phone every two minutes or turning a simple task into a tour through menus?

Accessibility is not improvised at the end. It is not the icing on the cake. It is part of the recipe. When it is added after everything else is finished, it often becomes the kind of interface where VoiceOver announces “button” and nothing more. A classic. Few things describe interface failure better than a screen reader saying “button” with all the resignation in the universe.

One risk: too much information, not enough usefulness

Smart glasses have a dangerous temptation: placing information on top of the world. For a blind person, the equivalent can be an avalanche of audio: alerts, descriptions, notifications, directions and warnings all competing with each other. That is not accessibility. That is noise with a battery.

A good app should know when to speak, how much to say and when to stay quiet. If a blind person is walking down the street, they do not need a speech about everything around them. They need short, prioritized and useful information. It is not the same to say “there is a café on your right” as to give a full report on the building facade. The second type of information can be useful, but only if delivered at the right moment and without overwhelming the user. Intelligence is not only recognizing things. It is deciding what deserves to be said.

Privacy and availability

When we talk about glasses with cameras, microphones, AI and third-party apps, privacy cannot be left until the end. These glasses see and hear part of the environment, and if they are opened to outside applications, users will need to look carefully at what permissions are granted, what data is processed, where it is stored and who has access to it. For a blind person, there is an added layer: many useful functions, such as reading a document or describing a scene, depend on the camera analyzing the surroundings. Extremely useful, but also sensitive. Apps will need to explain this clearly, without endless legal text written so that no human reaches the end alive.

There is also a practical point: these glasses are not yet widely available in every market. In Europe, Meta has faced regulatory obstacles. Opening the platform to developers does not mean that any user in Spain, the United Kingdom or elsewhere can buy the glasses tomorrow, install third-party apps and go out testing them in the street. The rollout, the countries, the conditions and the available features will all need to be followed. We know this movie: a feature is announced in the United States, the videos look wonderful, people get excited, and then in other countries the word “coming soon” lasts so long it should be included in a will.

Conclusion

Opening Ray-Ban Display to third-party applications could be one of those technical moves that ends up having very practical consequences. For blind users, the potential is real: visual recognition, text reading, navigation, communication, contextual assistance and gesture control. But potential guarantees nothing.

If applications are designed only for people looking at a screen, these glasses may be interesting for sighted users and far less useful for blind people. If developers understand that information must also arrive through audio, vibration and accessible gestures, this could become a promising platform. The news is not “the solution has arrived.” The news is that a door has opened. Now we need to see who enters, what they build and whether they remember that accessibility is not a favor or a decorative section in a press release.

Google Drive, Gmail and Photos: Google’s free storage is starting to come with conditions

The next story affects a huge number of people, because Google Drive, Gmail and Photos are part of everyday life on iPhone, Android, Windows, Mac and the web. When Google changes storage, it is worth looking closely at what is moving.

For years, a free Google account came with 15 GB of storage. It was not a gift sent down from the heavens, let us not get poetic about a multinational company, but it was useful: email, documents, photos, backups, shared files. A reasonably organized digital life, as long as one did not save absolutely everything as if the internet were an infinite storage room.

Now Google is testing an important change. Some new accounts are no longer receiving the usual 15 GB immediately. In certain regions, some people are seeing new accounts start with only 5 GB. To unlock up to 15 GB, Google asks the user to add and verify a phone number.

This does not mean that existing accounts are about to lose storage tomorrow. There is no need to run around deleting photos as if throwing furniture out of a window before a move. But it does mean that Google is touching a very sensitive part of its ecosystem, and that affects many people whether they use iPhone, Android, Windows, Mac or whatever device they have at home still surviving with dignity.

It is not only Gmail: it is the whole Google account

A common misunderstanding is to think this change affects only Gmail. Not exactly. Free storage is shared between Gmail, Google Drive and Google Photos. If an account has 15 GB, those 15 GB are not just for email. They also include Drive files, documents, spreadsheets, presentations, photos and videos, attachments, trash and spam.

Google Drive is not just another app. For many people, it is where personal documents, training material, work files, certificates, shared folders, backups and resources live. For many blind people, Drive is also widely used to share accessible documents, store training materials, move files between phone and computer or receive resources through links. Changing free storage conditions is not a minor adjustment.

What Google is testing

What is being seen in this test is that a new account may start with 5 GB of free storage. If the user adds and verifies a phone number, the account may unlock up to 15 GB without paying. Google presents this as a way to improve account security, support recovery and reduce abuse. And part of that makes sense: free accounts are also used for spam, automation and disposable accounts.

But there is another obvious reading: Google is tightening the conditions around free storage. It is not taking 15 GB away from everyone, but it is making those 15 GB less automatic for some new accounts. Before, the number was clear. Now it starts to look like a number with conditions attached. And when a company moves from “you have 15 GB” to “you can have up to 15 GB,” it is worth paying attention.

Who may be affected

For now, the key point is this: the test affects new accounts in selected regions. There is no solid indication that Google is reducing storage for older accounts. If you already have an account with 15 GB, this news does not appear to mean that you will wake up tomorrow with only 5 GB.

But it may affect people creating new accounts: someone opening an account to use Drive, someone creating a secondary account to organize documents, a family setting up an account for an older person, a student starting a course, an Android user setting up a new phone or an iPhone user who uses Drive to share files. In those cases, this new condition may appear.

Why 5 GB can fill up very quickly

Five gigabytes may sound enough if one thinks only about text documents. But as soon as photos, videos, audio files, email attachments and backups enter the picture, it can become small very quickly. For a user who uses Drive as their main cloud storage, 5 GB is not much. When the space fills up, practical problems appear: new files may not upload, Google Photos backups may stop, and Gmail may have trouble sending or receiving messages. This is not a tiny inconvenience. For many people, running out of Google storage means being blocked at exactly the moment they need to receive an important file, because technology has a particularly miserable sense of timing.

The sensitive part: the phone number

From a security point of view, it makes sense that Google wants accounts associated with recovery methods. But there is also a legitimate concern: not everyone wants to hand over a phone number in exchange for storage. Some users do not want to link more personal data to large platforms. Some use secondary accounts for privacy or organization. Some change numbers, use prepaid cards or share a family phone. Making two thirds of the free storage depend on providing a phone number is not a small detail, even if Google presents it as security and recovery.

Practical recommendations

The most sensible approach now is to do three things. First, check how much storage your Google account is using, not only Drive but also Gmail and Photos, because everything shares the same quota. Second, clean what you do not need: emails with huge attachments, duplicated videos, old files, trash and spam, which also occupy space until finally emptied. Third, do not use a single cloud service as the only place where your important information lives. Google Drive is convenient, but no cloud should be the only home for essential files. Personal documents, courses and important materials should have a copy elsewhere.

The cloud is useful. The cloud is not magic. The cloud also changes its conditions when the company controlling it decides the time has come. What a shock: a company looking after its business. Nobody could have predicted that, except anyone who has ever read a bill.

For blind users, the impact can be even more practical

In our community, Google Drive is widely used to share materials: courses, manuals, accessibility documents, audio files, guides, forms and shared folders. For a blind person, the cloud is not only storage. It is often a tool for work and independence: having the same file on the phone and computer, receiving documentation without depending on USB drives, sharing materials with others and accessing content from different screen readers.

When an account fills up, error messages are not always clear. Some people do not understand why Gmail is not receiving mail, why Drive is not uploading files or why Photos has stopped backing up. The problem is shared storage, but the warning may appear in different services. That creates confusion, and technological confusion always finds a way to appear on a Sunday afternoon, when nobody has the energy to investigate anything.

Conclusion

Google Drive, Gmail and Google Photos are part of daily life for millions of people. Google is now testing a change that may reduce the initial storage of some new accounts to 5 GB unless a phone number is verified to unlock up to 15 GB. As far as is currently known, this does not affect existing accounts. But it does point to a trend: free storage is becoming less automatic and more conditional.

For any platform, and especially for blind people who often use the cloud to receive, organize and share accessible materials, the recommendation is to be better organized. Check occupied space, delete what is unnecessary, remember that Gmail, Drive and Photos share the same quota, and do not depend on a single cloud service for everything important. Google Drive will remain useful. But it is increasingly wise to use it with more planning. Because when a company says “up to 15 GB,” the phrase may be small, but the consequences may not be.

“Hey Siri, remove the tree”: the latest odd iPhone curiosity

We close the news section with a lighter curiosity, but one absurd enough to deserve a place here. Siri once again reminds us that voice assistants can help a lot, although sometimes they interpret reality as if they had eaten cables for breakfast.

There are days when one thinks technology is advancing at great speed. Then Siri hears “remove the tree” and understands that you want to turn off the iPhone. At that point, any attempt to take the future too seriously becomes difficult.

In recent days, a curious iPhone behaviour has been circulating among users: if you say “Hey Siri, remove the tree,” on some devices Siri responds by asking whether you want to turn off the iPhone. Just like that. No special Shortcut is needed, no automation has to be configured, and no extra app is required. The phrase has no technical meaning, it is not an official Apple command and it certainly does not seem to belong to a secret manual for digital gardeners. But it happens for some users. And that is exactly why it has gone viral.

What is happening

Normally Siri would be expected to say it does not understand the request, ask what tree you mean or send you to a web search, which is the elegant exit voice assistants use when they have no idea what you are asking for. But in several videos shared by users, Siri interprets the phrase as something related to turning off the phone.

There is an important detail: Siri does not turn off the iPhone directly without asking. It requests confirmation. This matters, because we are not talking about someone walking past you, saying a magic phrase and switching off your phone like a fairground trick. Even so, the reaction is still strange.

Why it may happen

Apple has not officially explained why Siri understands that phrase in that way. The most likely explanation is that Siri is wrongly associating the phrase with a power-off command, either because of speech recognition, phonetic similarity or some internal combination that may only be understood by whoever designed that part of the system. And perhaps not even by that person, because modern software sometimes seems to be raised by keyboard-wielding goblins.

Since iOS 16, Siri can handle the command to turn off the iPhone, with confirmation. The odd part here is that “remove the tree” seems to fall into that same category. It does not look like a feature. It looks like a misunderstanding. That is worth saying, because the internet is very good at turning any curious bug into a “hidden trick” or “feature Apple does not want you to know.” Then people try half of those tricks and discover the real secret was losing ten minutes of life.

An anecdote, not a revolution

This story will not change anyone’s life. But it is interesting because it shows something that people who use voice assistants already know well: Siri, like other assistants, does not always understand what we say. And sometimes it not only fails, it fails with admirable confidence. You say something absurd and the system answers as if it had perfectly understood your deepest intentions.

For those of us who use iPhone with VoiceOver, Siri can be a useful tool: opening apps, calling someone, setting alarms, checking information, sending messages or performing quick tasks without touching the screen. But we also know it is not wise to trust every interpretation blindly. Voice assistants are useful. They are not infallible.

Conclusion

“Hey Siri, remove the tree” remains one of those amusing iPhone oddities that is worth mentioning more as a curiosity than as a useful feature. It is not an official function, it is not a documented command and it does not seem to be something Apple intentionally designed. It is one of those strange speech-recognition outcomes that reminds us that artificial intelligence and voice assistants still have days of inspiration and days of complete confusion.

The good part is that Siri asks for confirmation before turning off the iPhone. The funny part is that, for some reason, the tree seems to have more power than we expected. The practical point is the usual one: when using Siri, listen carefully to what it is asking you to confirm before accepting. One thing is asking the assistant for help. Another is letting it interpret forest poetry as a shutdown command.

As always, the purpose is to bring technology closer from a practical point of view, especially for those of us who use screen readers, accessibility tools and devices that are not a luxury, but part of daily independence.

If you are already on the English-language Canal TifloAcosta WhatsApp list, you only need to ask for the material whenever you are interested in receiving it.

If you are not yet on the list and would like to receive future content, courses, guides, news and materials related to technology and accessibility, the process is simple: send me a WhatsApp message at the Canal TifloAcosta contact number with your first name, last name, where you are writing from, and the word join. That lets me know who you are, where you are participating from and that you want to be added to the community.

Thank you for reading, sharing these materials and helping technology become a little less like a maze and a little more like a tool. Surviving updates already takes enough merit without every button being presented to us as if humanity had just discovered fire.
