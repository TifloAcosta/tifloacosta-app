Airplane Mode on your phone: it is not just for flying

We begin with something simple and familiar that often goes unnoticed: Airplane Mode. Beyond flights, it can be useful for rest, concentration, battery saving, travel, recordings and accessibility workflows.

Airplane Mode was born as something associated with flights, but it has remained on our phones as a more useful tool than many people realize. Some users only activate it when they board a plane, because they are asked to do so before take-off. In daily use, however, it can help save battery, avoid distractions, cut connections when needed, solve occasional network problems and use the phone with more control.

The basic idea is simple: the phone remains on, but it disconnects from the mobile network and, depending on the device and settings, also from Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. On current phones, both iPhone and Android, it is possible to turn on Airplane Mode and then turn Wi-Fi or Bluetooth back on if necessary. This matters, because we do not always want to isolate the phone completely. Sometimes we only want to cut off the mobile network, avoid calls or prevent the phone from using mobile data.

It is also worth separating Airplane Mode from turning the phone off. If you switch the phone off, you stop being able to use it. If you turn on Airplane Mode, you can still use many functions: read downloaded documents, listen to saved music, use notes, check files, record audio, take photos, review a guide, use the clock, keep an alarm active or work with apps that do not need a connection. The phone remains a tool, but it stops constantly knocking on the door of the internet and the telephone network, which already make quite enough noise in human life.

One of its most practical uses is reducing interruptions. Silencing the phone is not always enough. Even if it does not make a sound, notifications, messages, emails, app calls and alerts continue to arrive and can break concentration. If you need to write, study, record audio, prepare a publication, read with VoiceOver or TalkBack, or review a document without interruptions, Airplane Mode can be more effective than simply lowering the volume.

It can also help with sleep. Many people use the phone as an alarm clock and do not want to turn it off completely. In that case, Airplane Mode keeps the alarm and other local functions available, while preventing calls, messages and notifications during the night. That said, if you need to receive urgent calls from a family member, caregiver or medical service, Do Not Disturb or Focus modes with exceptions may be a better option. Airplane Mode is more drastic.

Battery life is another clear advantage. A phone uses power maintaining connections, searching for coverage, synchronizing data and receiving alerts. In an area with poor signal, battery use can increase because the phone keeps trying to find a network, as if it were searching for the meaning of life in a coverage bar. Turning on Airplane Mode reduces that background activity and can help preserve battery when the charge is low.

It can also help the phone charge a little more efficiently. It does not work miracles, because charging speed depends on the charger, cable, battery, temperature and device itself. But if you turn on Airplane Mode and keep the screen off, the phone consumes less power while charging. In a hurry, that can make a small difference.

One of the most useful tricks is using it to restart connections. Sometimes the phone shows coverage but cannot browse, remains without service, does not recover mobile data after leaving a garage, switches poorly between Wi-Fi and mobile data, or seems stuck on a connection that is not working. Before restarting the whole phone, you can turn on Airplane Mode for a few seconds and turn it off again. Very often, that is enough for the device to search for the network again from scratch.

It can also help with occasional Wi-Fi or Bluetooth problems. If headphones, a braille display, a watch or an accessory starts behaving strangely, toggling Airplane Mode can be a quick test before doing something heavier, such as unpairing devices or restarting the phone.

For travel, Airplane Mode remains very useful even when you are not inside a plane. If you go abroad and do not want to use mobile data or receive unpleasant roaming surprises, you can activate Airplane Mode and then turn on Wi-Fi only. That lets you connect in hotels, airports, homes or places with known networks, without letting the phone use the mobile network. On phones with dual SIM or eSIM, this can be especially important, because sometimes one line may connect where it should not if settings are not reviewed properly.

For blind and low-vision users, one detail deserves attention: Airplane Mode does not necessarily prevent the use of Bluetooth. You can turn it on and then turn Bluetooth back on to continue using headphones, compatible hearing aids, keyboards, watches or braille displays. This makes it possible to read documents, listen to VoiceOver or TalkBack, control the phone with a braille display or use accessories without keeping the mobile network active.

Another useful case is recording without interruptions. If you are going to record a voice note, an explanation, an accessibility demonstration, an audio piece for publication or a video, Airplane Mode prevents calls and many unexpected notifications. For those of us who use or explain screen readers, this matters: a notification can interrupt the recording, expose private information or make the screen reader say something that had no business being there.

It also helps produce cleaner screenshots. When preparing a guide or explanation, you do not want a notification appearing in the middle of the process. Turning on Airplane Mode before taking screenshots or recording the screen reduces that risk. It will not organize the screen for you, because let us not ask miracles from a little airplane icon, but it does prevent quite a few interruptions.

It can also be useful when you lend your phone to someone for a moment. If someone needs to look at a photo, play for a while, review a document or use an offline app, Airplane Mode prevents calls, messages and notifications while that person has the device in hand. It is not a complete privacy measure and does not replace Guided Access, parental controls or app locks, but as a quick gesture it can help.

The same applies when a child is going to use the phone with an offline app, downloaded music or saved content. Airplane Mode can prevent accidental calls, mobile data use or unexpected access to the internet. If Wi-Fi is turned back on afterwards, part of that protection disappears, so it is important to know exactly what is being allowed.

Airplane Mode is also practical for reading or listening to downloaded content: books, documents, courses, podcasts, music, videos, maps or notes. On trips, in waiting rooms, hospitals, clinics or areas with poor coverage, having material downloaded and turning on Airplane Mode can turn the phone into a stable tool without depending on signal.

Still, Airplane Mode should not be sold as a universal solution. It has clear limits.

If it is enabled and Wi-Fi has not been turned back on, you will not receive regular phone calls, SMS messages or mobile data. If you are waiting for an important call, it is not a good idea to use it without thinking. If you need alerts, urgent notices or phone location features, you should also be careful. A phone in Airplane Mode may be harder to locate if it is lost, because it has fewer ways to communicate.

It is not a complete privacy measure either. It reduces connections, but it does not replace a proper review of permissions, location settings, installed apps, cloud backups and security settings. It helps disconnect; it does not turn the phone into a safe.

  • Focus without turning the phone off.
  • Sleep without notifications.
  • Save battery in critical moments.
  • Charge the phone with less background consumption.
  • Force reconnection when mobile data or coverage fails.
  • Avoid roaming while travelling.
  • Use Wi-Fi without mobile network access.
  • Use Bluetooth with headphones, hearing aids or braille displays.
  • Record audio or video without interruptions.
  • Take cleaner screenshots.
  • Lend the phone for a moment with fewer interruptions.
  • Read or listen to downloaded content without depending on the internet.

The key is to use it according to the situation. If you want complete isolation, turn on Airplane Mode and leave Wi-Fi and Bluetooth off. If you only want to avoid the mobile network, turn on Airplane Mode and then turn Wi-Fi back on. If you need accessories, turn Bluetooth back on. If you need to remain reachable for specific people, Focus modes or Do Not Disturb with exceptions may be more appropriate.

Airplane Mode began as a requirement for flying, but in daily life it can be a simple tool for regaining some control over the phone. It does not switch the device off, but it does switch off much of the noise. And sometimes that is exactly what we need: for the phone to remain useful, without behaving as if the entire planet had the right to enter our pocket every five minutes.

Google presents Gemini Omni: an AI for creating and editing video by talking to it

After a simple everyday tool, we move to one of those artificial intelligence developments that should be watched with interest and caution. Google wants video creation and editing to become increasingly conversational.

Google has presented a new artificial intelligence technology called Gemini Omni, and it deserves attention because it is not only about generating videos from scratch. The main idea is that users can create and edit video using text, images, audio or even another video as a starting point.

Put simply, we are no longer talking only about writing a sentence and waiting for AI to generate a clip. Google wants video to be modified step by step, by having a conversation with the tool. For example, asking it to change the background, replace an object, transform the visual style, alter the camera angle or keep a scene coherent while changes are made.

This matters because until now many AI video tools have worked more like a creative lottery: you wrote a prompt, crossed your fingers and, if the result was useless, started again. With Gemini Omni, Google is trying to move closer to controlled editing. Not perfect, not magical, but more like giving instructions about a specific scene.

The first version is called Gemini Omni Flash. According to Google, it can work with different types of input: text, images, video and audio. It can also apply visual references, change characters or objects, transform motion, follow specific styles and maintain some continuity from one edit to the next.

This is one of the key points: the tool does not only generate; it also edits. It can take an existing video and modify it. That opens interesting possibilities for creators, educators, communicators, trainers and people who need to prepare audiovisual material without mastering complex editing programs. At least in theory, because then the real interface arrives and reminds us that humanity still designs buttons as if hiding coins on a beach.

Gemini Omni is integrated with Google Flow, Google’s tool for AI-based audiovisual creation. Flow already works with models such as Veo, which generates video with audio, and now adds more advanced capabilities for editing scenes through natural language. These functions are also expected to appear in the Gemini app and in YouTube Shorts.

Not everything is open to everyone. Google indicates that a Google AI subscription is required and that functions may vary depending on the plan, country, language and regional rollout. This means it should not be announced as a universal free tool. It may first reach certain users, territories or paid tiers, and that reading should be made with an international audience in mind, not only users of Spanish.

It is also important to distinguish Gemini Omni from Veo. Veo is Google’s model specialized in generating video, including audio, sound effects, ambience and dialogue. Gemini Omni goes further in the multimodal and conversational side: it allows the use of different references and more flexible video editing, not only asking for a new scene from text.

Another important point is transparency. Google says that content created or edited with Gemini Omni in Gemini, Flow or YouTube will include SynthID digital watermarking and C2PA credentials. This aims to help identify when a video has been generated or modified with AI. It does not solve every problem, but it does point in a necessary direction: if convincing video is becoming easier to fabricate, it must also become easier to know when we are looking at artificial or altered content.

The practical reading is this: Google is not presenting just another generator of pretty videos. It is trying to build a new way of editing video through conversation. That could change content creation significantly, especially for people who do not use professional editing programs or need to produce quick pieces.

Still, caution is needed. The technology promises a lot, but we will have to check real quality, limits, availability, cost, languages, usage rights and tool accessibility. For a community that is increasingly international, one question cannot be left out: will blind and low-vision users be able to operate Flow, Gemini or these editing functions independently with a screen reader, in English, Spanish and other real-world contexts? In the sources consulted, I have not found a clear explanation from Google on that specific point.

So, yes, this is good news, but with qualifications. Gemini Omni may become a powerful tool for AI video editing, especially if it delivers on what it promises: working from different types of input, modifying existing scenes and maintaining coherence across changes. But until it is tested in real conditions, especially with assistive technologies, it is better to describe it as an opportunity rather than a finished solution.

The direction of travel is clear: it will become less necessary to know how to manage a complex timeline in order to create or modify a video. The hard part will be ensuring that this ease of use is not limited to people who can see the screen and visually understand what is changing. If the future of audiovisual editing is built around conversation with AI, that future should also be usable by people who do not edit by looking, but by listening, reviewing, describing and controlling each step in another way.

WhatsApp is preparing major changes: more privacy, better groups and less dependence on phone numbers

We continue with WhatsApp, an app that seems simple until you manage groups, communities, privacy, phone numbers and features that arrive in stages as if they were hidden pieces in a treasure hunt.

WhatsApp has been moving for some time in a fairly clear direction: more user control, more tools for groups and less direct exposure of the phone number. Some of these functions are still in beta, so they should not be presented as if they were already available to everyone. But together they show where the application is heading.

One of the most interesting developments concerns messages that disappear after being read. Until now, disappearing messages used fairly simple time periods: 24 hours, 7 days or 90 days from the moment they were sent. What WhatsApp is now testing is different: the timer would begin when the recipient opens the message.

The idea is straightforward. If someone receives a message configured to disappear after being read, the clock starts when the person sees it. Short periods such as 5 minutes, 1 hour or 12 hours are being tested. This changes the usual habit of opening something, reading it and replying later. With this system, looking at the message already has consequences: from that moment, it starts to expire.

It is not a function intended for every message or every conversation, but it may make sense when something is shared that should not remain available for days. Even so, it is worth remembering that a disappearing message does not mean it is impossible to preserve. There can always be screenshots, photos taken with another device or any other human acrobatics proving that absolute privacy does not exist, however attractive an app’s little lock icon may be.

Another interesting development concerns groups. WhatsApp is testing automatic welcome messages for new members. Administrators will be able to prepare a text that appears when someone enters a group for the first time. It can explain what the group is about, basic rules, what may be posted or how the new member should introduce themselves.

This may seem small, but in large groups it can reduce a lot of noise. Anyone who has managed a community knows that some questions are repeated again and again, as if every new member arrived from an island with no memory. A well-written welcome message can avoid part of that chaos and give context from the first minute.

The function would not be enabled by default. Administrators would configure it from the group settings. According to what has been seen in beta, there would be a single shared message for the group, not a different one for each administrator. That makes sense: if every administrator could add their own welcome sermon, the solution would become heavier than the problem.

This function also fits with other changes WhatsApp has been testing so that joining a group is not like entering a room where everyone has been talking for three hours and nobody explains anything. There has also been discussion of shared recent message history, allowing new members to see a limited part of what happened before they joined, provided administrators allow it.

Perhaps the most relevant medium-term change is usernames. WhatsApp has depended on the phone number as the main identity for years. That has been convenient, but also problematic. To talk to someone, it has often been necessary to give them your number directly. In open groups, buying and selling, services, communities or occasional contacts, that is not always a good idea.

Meta has already documented changes related to usernames and business-scoped IDs. The idea is that, during 2026, WhatsApp will begin allowing users and businesses to communicate through usernames, without always having to show the phone number. The number would still be needed to create and verify the account, but it would not necessarily have to be visible in every interaction.

This could matter for spam and unwanted contacts. It will not eliminate the problem, because spam always finds a crack, like damp in a wall. But it could reduce direct exposure of a personal number and add another layer of control. If a person can share a username instead of a phone number, they have more room to decide who can find them and how.

There has also been talk of additional mechanisms, such as keys associated with usernames, to prevent anyone from contacting you simply by guessing your identifier. We will have to see how WhatsApp finally implements it, because a good idea badly executed can become another source of confusion, especially for less experienced users.

Among the smaller changes, interface adjustments, quick reactions for status updates and changes to chat organization are also appearing. They are less striking details, but they form part of the same movement: WhatsApp is trying to become less of a simple conversation list and more of a full platform for communication, communities, businesses, status updates and services.

The general reading is clear. WhatsApp is trying to solve three problems it has been carrying for years: disorganized groups, limited privacy around phone numbers and little control over how long a message remains visible.

Are these good news? In principle, yes. But with the usual caution. Many of these functions are in beta, may change before reaching the general public and may take quite some time to appear on all accounts, depending on country, platform, language, account type and rollout calendar. WhatsApp rolls out new features gradually, and sometimes a function seems just around the corner and then spends months wandering through the corridors.

For those of us who use WhatsApp in large communities, broadcast groups, coordination or user support, these developments may be useful if they arrive well implemented. A good welcome message can greatly improve entry into a group. Usernames can improve privacy. Messages that disappear after reading can provide more control in specific conversations.

What matters is that all of this is clear, accessible and easy to manage. Because a privacy function nobody understands becomes decoration. And a group administration tool that is not used properly becomes another lost button inside settings, that cemetery where so many good ideas go to take a nap.

Apple keeps moving pieces toward a glucose sensor without finger pricks

We close with health and technology. Apple continues working on one of those functions that should not be announced lightly, because measuring glucose without finger pricks is not a decorative watch feature: it can affect important decisions in many people’s daily lives.

Some technologies sound good in a presentation, and then there are technologies that, if they work well, can change daily life for many people. Non-invasive glucose measurement clearly belongs to the second group.

According to recent information published by Mark Gurman in Bloomberg, Apple has made internal changes to the team working on its long-awaited non-invasive glucose sensor. The project, which has reportedly been in discreet development for years, is now said to be led by Zongjian Chen, an engineer with a long background in Apple’s semiconductor area.

This is not an official Apple announcement. It does not mean that the next Apple Watch will measure glucose without finger pricks. That should be said from the beginning to avoid feeding expectations that later crash into reality, a sport we already practice far too often in technology.

The important point is another one: the project is still alive.

For years, there has been talk about Apple possibly measuring blood glucose without piercing the skin. The idea would be to use optical sensors capable of analyzing how light behaves as it passes through tissue, in order to estimate glucose levels without drawing blood and without depending on a sensor inserted under the skin.

Put like that, it sounds simple, but it is not. Measuring glucose is not like counting steps or estimating calories. An incorrect reading can influence decisions about food, medication or insulin. That is why this function cannot be “more or less right”. It must be reliable, validated and accepted by regulators.

In fact, the FDA warned in 2024 that people should not use smartwatches or smart rings that claim to measure glucose by themselves without piercing the skin if they have not been authorized for that purpose. That warning matters, because today some devices show glucose data coming from external sensors, but that does not mean the watch itself is directly measuring glucose.

The difference is enormous. One thing is for a watch to be a convenient screen for checking data. Another very different thing is for the watch to become the instrument that obtains the measurement.

For that reason, this news should be taken as a positive sign, not as an immediate promise. Apple does not appear to have abandoned the project, and the leadership change may indicate that it wants to strengthen a particularly complex technical area. But there is no date, no announced product and no guarantee that this function will arrive soon.

If Apple one day manages to integrate reliable non-invasive glucose measurement into an Apple Watch, it would be one of the most important health functions ever to reach a consumer device. It would matter for people with diabetes, for those who need frequent monitoring and also for people who might benefit from early alerts or preventive tracking.

But in this area, it is not enough for the technology to be brilliant. It has to be safe, accurate and accessible. And when we talk about accessibility, we also mean that all information can be consulted properly with screen readers, that alerts are clear, that data is understandable and that the experience does not depend on seeing pretty graphs on a screen.

The news, therefore, is good. Not because it announces an imminent arrival, but because it confirms that Apple continues working on a technology that could have a major impact if it is done well.

And in health, especially when we talk about glucose, that is what matters: not arriving first, but arriving when the data is reliable enough for a person to trust it.
