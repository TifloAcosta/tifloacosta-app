Technology, privacy, artificial intelligence and accessibility, without the trade-show wrapping

Hello, hello, beautiful people.

Tony here, with issue 16 of the year, ready to tell you about four things that, at first glance, do not seem to have much to do with one another: WhatsApp finally rolling out usernames, Apple clashing with Europe over Siri AI, a serious leak around the next iPhone, and a look at how sport is finally beginning to be told also for those of us who do not see it.

But as I was writing, I realized that the four stories pull on the same thread. They are not really about new buttons or chips with grand-sounding names. They are about who decides, who controls your data, who can choose and who is left outside when something starts to matter in everyday life.

As always, I am not here to chase headlines like someone running after a bus that has already closed its doors and pulled away. I prefer to stop, look under the hood and tell you what is there, what it may mean for you and what is worth looking at calmly before we get carried away by excitement, anger or, quite simply, well-dressed marketing.

Because one thing is for a feature to sound modern in a keynote, and quite another for it to be useful, accessible, respectful of your privacy and not force you to memorize twenty new menus just to do what you already did before. Technology, when it makes the effort, can help enormously. And when it does not, it can become astonishingly heavy going.

WhatsApp launches usernames: what they are, what they are for and what you should know

I will start with WhatsApp because, whether we like it or not, for most of us it is almost a digital prosthesis by now. And because, while I was writing these lines, the news stopped being a rumor: on June 29, WhatsApp began allowing username reservations, ahead of the full launch of the feature. So we are arriving with the ink still fresh.

Contacting someone without showing your number

The idea is easy to explain, even if it has taken years to arrive: until now, if someone wanted to write to you on WhatsApp, they needed your phone number. Convenient for almost twenty years, no doubt, but with an obvious crack: that same number is used for banking, two-step verification, medical appointments, the plumber calling you back and half your life besides. You do not always feel like handing it to a group you have just joined or a company you only want to ask a quick question.

With usernames, Meta gives you the option to share an identifier instead of your number. Someone will be able to write to you by knowing that name, without your phone appearing anywhere. Your number is still there, holding the account together underneath, but it stops being the first thing you show to a stranger.

Do not confuse it with your profile name

This is worth keeping clear. The profile name, the one you already had, can be repeated thousands of times: WhatsApp is full of Antonios, Marias and Pedros. The username, on the other hand, is unique. If you reserve @tonyacosta, nobody else on the planet will be able to use it while you keep it.

In other words: the profile name is how you introduce yourself. The username is your contact address inside the app.

It can include letters, numbers, periods and underscores, but with rules: it cannot be only numbers, it cannot look like a website, and it should not pretend to be another person, brand or institution. The usual mischief, in other words, except that this time the platforms have already learned a few lessons the hard way.

What it can really be useful for

Above all, it is useful for dealing with new people without opening the door to your phone number: when you enter a group full of strangers, when you write to a company, when you take part in a course, when you belong to a community or when, like me, you run a channel and want to offer a contact route without putting your personal mobile number in every post.

For those of us who participate in communities of blind and low-vision people, where groups, links and contacts are shared almost daily, this can help quite a bit. It does not fix every evil of the internet, that would require rather more than an update, but it can help stop us from handing out our phone number with the enthusiasm of sweets at a children’s party.

Do not worry: it is not mandatory

Nobody is going to force you to create a username. If you do not want to touch anything, WhatsApp will keep working exactly as it has until now: chats, groups, calls, video calls, all tied to your usual number.

So there is no need to experience this as an imposition. It is one more option on the table. Whoever wants it can use it. Whoever does not can carry on as before. We are not going to wake up one day and discover that sending a “good morning” requires passing a crash course in digital identity.

Reserving the name before the feature fully arrives

WhatsApp has opened username reservation before activating the full feature. Reserving means claiming now the name you want to use later: if it is available and meets the rules, it is saved under your name and nobody can get ahead of you in the meantime.

It makes sense: we are talking about an app with more than three billion users. If everyone tried to choose a name on the same day, half of us would end up with impossible combinations of dots and numbers. Reservation brings a little order to the chaos before it arrives.

That said, reserving is not the same as being able to use it for everything right away. Some accounts will see the reservation option first, while full use will be activated later.

Where to find it

The route is short: WhatsApp, Settings, Account, Username.

On iPhone, open WhatsApp and then Settings. On Android, usually go through More options and then Settings. Once inside, look for Account, and there you should find Username.

If you use a screen reader, the label to look for is exactly that: “Username”, or “Nombre de usuario” if your app is in Spanish.

And if it does not appear yet, do not assume that VoiceOver, TalkBack or your phone has gone wrong. The rollout is gradual, so the most likely explanation is simply that it has not reached your account, country or version yet. Update the app and check again in a few days. Yes, waiting again: that extreme sport technology keeps forcing us to practise without medal or podium.

How to create or reserve it

The process is direct: Settings, Account, Username, and you type the name you want. WhatsApp checks whether it is available; if it is and it meets the rules, you reserve it, if the option already appears in your account. If it is already taken, you will have to try another variant, and the app may suggest alternatives.

Think about it calmly before typing it. If it is for personal use, avoid adding information that identifies you too much, such as your full name or date of birth. If it is for a channel, an association or a business, the logical thing is to use the same name you already have on other networks, so people recognize you without thinking.

A detail you should not overlook if you use a screen reader

The name has to be comfortable to hear, dictate and write. A very long one, full of dots, numbers and underscores, may be perfectly valid for the system and a nuisance in practice. VoiceOver or TalkBack will read it without a problem, but that is not the same as someone hearing it and remembering it without having to ask for pen and paper.

Also avoid combinations that lead to confusion, such as the letter “o” and the number zero, or lowercase “l” and the number one. The shorter, clearer and easier to spell, the better for everyone, not only for people who use assistive technology.

The username key

WhatsApp is also preparing an additional key for the username. If you activate it, knowing your username will not be enough for someone to write to you for the first time: they will also need that key.

It may be useful if your name starts circulating more than you would like. It does not replace blocking or the rest of the privacy options, but it adds another barrier. It is like saying: you may know where the door is, but you also need to know how to knock.

No, there will not be a public search directory

WhatsApp has clarified that there will be no username directory and no public suggestions for finding people. To contact you, someone must know your exact name, and if you have the key enabled, the key as well.

This matters: the username does not turn WhatsApp into an open social network like Instagram. It is different if you publish it on a website, a card or a biography: if you make it public, anyone will be able to use it to write to you.

What I think about all this

This feature is not going to remove the phone number from WhatsApp: the account is still tied to a phone, and that does not change. What it gains is a less exposed contact method for certain situations.

I would reserve the name as soon as the option appears, especially if you run a channel, an association or any public-facing project. Reserving it does not force you to use it now or publish it tomorrow; it only prevents someone else from getting there first.

For private use, there is no rush: you can wait, see how things evolve and decide calmly. The new feature does not perform miracles, but it does improve your privacy a little. And nowadays, stopping ourselves from giving away our phone number as if it were fairground advertising is already a win.

Siri AI, Apple and Europe: artificial intelligence trips over bureaucracy

After WhatsApp, it is time to talk about Apple, Europe and Siri AI. And this time it is not a hidden setting in a menu: it is a real fight between privacy, competition and features that could significantly improve accessibility.

What just happened

Apple introduced Siri AI at this June’s WWDC: a much smarter version of Siri, powered by Apple Intelligence, able to understand context, scan your messages, emails and photos, draft text, interpret what is on screen and search the web for up-to-date information. And Apple confirmed in writing during WWDC what was already coming: in the European Union, Siri AI will not arrive with iOS 27 or iPadOS 27. It will be available on macOS 27 and visionOS 27, interestingly the systems used by fewer people.

Craig Federighi, Apple’s senior vice president of software engineering, said plainly that they are “deeply disappointed” that European users will be left without the feature. The European Commission, for its part, argues that the law is not stopping anything; the problem is how Apple wants to build it. As usually happens in these messes, each side tells the story in a way that leaves the villain sitting opposite them.

Why this is not a minor detail

Siri AI is not “Siri, but a little smarter.” It is the difference between an assistant you ask for something simple and it answers as if it has just woken from a ten-year nap, and an assistant that truly understands what you have on your phone, what you need and how to give it to you without making you open four different apps.

For those of us who use VoiceOver, this matters more than usual. A well-integrated assistant can reduce steps, interpret what is on screen and make everyday tasks easier without forcing us to search for the exact button in the exact place. I am not talking about setting alarms or asking the temperature. I am talking about real autonomy.

Where the clash with Europe lies

The Digital Markets Act aims to stop giants like Apple from keeping all the advantages of their own systems for their own services, leaving competitors watching from the sidelines. In this case, Europe says that if Siri AI has privileged access to certain iPhone functions, other assistants must be able to compete under similar conditions. Apple replies that opening that access could put users’ privacy and security at risk.

And here is the uncomfortable part: both sides have a point. An AI assistant with deep access to the phone is not just any app; if it can read your messages, move between apps and perform actions, it needs serious controls. But it is also not acceptable for Apple to decide that its assistant can walk straight into the kitchen while everyone else waits on the doormat.

The proposal Apple put on the table

Apple proposed something called Trusted System Agent: a security intermediary between the iPhone system and third-party assistants, with an eighteen-month transition period to roll it out gradually. A European official summarized it without much diplomacy: that proposal would have allowed Siri AI to consolidate itself alone for at least two years before anyone else had a real chance to compete. The Commission rejected it.

The good news, if it can be called that, is that the dialogue is still alive. Tim Cook met this week with Henna Virkkunen, the European technology chief, and both sides spoke of a “constructive exchange.” In diplomatic language, that can mean anything from “we have genuinely moved forward” to “at least nobody threw the laptop out the window.”

My reading of the matter

My impression is that Apple is defending something legitimate and, at the same time, defending its control over the iPhone. Both things are true at once; there is no need to choose a team. Apple is right to say that an assistant with such deep access can be dangerous if opened badly. And Europe is also right not to want one company to use control of the system to keep everyone else permanently at a disadvantage.

The sensible point should be in the middle: strong privacy, clear permissions, visible controls for the user and real competition. What does not seem fair to me is that European users end up paying for this fight by losing useful features, precisely when some of them could genuinely improve accessibility.

Because in the end, someone buys an iPhone that is not exactly cheap, hears that a much smarter Siri is coming, and then finds out that in Europe we have to wait. Innovation travels by high-speed train until it crosses a regulatory border, and then it seems to board a bus with three transfers.

For now there is no closed agreement, and the fact that the parties are still talking is a good sign, although it guarantees nothing in the short term. For those of us who use iPhone with VoiceOver, this is worth following closely: Siri AI could be a very useful tool if it arrives well integrated and with accessibility cared for from the start. For the moment, the answer is the least technological one of all: wait. And this time, without Siri even being able to explain why.

The leaked iPhone 18 and the rumor of the twentieth-anniversary iPhone

And now, iPhone territory. But not the usual September rumors: we are talking about real internal material, leaked after a security breach at an Apple supplier.

A different kind of leak

Apple has not officially presented the next models yet, but for days now information has been circulating about the iPhone 18 Pro and the so-called twentieth-anniversary iPhone, which some are already informally calling “iPhone 20.” Up to that point, nothing extraordinary: every year brings predictions, mockups and that technological wildlife that looks at a screw and announces the future with complete confidence.

What is different this time is the origin of much of that information: a security breach at Tata Electronics, one of Apple’s important suppliers in India, from which confidential documents, component lists, suppliers, photos and videos of the future iPhone 18 Pro are said to have emerged. It is not that someone imagined the next model well. Internal material has surfaced in a way that is anything but elegant. If this were a family lunch, nobody just took an extra croquette: they carried off the whole fridge.

What the leaked videos show

The most commented material consists of images that supposedly show the iPhone 18 Pro undergoing drop tests, something common in any phone development process: controlled knocks, falls and stress before the product reaches stores. The videos show a gray device, with the Apple logo and three rear cameras, falling in a laboratory setting. It does not look like an amateur mockup or a pretty render for social media: it looks like real test material.

That does not mean the design is one hundred percent final; Apple can still adjust details before launch, but it does make fairly clear where the product is heading.

A design that is not here to surprise

The first reading is that the iPhone 18 Pro does not bring a visual revolution. It would keep a line very similar to recent Pro models: recognizable body, polished finish, three rear cameras. Apple seems more focused on refining than reinventing.

That may disappoint anyone expecting a radical change every year, but by now it should not surprise anyone: Apple has spent generations refining the same design language while changing chips, cameras, battery and materials inside. Personally, I do not think a continuing design is a problem in itself. The problem appears when you pay the price of a revolution and get the feeling of “I have seen this before.” Then each person must decide whether the internal improvements justify the change.

What really worries Apple

The videos are the eye-catching part, but the really delicate thing is something else: component lists, suppliers and internal parts are said to have leaked. For us as users, that may sound unexciting. For Apple, it is very serious, because knowing which supplier makes each part and how production is organized is extremely valuable information for competitors, analysts, investors and copycat manufacturers. It is not only that the finished dish has been seen: part of the recipe and the suppliers of the ingredients have also been exposed. And Apple will not like that at all.

What is expected from the iPhone 18 Pro

The major internal novelty would be the A20 Pro chip, built with more advanced technology, possibly 2 nanometers. There is no need to memorize the number: the important thing is that it promises more power and better efficiency, exactly what Apple needs if it wants Siri to better understand what we do and apps to process image, voice and text without leaving the phone burning hot. There is also talk of battery improvements linked to chip and modem efficiency, the modem being the part that manages mobile connections. And, to be honest, in the end nobody asks how many nanometers the chip has: they ask whether the phone reaches the evening alive.

In the camera, leaks point to a variable aperture in the main sensor, which in plain language means regulating how much light comes in: more indoors or at night, less when the scene is very bright, and also more control over background blur. For us, a better camera is not just a prettier photo: it also improves visual recognition apps, document reading, object identification and code scanning, tools many of us use every day.

As for the drop tests, it is also worth not going wild with enthusiasm: a phone surviving in a lab does not mean it is indestructible. One thing is a prepared fall and another is the phone slipping in the street, straight onto the stone corner that seems to have been born to ruin your afternoon. Still, any improvement in resistance is welcome in a device many of us use as a daily tool for autonomy.

There is also talk of a smaller Dynamic Island, that upper area where Apple places the front camera and sensors, and which it managed to sell as a feature when it was really a screen cutout; credit where due, turning a limitation into a pretty name is an art. A simpler camera button is also rumored, after the current one, with gestures and pressure, proved more sophisticated than necessary for many people. For those of us who use VoiceOver, the more predictable a physical button is, the better.

The calendar changes and the foldable appears

The other novelty is not inside the phone, but in how Apple may sell it. Apple could split the launch: in autumn 2026, the more expensive models would arrive first, the iPhone 18 Pro, Pro Max and possibly the first foldable iPhone, leaving the entry models, the regular iPhone 18 and the 18e, for spring 2027. It makes all the commercial sense in the world: first show what leaves the highest margin, and then talk about the rest.

As for the foldable, it would work closed as a normal phone and open as a small tablet, useful for reading or working with documents, but with questions still open around price, weight and durability. For us, a bigger screen is not automatically an advantage: what really matters is how VoiceOver behaves inside it and whether apps truly adapt. A phone does not become useful because it folds. It becomes useful if it solves something real.

The rumor of the twentieth-anniversary iPhone

The first iPhone was introduced in 2007, so 2027 marks twenty years. For the tenth anniversary, in 2017, Apple released the iPhone X: goodbye Home button, hello Face ID, and a change that shaped the rest of the decade. With that precedent, it is normal for expectations around 2027 to be sky high.

There is talk of a serious redesign: an almost edge-to-edge display, wraparound glass, a front that looks practically all screen. It sounds spectacular, but it also brings risks: the more the screen reaches the edge, the easier it is to touch by accident, the more delicate repairs become and the more critical glass resistance is. A beautiful phone that cracks if you look at it wrong is not innovation; it is a sculpture with a monthly fee.

There is also speculation about hiding sensors under the screen so that Face ID and the front camera keep working without leaving a visible trace. My opinion here is quite clear: I would much rather have a small reliable cutout than a perfect design with a temperamental Face ID that fails exactly when you are in a hurry.

How I see it

Apple seems to be playing two different games: the iPhone 18 Pro as a powerful evolution inside and quite conservative outside, and the anniversary model as the attempt to surprise us again with design. The Tata leak is serious precisely because it shows too much: the product, the tests, and much of the industrial machinery Apple prefers to keep behind the curtain. They like us to see the dish served, not the cook sweating in the kitchen.

For those of us who follow technology from the accessibility angle, the underlying question is not whether the next iPhone will be prettier or more powerful, but whether all this will translate into a phone that is easier to use and more autonomous in daily life. Because in the end, a phone may have curved glass, a new chip, leaked videos and a shiny anniversary name, but if it does not improve everyday life, it all remains expensive fireworks. And we already have more than enough of those.

Accessibility in sport: following a match should not depend on having eyes

I will close this issue with a topic that matters to me personally: accessibility in sport. And I am not only talking about knowing the score. I am talking about being able to experience the emotion, the context and the shared moment, like any other fan.

An issue we have treated as secondary for far too long

For years, sports accessibility for blind, low-vision or deafblind people has been treated as something nice, pretty for a press release, but rarely understood for what it really is: a right to access information and emotion.

Sport is one of the few truly universal conversations we still have. A goal, an Olympic final, a decisive point at Wimbledon can move millions of people at the same time. So the question I ask myself is simple: if sport is shared emotion, why has it been told for so long only with sighted people in mind?

Because following sport is not just knowing who won; for that, someone can tell you at the end. It is understanding what is happening, where it is happening, how the play moves, how the crowd reacts, whether an action was calculated or desperate. Sport is rhythm, space, strategy and bodies in motion, not just a scoreboard.

From traditional radio to real audio description

For decades, radio was the great ally of many blind sports fans, and when it is done well, it remains very descriptive. But it is worth not confusing it with sports audio description as such. Radio commentary narrates the match for everyone equally. Audio description focuses specifically on replacing the visual information that a blind or low-vision person cannot capture independently: it is not enough to say “they attack down the wing”; the play has to be situated, the space, movement, players’ reactions and surrounding atmosphere described.

It is not about speaking more; it is about speaking better: knowing when to describe, when to let the ambient sound breathe, and when to let the crowd’s own emotion become part of the experience. The silence before a penalty also tells a story. The ovation after an impossible play does too.

The stadium: the right to be there

One of the first major advances has been audio-descriptive commentary inside stadiums, through headphones, a receiver or an app, so that a blind person can attend the venue and truly follow the match. For a long time, the alternative was unfair: either you went to the stadium without properly knowing what was happening, or you stayed at home and followed it better on the radio. And going to the stadium is not just watching the match: it is meeting friends, feeling the crowd, singing, celebrating with everyone else.

The ideal, and I am very clear about this, is to be able to hear the audio description from any seat, not from an area separated from the rest. That is the difference between integration and inclusion: integration lets you in; inclusion lets you participate on equal terms.

When the service moves into an app

The next evolution has been taking all this to mobile apps and web players, and that changes everything: before, audio description almost always depended on being physically inside the stadium; now it can reach someone at home, in another country or following the match from anywhere with a connection.

FIFA and UEFA have been moving in this direction for some time, and for the 2026 World Cup FIFA is announcing audio description for matches and ceremonies through a specific app, with languages depending on the venue: English and French in Canada, and English and Spanish in the United States and Mexico. But here I ask for caution: announcing an app is not enough. It has to work well with screen readers, the audio must arrive in sync, the language has to be selectable without complications, and the buttons must be properly labeled on both iPhone and Android. An inaccessible app for offering accessibility would be one of those contradictions modern technology produces with such enthusiasm.

When sound becomes space

One of the advances that has caught my attention most does not simply describe with words, but turns the game’s own data into sound. Action Audio, already used in tennis, starts from a brilliant idea: if systems already exist that track the trajectory of the ball, those data can be translated into spatial audio, so you can perceive direction, speed and movement without waiting for anyone to describe it. It does not replace human commentary; it complements it. And in sports as fast as tennis, where the ball changes direction in tenths of a second, it can make an enormous difference.

Here, for me, appears the most important idea in this whole section: do not adapt at the end, design from the beginning. When accessibility arrives late, it almost always arrives limping. When it is considered from the start, it usually improves the experience for many more people, not only for those of us with a disability.

Touch also plays

For a long time, talking about sports accessibility almost meant talking about audio, leaving out many deafblind people and also limiting those who would benefit from complementary tactile information. That is why haptic technologies matter so much: they can translate game actions into vibrations or tactile signals. The idea is no longer only to hear the match, but to feel it.

FIFA has announced haptic devices in some stadiums at the 2026 World Cup, and proposals such as OneCourt or Touch2See already convert sports data into tactile information. They are promising solutions, but they require great care in design: for a deafblind person, it is not enough for something to vibrate “when something happens.” That information needs structure, rhythm and intensity. Touch should not be a technological ornament. It should be a language with its own grammar.

Low vision: not everything is solved with audio

Blindness and low vision are often spoken of as if they were the same thing, and they are not. Many people with low vision benefit greatly from purely visual improvements, if they are well designed: high contrast, magnification, trajectory lines, ball highlighting, better lighting and magnification devices.

This brings me to something I always repeat when I talk about accessibility: it cannot be rigid or treated as one-size-fits-all. Not all blind people need the same thing, not all low-vision people benefit from the same contrast, and not all deafblind people receive information in the same way. Some will use audio, others touch, others residual vision, and many a combination of all three. The future should allow choice: level of description, language, speed, type of voice, volume, ambient sound, vibration, contrast and amount of data. This is not a whim; it is recognizing that real life does not fit into a single setting.

Beware of the separate channel as an excuse

There is a temptation worth watching carefully: always creating a separate accessibility channel and considering the matter solved. Specific channels can be useful, even necessary in many cases, but they should not serve as an excuse to leave the main experience inaccessible. If accessibility is hidden, poorly labeled, available only in a secondary app or limited by territory, the progress is real but incomplete.

The ideal is not that we have to search for “the special channel.” The ideal is that any relevant sports broadcast includes audio description, subtitles, sign language when appropriate, haptic information and customizable options as standard, integrated rather than separate.

What is still missing

Continuity is missing, because accessibility cannot appear at major events and disappear for the rest of the year. Global reach is missing, because too many services depend on territorial rights or specific platforms. Real languages are missing, because if an event is sold as global, accessibility needs an equally global strategy. And above all, blind, low-vision, deaf and deafblind people need to be truly involved from the beginning of the design process, not at the end as a last-minute review. Deafblindness, in particular, needs its own solutions: saying “audio description” and considering the matter closed is not enough.

My conclusion

Sports accessibility is not charity or a pleasant detail for looking good. It is recognizing that sport also belongs to those who cannot see or hear it in the conventional way. When a match is made accessible for someone who cannot see, the system is forced to explain the sport better. When someone who cannot hear is considered, the atmosphere and rhythm are represented better. When someone who neither sees nor hears is considered, tactile languages open up and can transform the entire experience.

Sport does not belong only to those who can see it. It also belongs to those who hear it, feel it, follow it with their fingers or reconstruct it from a good description. And perhaps that is, in the end, the best definition of accessibility I can think of: not inventing a different sport for every person, but allowing everyone to be part of the same moment.
