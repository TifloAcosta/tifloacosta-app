iOS 26.6 prepares an alert for potentially dangerous messages

Apple is working on a new security measure for the Messages app on the iPhone. The idea is simple: if the system detects that a message may be malicious, it would display a warning before we continue interacting with it.

The feature has been discovered in the code of an advanced iOS 26.6 beta. The text that was found is equivalent to “Malicious message detected” and would be accompanied by several options, including leaving the decision until later, sharing the message with Apple so it can investigate it, or choosing not to report it.

We need to keep our feet on the ground here. The alert has not yet been seen working widely, and Apple has not explained what kinds of threats it will be able to recognize. It could help detect phishing links, attempted scams or messages designed to exploit a system flaw, but its real scope is not yet known.

Nor does this mean that Apple will automatically receive our conversations. According to what has been discovered, sending anything to the company would require an explicit decision by the user and would be limited to the specific message they choose to report.

The main benefit would be that it forces us to stop for a moment. Many scams work because they create a sense of urgency: a fine that supposedly expires today, a blocked bank account, a parcel that requires an immediate payment or a relative claiming to have changed their phone number. When urgency appears, it is easy to act before thinking. A clear warning can interrupt that impulse.

Even so, we must not treat this new feature as an absolute guarantee. If the iPhone does not show a warning, that does not prove the message is safe. Detection systems can make mistakes, miss a threat or flag something as suspicious when it is actually legitimate.

For those of us who use VoiceOver, the feature could be especially useful. Many scams rely on visual details, misleading web addresses or designs that imitate a bank or company. A warning from the system itself could provide another, more understandable point of reference. Even so, we will need to check that VoiceOver reads the full warning, announces its buttons clearly and places the focus where it belongs.

There is no point installing a beta to look for this option, because it is not yet available to the public. When Apple releases iOS 26.6, we will find out whether it is included from day one or activated later.

My assessment is positive, but there is no reason to get carried away. Any additional protection is welcome, especially when it helps slow down impulsive decisions. The main defence will remain the same as always: do not hand over passwords or codes received by message, do not open unexpected links, and check any warning by going directly to the official app of the company or service concerned.

WhatsApp prepares two new features: its own cloud for backups and birthday reminders

WhatsApp is developing two different features that are worth covering together. One affects something important: the backup of our conversations. The other is much simpler, but it could save us from forgetting the occasional birthday and having to come up with an emergency explanation.

The first new feature would allow iPhone backups to be stored in a cloud managed directly by WhatsApp instead of having to rely on iCloud.

At present, the conversations, photographs, videos, documents and voice notes included in the backup take up space in our iCloud account. Apple provides 5 GB free of charge, and that space is shared with photos, files, the iPhone’s own backup and the rest of our data. If we belong to several groups where plenty of multimedia content is shared, the WhatsApp backup can grow with remarkable ease, even if our storage account is not quite so delighted.

The future option would keep iCloud as the default system but allow us to choose WhatsApp’s cloud instead. Testing points to an initial free allowance of 2 GB and possible paid plans with more capacity. Those prices and limits could still change before launch, so they should not be treated as final.

The backup stored on WhatsApp’s servers would be protected by mandatory end-to-end encryption. This would prevent Meta or WhatsApp from reading its contents. The protection could be managed through a passkey, a password or a long recovery key.

Current iCloud backups can also be protected with end-to-end encryption, but that protection has to be enabled manually within WhatsApp. The difference would be that encryption in the app’s own cloud would be required from the outset.

Having another option is good news, although 2 GB of free storage will not solve the problem for everyone. Before switching, we will need to check how much space the backup uses and decide where it is most convenient to store it. We will also need to review the accessibility of the screens used to choose a provider, check the available space and restore data with VoiceOver and TalkBack.

The second feature is a birthday reminder. WhatsApp is preparing a section showing upcoming dates and could notify us when the day arrives.

It will not take the information from Facebook or from some secret Meta address book. It will use the date of birth stored in each contact’s record. If we have only saved the person’s name and telephone number, WhatsApp will not be able to guess when we should wish them a happy birthday, no matter how fashionable artificial intelligence may be.

The feature has been discovered in the code of an Android beta. Nobody can use it yet, not even people who have that beta installed, and there is no word on when it will reach the iPhone. From an accessibility point of view, receiving these alerts inside an app we use every day could be convenient, provided the birthday list is well organized and reads correctly with TalkBack and VoiceOver.

Neither of these features requires us to do anything now. Both are still in development. The first could give us more freedom to decide where our data is stored; the second will not change the world, but it may save us from having to say, “Of course I remembered; I was waiting for the right moment.”

OpenAI wants to bring ChatGPT into the living room with a speaker that listens, watches and moves

OpenAI is working with Jony Ive, the renowned former Apple designer, on a new family of devices. The first mass-market product could take the form of a portable speaker, although simply calling it a speaker would fall well short of the idea.

Before going any further, one clarification is necessary: OpenAI has not officially unveiled the product. Its collaboration with Jony Ive and the existence of the project are confirmed, but the camera, sensors, battery, movement and many of its possible functions come from leaks. The device is real as a project, but its final design could still change.

The aim would be to create a screenless computer built around ChatGPT. It could answer questions, play content, control devices in the home, manage messages and use authorized information from email, calendars or other services to provide more personal assistance.

Unlike traditional speakers, it would not simply wait for a wake word. Its cameras and sensors would try to understand what is happening around it, recognize objects and connect the surroundings with the conversation. If we were cooking, for example, it could identify what is in front of us and adapt its instructions. If we were getting ready to go out, it could remind us about an appointment or warn us that we had forgotten something.

The leaks also mention mechanical parts capable of moving. We do not know whether it would rotate to point the camera, follow the speaker’s voice or use movement only to show that it is paying attention. In any case, OpenAI wants it to feel like an active presence rather than a box sitting motionless in a corner.

The new GPT-Live voice system fits this idea perfectly. It can listen and speak at the same time, accept interruptions and maintain a less rigid conversation. For a device without a screen, that naturalness is not a luxury: it is the difference between speaking comfortably and having to repeat commands as though we were filling in a form over the telephone.

For blind people, the potential is obvious. A device controlled mainly through speech and able to interpret images could describe a room, read a label, recognize a product, locate an object on a table or help us identify the controls on a household appliance.

It could also simplify tasks involving email, messages and the calendar. Asking it to find an invoice, read important messages or create an appointment from an email could save many steps. Not because those apps are necessarily inaccessible, but because a direct instruction can be much faster than moving through several screens with a screen reader.

The connected home would be another interesting area. Instead of remembering the exact name assigned to every light bulb or plug, we could give more natural commands: turn everything off except the hallway light, tell us when the washing machine has finished or switch on a fan if the temperature rises.

But if it is really going to help us, OpenAI will need to do its homework from the beginning. The initial setup should be possible by voice; the buttons should be distinguishable by touch; every status light should have an audible alternative; and there should be a physical switch that genuinely disconnects the microphone and camera.

Privacy will be the major test. We are talking about a device with a camera, microphones and potential access to messages, calendars and household routines. The more it knows, the more useful it may become, but the information it handles will also be more sensitive. Users will need to know when it is listening, when it is watching, what it stores and what it sends over the internet.

Apple has also sued OpenAI over the alleged misuse of trade secrets involving former employees. These allegations have yet to be proven, but the proceedings could delay or alter the project.

There is currently no commercial name, final price or official date. Reports point to a presentation before the end of 2026 and a possible launch during 2027, but this remains a forecast.

The important question for our community will not be whether the device looks attractive or turns its head when we enter the room. It will be something much simpler: whether we can take it out of the box, set it up and use it independently. If the answer is yes, it could become a very interesting tool. If it needs a visual app for everything that matters, it will be another great invention that remembered accessibility only after the party was over.

Apple Intelligence clears the biggest obstacle to operating in China

Apple Intelligence is much closer to arriving in China after completing one of the registrations required by the country’s authorities. This does not mean that it can already be activated or that Apple has announced a date, but it does remove one of the main obstacles that has remained since its introduction in 2024.

China requires generative artificial intelligence services to register, assess risks, control certain content and assume clear responsibility for how they operate. For a foreign company, translating the system and making it available to users is not enough.

Apple also needs to work with authorized local partners. The company has mainly chosen Alibaba, whose Qwen family of models would take part in functions involving text and images. Baidu would also be involved in the project, although Apple has not yet explained publicly what role each company will play.

The consequence is clear: Apple Intelligence will not work in exactly the same way in every country. The integration and design will remain Apple’s, but some responses and capabilities will depend on Chinese models and will be subject to the content rules of that market.

For Apple, this step is far more important than it may seem. China remains one of its largest markets, but for two years it has sold Apple Intelligence-compatible iPhones there without being able to activate those very features. Meanwhile, Huawei, Xiaomi, Oppo and other local manufacturers have introduced assistants capable of summarizing text, editing images, drafting messages and carrying out various tasks within the phone.

An expensive phone can live for a while on the strength of a good camera and a powerful processor. But when the device next to it offers artificial intelligence features that ours advertises but cannot use, the explanation becomes difficult. Apple needed to close that gap to compete on equal terms and to present its next iPhones with all its cards on the table.

We still do not know which functions will arrive first. It is reasonable to expect writing tools, summaries, notification organization and some image-related capabilities. It is also unclear what will replace, in China, the requests that can be sent to ChatGPT in other countries.

The new AI-powered Siri deserves a separate mention. The registration of Apple Intelligence does not confirm that this Siri will launch at the same time. Apple could release the functions we already know first and leave the more advanced assistant for a later phase, especially because it needs access to more personal information and must be able to work across apps.

For blind people living in China, the arrival could have a direct impact. Summarizing long emails, organizing notifications, drafting messages or interpreting images can reduce the number of steps required with VoiceOver. These tools do not replace the screen reader, but they can make large amounts of information easier to manage.

The future Siri would be even more interesting if it delivers what has been promised: finding an email, extracting a date, creating an appointment or moving information between apps through a spoken request. Reducing ten or fifteen actions to a single instruction could genuinely improve independence.

VoiceOver, however, does not depend on this approval and already works in China. This news does not suddenly make the iPhone accessible; it adds possible artificial intelligence tools that would complement the accessibility already available.

Privacy also remains unresolved. Apple will need to explain which parts are processed on the device, what data Alibaba and Baidu handle, where it is stored and how closely the Chinese architecture resembles the one used in Europe or the United States.

In summary, Apple Intelligence has passed through a very important customs checkpoint, but the shop is not open yet. We are still waiting for official confirmation, a date, the list of functions and clarification about the new Siri. For Apple, this is a strategic decision of the highest importance; for blind users in China, it could finally mean access to tools that have already been part of the iPhone experience in other countries for some time.

Also in the news

Alongside the main stories, these other topics have also seen plenty of activity over the past week and are worth keeping on our radar.

ChatGPT introduces a more natural voice conversation

OpenAI has begun rolling out GPT-Live in ChatGPT. The system can listen while it speaks, accepts interruptions more naturally and makes turn-taking less rigid. For those of us who use speech as a primary means of access, the improvement could be significant. The new mode does not yet include video streaming or screen sharing, however, as those still depend on the previous experience on compatible devices.

GPT-5.6 comes to Word, Excel and PowerPoint

Microsoft has added GPT-5.6 to Microsoft 365 Copilot and presents it as the preferred model for complex work in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Copilot Chat and Cowork. It can help prepare documents, analyze data and create presentations with fewer instructions. For JAWS and NVDA users, the important question will be whether generated content maintains an accessible structure and whether Copilot’s responses can be navigated comfortably.

Ray-Ban Meta glasses reach more blind people

The American Council of the Blind will provide Ray-Ban Meta glasses free of charge to blind veterans who meet the eligibility requirements during its convention, on July 27 and while supplies last. The glasses can make calls, send messages and request descriptions of the surroundings without using the hands. Their usefulness is clear, but they also increase the privacy debate: we are carrying a camera that can see and record what happens around us. Assistance and control over data will need to progress together.

A tablet that lets users touch maps and images

The European ABILITY project has developed a prototype 10.5-inch multisensory tablet. Its screen can create different tactile sensations and combine those textures with sound and descriptions. The idea is to allow a blind person to explore maps, charts and images in a more spatial way. It remains a prototype rather than a commercial product, but it addresses a need that screen readers cannot solve on their own.

Microsoft will use more artificial intelligence to find flaws in Windows

Microsoft is applying artificial intelligence to the detection and correction of vulnerabilities. This could lead to monthly updates containing a larger number of fixes. The July Windows 11 update also introduces changes to Magnifier, visual options and Bluetooth stability improvements. For those of us who depend on screen readers or braille displays, keeping the system secure is important, but so is checking compatibility before installing especially large updates.

Artificial intelligence systems can reproduce censorship too

A study by Meta’s Oversight Board concludes that several models reject requests that criticize governments in restrictive countries more often than comparable requests involving more open democracies. This matters because we increasingly use these systems to search for, summarize and explain information. Artificial intelligence can do more than make mistakes: it can also show us an incomplete version of the world without our noticing.

Tesla talks about a wheelchair-accessible robotaxi

Tesla has said it is working on an autonomous vehicle designed to transport people who use wheelchairs, although it has not shown the model or provided a date. The subject also matters to blind people because robotaxis could offer independence to those of us who cannot drive. To be genuinely accessible, they will need to help users locate the vehicle, confirm the destination, enter safely and resolve problems without relying on a visual screen.

Nvidia and Japan accelerate what is known as physical AI

Nvidia has partnered with major Japanese robot manufacturers, including Fanuc and Yaskawa, to develop machines that can perceive their surroundings more effectively and work alongside people. The immediate applications are in factories, hospitals and logistics. In the longer term, this technology could reach household robots capable of locating objects or carrying out physical tasks, although there is still a long way to go before we have one helping us at home.

AI data centers face growing opposition

Governments and cities in several countries are considering limits or pauses on the construction of large data centers because of their consumption of electricity, water and land. The story is a reminder of something that is often forgotten: artificial intelligence does not live in a magical cloud. Behind every answer are buildings full of servers, networks and cooling systems. That cost will eventually influence prices, energy and political decisions.

Samsung strengthens its foldable phones with Flex Titanium

Samsung has introduced a new foldable display structure that combines a film with a titanium plate. The company promises greater durability and a less visible crease. The next devices will be unveiled at the Galaxy Unpacked event on July 22. For TalkBack users, the important point will be to check how the interface adapts when the phone is opened and closed, and whether the outer display retains all the necessary functions.
