Tech Pills 18 2026

Hello, beautiful people.

This week, technology did not knock on the door: it walked straight in without waiting to be invited. Apple is preparing a new way to charge us for its devices, the European Union is once again setting limits for Google, and foldable phones no longer seem able to decide whether they are phones, tablets, or both at once.

So here we are again, sorting through the noise to keep what may genuinely interest us. This new edition brings three main subjects, examined calmly and with particular attention to how they may affect us as technology users and, especially, as blind people.

Then, as we did in the previous edition, we will finish with ten shorter stories that have also shaped the technology news of recent days. They do not need a full article, but they deserve more than a headline that rushes past without really telling us anything.

Let us begin.

Apple is preparing a new way to get its devices: pay to use them, upgrade them, or return them

For years, we have watched the prices of Apple devices rise with rather more enthusiasm than our incomes. Buying a high-end iPhone, Mac, or iPad now requires more than desire: it takes planning, financing, or a remarkable ability to look the other way when the price appears.

Against that background, Apple's latest planned move begins to make sense.

Published reports indicate that the company plans to introduce a new programme in the United States called Apple Upgrade. It is expected to launch on Tuesday, July 28, 2026, in partnership with Klarna, which would finance the transactions. Apple has not officially announced it at the time of writing, so it should be described as a planned launch rather than a service that is already operating.

Apple Upgrade would not be simple financing

The idea is not merely to divide the price into monthly instalments.

Apple Upgrade would work rather like leasing a car. The customer would choose a device, pay a monthly amount for a set period and, at the end, decide what to do with it.

The expected options would be to return it, replace it with another model under a new agreement, or keep it by paying the remaining amount, commonly known as the residual value.

The programme is also expected to allow customers to pay off the device early or replace it before the agreement ends. Some of these operations could involve additional charges.

The terms would reportedly be 24 months for the iPhone and Apple Watch, and 36 months for the iPad and Mac. Before approving the agreement, Klarna would carry out a soft credit check, which should not normally affect the applicant's credit score.

The word subscription may be misleading

Some reports describe Apple Upgrade as a subscription. I think that word needs considerable qualification.

It would not be like Apple Music, where we pay while we wish to use a service and can leave when we choose. This would involve a two- or three-year financial agreement, a credit check, payment obligations, and conditions governing whether the equipment is returned, upgraded, or purchased.

I would therefore understand it more accurately as a technology lease or financing arrangement with options at the end of the contract.

The monthly payment may look easier on the eye, but the commitment remains. The device would not necessarily belong to us during the payment period either.

Klarna provides the money and assumes the risk

Apple would not finance the devices directly. That work would be handled by Klarna, a Swedish company with international operations that has become particularly well known for buy-now-pay-later services.

Klarna would assess the customer, finance the transaction, and manage the payments. This would allow Apple to offer an integrated monthly-payment system in its stores without directly assuming the risk that a customer might stop paying.

The partnership has not appeared from nowhere. Klarna already offers several payment methods for Apple products and is integrated with Apple Pay in the United States. The company describes itself as a digital bank and flexible-payment provider with international operations.

Which devices would be included

Apple Upgrade would cover most iPhone, iPad, Mac, and Apple Watch models, but not necessarily every one of them.

Published reports suggest that some entry-level models would be excluded, including the standard iPad, the Apple Watch SE, certain lower-cost iPhones, and the MacBook Neo.

This point deserves caution. The lists that have circulated are not entirely consistent, and Apple has not yet published official terms. I would therefore not regard the final list of eligible devices as settled until the announcement appears and the details can be checked directly in Apple's store.

What does seem fairly clear is that Apple initially wants to direct the programme towards mid-range and high-end models. That is logical: the more expensive the device, the more tempting a seemingly small monthly payment becomes.

AppleCare would not be included

This would be an important difference from the current US iPhone Upgrade Program.

That programme allows customers to upgrade their iPhone after making the equivalent of 12 payments and includes AppleCare+ with Theft and Loss. Apple currently confirms those conditions on its own website.

Apple Upgrade, by contrast, would not include AppleCare+ in the monthly payment. Anyone wanting protection against accidental damage, faults, theft, or loss would have to purchase it separately.

That is not a minor detail.

If we are using a device that must be returned in good condition and has not yet been fully paid for, going without insurance could become very expensive. The advertised monthly payment may look attractive, but a serious comparison would need to include the cost of AppleCare+ or any other protection we choose.

The current iPhone upgrade programme would disappear

Apple would reportedly stop accepting new customers into the US iPhone Upgrade Program as Apple Upgrade begins operating.

The current programme allows customers to buy the phone through a 24-month loan, upgrade after paying the equivalent of 12 instalments and, after completing all 24 payments, own the iPhone outright.

The new system expands the idea to more products, but it also changes the approach considerably.

It would no longer be only about getting a new iPhone every year. Apple wants a formula that can apply to the phone, the watch, the iPad, and the Mac, while keeping the customer within a cycle of regular upgrades.

An iPhone could be restricted after missed payments

This is one of the aspects that has generated the most headlines, although it still requires official confirmation.

References have been found in the code of iOS 27 beta versions to a system that could restrict certain functions on a financed device if the customer stopped paying. Available information describes a limited mode in which some essential applications would continue to work, including Phone, Settings, Health, Accessibility, Passwords, and Magnifier.

This does not mean Apple is about to impose such a restriction on every iPhone bought in instalments. The code indicates that the company has created or is testing this capability, but we still do not know exactly how it would be applied, what safeguards users would have, how warnings would be issued, or what the contractual conditions would be.

I would not regard every feature described by some publications as confirmed for the first day of Apple Upgrade either.

The direction, however, is fairly clear: if Apple and Klarna are going to lease expensive equipment for two or three years, they will seek a way to prevent someone from stopping payment, resetting the iPhone, and continuing to use it as though nothing had happened.

It is not a pleasant measure, but neither is it surprising. What matters will be how far it goes and what happens when there is an error, an incorrectly charged instalment, an open complaint, or a temporary financial difficulty. Protecting a contract is one thing; turning a disagreement with a finance company into an almost unusable phone is quite another.

Why Apple wants to launch this now

The simplest explanation is that its devices are becoming increasingly expensive, and a monthly payment sells more easily than a four-figure price.

That does not necessarily mean the product is cheaper. It means the figure is easier to digest.

A device with a four-figure price can be intimidating. A relatively small monthly payment looks much more manageable, even if it must then be paid for two or three years and we still have to decide what to do with the device at the end.

Apple also gains another obvious advantage: a guaranteed upgrade cycle.

When someone buys an iPhone outright, they may keep it for four, five, or six years. When they enter a programme designed around returning it and obtaining another every two years, the next sale is virtually built into the contract.

Apple stops selling only a device and begins selling continuity within its ecosystem.

The system may be convenient for the customer, but for Apple it is an excellent machine: recurring payments, predictable upgrades, and customers who find it harder to move to another brand.

Limited availability and alternatives in each market

According to published information, Apple Upgrade would begin exclusively in the United States.

Apple has not announced an international expansion. A financial arrangement of this kind requires commercial, legal, and credit terms to be adapted for each market, so it cannot be assumed that it will reach other countries at the same time.

The idea itself, however, is not new. Different markets already offer traditional financing, trade-in programmes, leasing through banks or mobile operators, and plans with a final payment or residual value.

These products are not equivalent. Some leave the customer owning the device, others require it to be returned, and others allow it to be purchased through a final payment.

Any comparison must therefore be made against the offers available in the reader's own country and against the total cost of the agreement, not only the advertised monthly payment.

Not every monthly payment means the same thing

This is where we should pause before signing anything.

Some plans leave us owning the device once all payments have been made.

Others require us to return it.

Some allow us to buy it by paying a final amount.

Others require us to maintain a mobile service, have income paid into a particular bank, or use a specific card.

There may also be interest, fees, insurance, penalties for damage, limits on early cancellation, and requirements concerning the condition in which the device must be returned.

That is why asking only how much it costs per month is not enough.

The important questions are different:

How much will I have paid in total?

How much will I need to pay if I want to keep it?

What happens if I want to cancel early?

Who pays for a repair?

What damage is considered acceptable when it is returned?

What happens if the device is stolen?

What additional commitments does the offer require?

And, above all, what do I have left at the end?

That is the fundamental difference between leasing and buying. If we use an iPhone for two years and then return it, we have enjoyed the phone, but we have not created any value that we can recover: we hand in the device and begin paying for another. If we buy it, by contrast, even if the monthly payment is higher, the iPhone eventually becomes ours. We can keep it, sell it, give it away, or trade it in.

There is no single answer that suits everyone. Someone who wants to change phones every two years and prefers to know the monthly cost in advance may find leasing convenient. Someone who usually keeps an iPhone for four or five years will probably be better off buying it. And anyone comfortable with the second-hand market may recover more money by selling it directly than by handing it to Apple, a bank, or a mobile operator.

My view

Apple Upgrade is not a bad idea. It may make devices easier to obtain when paying the full price at once would be difficult. It can also avoid the trouble of selling used equipment and make spending easier to organise through a stable monthly amount.

But let us not confuse convenience with saving money.

Apple has not discovered a way to give us an iPhone. It has found a more effective way to charge us for one and, at the same time, keep us regularly replacing products within its ecosystem.

The small monthly payment is the shop window. The total cost, residual value, insurance, return conditions, and continuing payment obligation are the whole shop.

Once Apple Upgrade is official, we will need to know the payments, interest, penalties, and final value of each device. Until then, any claim about how much the customer will save is premature.

If it eventually expands to other countries, it will have to be compared with the financing, upgrade, trade-in, and leasing arrangements available in each market.

Getting a new iPhone every two years may be very pleasant. What is rather less amusing is discovering after 24 monthly payments that we have paid a substantial amount and the only object we actually own is the charging cable.

Google receives a serious warning from the European Union: Android will have to open up to other artificial intelligence assistants

For quite some time, we have discussed the difficulties Apple is facing in the European Union when integrating new artificial intelligence features into its devices. Anyone who thought these demands applied only to Apple will now have to reconsider, because Google has encountered a rather similar situation.

The European Commission has adopted two binding decisions that directly affect two of Google's most important pillars: Android and its search engine. As if that were not enough, a few days later it also imposed a fine of 890 million euros for breaches of the Digital Markets Act.

This is therefore more than a simple warning. The European Union is forcing Google to change some of the rules on which it has built a large part of its business.

Android will have to allow assistants other than Gemini

The first decision concerns artificial intelligence on Android.

We can already install different assistants and artificial intelligence applications, but that does not mean they all have the same opportunities. Gemini is playing at home. Because it belongs to Google, it can integrate more deeply with Android, be activated from particular parts of the system, access the phone's context, and perform actions inside other applications.

An artificial intelligence service from another company may be installed, but it does not necessarily receive the same keys to every room.

The European Commission considers that this difference prevents genuine competition. It has therefore ordered Google to allow other artificial intelligence assistants to access eleven important Android functions under equivalent conditions.

Among other things, users must be able to activate their chosen assistant with a voice command, in a way similar to the current use of “Hey Google”. They must also be able to ask it to act inside applications, complete tasks across several of them, or use certain information from the device's context to provide more useful answers and actions.

This could make it possible, for example, to choose another artificial intelligence service as the main assistant and use it to reply to messages, request a taxi, add an appointment to the calendar, or complete a task that requires moving between several applications.

The Commission estimates that around 60 per cent of users in the European Union have Android phones, so this opening could have a considerable impact.

What could this mean for a blind person?

For our community, the story is particularly interesting.

A voice assistant is not merely a convenience. In many cases, it is a quick way to control the phone without having to move through applications, find buttons, or cross screens that are not always as accessible as they should be.

Being able to choose the default assistant could open the door to alternatives that understand natural language better, describe images more accurately, work more effectively with documents, or perform tasks that Gemini still does not handle satisfactorily.

It could also encourage specialist companies to develop assistants designed specifically for people with disabilities.

However, Android's technical support for this integration does not guarantee that every assistant will be accessible. An application can receive extensive permissions and still contain unlabelled buttons, confusing screens, or a poor TalkBack experience.

Opening the system can therefore create opportunities, but we will then have to see how each company uses them.

It does not mean we can replace Gemini tomorrow

Expectations need to be kept under control, because these measures will not be implemented overnight.

The final decision was adopted on July 16, 2026. Google must incorporate most of the measures into Android 18 by August 1, 2027, at the latest. Simultaneous activation of several assistants through wake words has a longer deadline: Android 19, and no later than August 1, 2028. The Commission will supervise the process, and Google must provide regular progress reports for the next two years.

We do not yet know which assistants will be interested in using these functions, what requirements they will have to meet, or how the choice will be made in Android settings.

Nor is Google being required to hand complete control of the phone to any application that appears in Google Play. There will have to be permissions, security controls, and mechanisms to protect user data.

Google warns about the risks

As expected, Google has not welcomed the decision enthusiastically.

The company says that external assistants can already use some Android functions safely and that phone manufacturers participate in reviewing them. According to Google, opening more sensitive permissions could endanger device security and allow certain applications to access particularly delicate information.

The argument is not new. It closely resembles the one Apple has used to explain its difficulties with artificial intelligence in the European Union.

Both companies are essentially saying that an advanced assistant needs to know the content of messages, emails, photographs, calendars, and applications if it is to act in a genuinely useful way. But the more data and permissions it receives, the greater the potential harm from an untrustworthy or poorly protected application.

The European Commission responds that opening must be carried out with safeguards and that the decision already includes measures to protect privacy, device integrity, and user security.

Google will also have to share data from its search engine

The second decision concerns Google Search.

For years, every search, every displayed result, and every selected link has helped Google improve its service. The more people use the search engine, the more information the company obtains to understand what users want to find and which results are genuinely useful.

That enormous volume of information provides an advantage that is extremely difficult for a small search engine or a new artificial intelligence service to match.

The European Commission has ordered Google to share certain search data with competitors that meet the requirements. This includes both traditional search engines and artificial intelligence chatbots that use Internet searches to provide up-to-date answers.

An important clarification is needed here: this does not mean giving other companies each user's personal search history.

Google will have to share anonymised data relating to queries, result positions, clicks, and views. Account names, IP addresses, and individual histories will not be provided. Queries that may contain full names, addresses, passwords, bank-account numbers, or other sensitive information must also be removed or altered.

Recipients will not be allowed to use the data to create advertising profiles, train general-purpose artificial intelligence models, or systematically reconstruct Google's results. They may use it only to improve their own search services.

Before receiving the information, they will have to demonstrate compliance with the security measures, undergo an independent audit, and repeat those checks periodically.

What could change for us?

In the medium term, this measure could help other search engines and assistants provide more accurate results.

One of the major weaknesses of some artificial intelligence systems appears when they have to find recent information on the Internet. They may write very well, but they do not always locate the best sources or rank the results correctly.

If these services receive data that helps them improve query understanding, indexing, and result selection, they could provide more reliable answers.

It might also encourage the growth of privacy-focused search engines or tools that present information in a cleaner and more accessible form.

Even so, sharing data does not automatically turn a small search engine into another Google. Creating and maintaining an index of the Internet, updating millions of pages, and answering any query quickly still requires enormous infrastructure.

The measure may reduce part of Google's advantage, but it does not remove every obstacle.

Nor will the process be immediate. During August 2026, Google must publish information enabling interested companies to apply for access. In September, it must provide contracts and test data; in November, the anonymised data set must be ready; and in January 2027, it must finalise its pricing proposal.

And, in addition, a fine of 890 million euros

These two obligations are accompanied by a substantial financial penalty.

On July 23, 2026, the European Commission fined Google a total of 890 million euros for two separate breaches of the Digital Markets Act.

The first fine, of 460 million euros, concerns the search engine. The Commission says Google favoured its own shopping, hotel, transport, and sports-results services by placing them more prominently than equivalent services from other companies.

The second fine, of 430 million euros, concerns Google Play. According to the Commission, Google restricted developers' freedom to tell customers that they could find lower prices or purchase subscriptions through websites and alternative stores.

This does not mean Google Play will disappear or that every application will become free. It means that a developer must be allowed to tell users that another way to buy or subscribe exists without Google imposing restrictions or commissions regarded as excessive.

The Commission has ordered Google to end these practices.

Google and Apple have chosen different paths

The comparison with Apple is unavoidable.

Google chose to integrate Gemini deeply into Android and make it available to users in the European Union while resolving its differences with the Commission. Users have been able to use the service, but the company must now open part of that integration to competitors.

Apple has been more cautious. It has preferred to delay some artificial intelligence features rather than release them and later be forced to share particular forms of access with other companies.

They are different strategies, but both companies have ended up facing the same issue: the European Union does not want the owner of an operating system to reserve for its own artificial intelligence functions that it denies to everyone else.

The positive side of Google's approach is that its users did not have to wait to use Gemini. The less comfortable part comes now, as it must modify Android so that this advantage is no longer exclusive.

What really matters

This is not simply a question of deciding whether the European Union is for or against Google.

What is at stake is who controls the most important functions on our phones and how much choice we have.

Opening Android may encourage competition and allow better, more specialised, or more accessible assistants to appear. But it also requires great care with permissions that may provide access to messages, photographs, documents, location, and other personal data.

Sharing information from the search engine may help create more capable alternatives. But it will be necessary to confirm that anonymisation works and that recipients respect the established conditions.

Opening alone does not guarantee better services. Security alone should not be used as an excuse for a company to reserve every advantage for itself.

The balance will not be simple. But one point is becoming increasingly clear: in the European Union, owning the operating system no longer means being entitled to keep all the keys exclusively.

Although these obligations arise from European Union law, some of the technical solutions could eventually influence Android beyond that territory. That will depend on how Google chooses to implement and distribute the changes.

Foldable phones are no longer a curiosity: what can they offer a blind person?

Samsung has just introduced its new generation of foldable phones and, this time, it has not limited itself to updating a couple of models. The company has presented a fairly complete family consisting of the Galaxy Z Flip8, Galaxy Z Fold8, and Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra.

The Flip8 retains the clamshell format: a normal-sized phone that folds in half to take up less room. The Fold8 models open like a book and turn the phone into a kind of small tablet. The Fold8 Ultra, Samsung's new flagship, has an eight-inch inner screen, weighs 215 grams, and is only 4.1 millimetres thick when open. Samsung also says it has reduced the visibility of the crease and strengthened the hinge.

Whether or not we like these phones, the presentation makes one point fairly clear: foldables are no longer the strange experiment that some brands displayed merely to show how far their technology could go.

They have stopped asking permission to enter the market. They are already here.

They are still a minority

We should not be carried away by manufacturers' enthusiasm. Foldable phones still represent a very small share of worldwide sales. In 2025, they accounted for approximately 1.6 per cent of phones sold.

The difference is that, while the overall phone market is stagnant or even declining, foldable sales are expected to grow by around 20 per cent during 2026. Interest is also increasing in book-style models, particularly at the high end of the market.

We cannot yet say that foldables are the future of every phone. What we can say is that they are no longer simply a Samsung indulgence.

Two different ways to fold a phone

The term foldable phone actually covers two quite different ideas.

First, there are clamshell models such as the Galaxy Z Flip and Motorola Razr. They are used like conventional phones but can fold in half for easier storage.

Their main advantage is not a larger screen. It is that a fairly tall phone can become a small, compact object.

Then there are book-style foldables such as the Galaxy Z Fold. When closed, they are used as phones, although they are usually somewhat thicker. When opened, they reveal an inner screen close to the size of a small tablet.

These are the models now receiving the most attention. They allow two applications to be placed side by side, content to be moved between them, documents to be handled, or the phone to be used as a small pocket computer.

Android now explicitly supports this kind of device. On large screens, several applications can run at the same time, windows can be resized, and items can be dragged from one application to another.

Samsung opened the way, but it is no longer alone

Samsung was the company that pushed hardest to turn foldables into a commercial product. Its first models had problems, some of them quite serious, but the company did not abandon the idea. It has continued to improve the hinges, reduce thickness, increase durability, and adapt Android to two screens.

That persistence has given Samsung an advantage, although it now has many more competitors.

Google offers the Pixel 10 Pro Fold. It has an eight-inch inner screen, a gearless hinge, and IP68 protection against water and dust, something that for years seemed particularly difficult to achieve in a foldable device. Google has also worked on continuity between screens and the simultaneous use of two applications.

Motorola, which had already revived the clamshell format with its Razr family, has also moved into book-style foldables. Its Motorola Razr Fold has a 6.6-inch outer screen and an 8.1-inch inner display. In other words, Motorola is no longer content with making a phone smaller when folded; it also wants to compete in the market for phones that become tablets.

HONOR has introduced the Magic V6, with which it aims to show that a foldable does not necessarily have to be heavy and bulky. It measures 8.75 millimetres when closed and weighs around 219 grams, figures that bring it quite close to certain conventional high-end phones.

OPPO continues its Find N series. The new Find N6 focuses particularly on reducing the crease left in the screen and on providing tools for working with several applications.

Huawei offers models such as the Mate X7 and has gone even further with phones that fold twice, becoming a screen of around ten inches when fully opened. Xiaomi and vivo have also developed their own foldable families, although the availability of some models varies considerably from one country to another.

In short, almost every major Android manufacturer has now tried this category. Some sell their foldables worldwide and others reserve them for particular markets, but the direction is fairly clear.

Apple remains the major absentee

Apple is the only major owner of a mobile operating system that still does not sell a foldable phone.

No foldable iPhone has been officially announced. Everything known about it comes from leaks, supply-chain reports, and people connected with the project. We should therefore not present as certain anything that Apple has not confirmed.

That said, the reports increasingly agree that Apple is working on this format.

Several reports place the possible introduction of the first foldable iPhone in the second half of 2026, perhaps alongside the iPhone 18 Pro models. The date is not final, however, and the project is reported to have encountered engineering difficulties. According to the leaks, it would open like a book rather than use a clamshell design.

There has also been talk of an inner display close to the size of a small tablet and an adaptation of iOS to make better use of the space when the device is open. Apple has confirmed none of these details.

We do not know what its commercial name will be, how much it will cost, when it will arrive, or what its final specifications will be. Nor do we know whether Apple will manage to reduce the screen crease significantly, as some leaks claim.

What does seem fairly likely is that, if it eventually arrives, it will not be cheap.

Apple does not usually enter a new category by competing on price, particularly when the product would combine some of the functions of an iPhone and a small tablet.

Is Apple arriving late?

It is often said that Apple is late to foldables. From a strictly chronological point of view, that is true. Samsung has been selling them for several generations, and other manufacturers have had time to experiment.

But arriving late does not always mean arriving too late.

Apple was not the first company to make a music player, smartphone, tablet, or connected watch. Its usual strategy is to wait until the technology is sufficiently mature, observe where others fail, and then present a more controlled version.

That does not guarantee that its foldable will be better. Apple makes mistakes too. But it does mean that we are unlikely to see a foldable iPhone presented merely as a technology demonstration.

Before Apple takes the step, it will need to be convinced that the hinge can withstand years of use, that the flexible screen will not generate too many repairs, and that iOS offers more than simply stretching applications across a larger surface.

Apple's entry could also have an important effect on the wider market. Display manufacturers expect strong growth in foldable panels during 2026, driven in part by the anticipated arrival of the first iPhone of this kind.

What a foldable can offer a blind person

We now come to the part that really interests us.

For a sighted person, the advantage of an eight-inch inner screen is obvious. It offers larger videos, more comfortable photo editing and reading, and the ability to open several applications.

A totally blind person does not automatically receive those advantages. We do not need a larger screen to see a film more clearly or to look at a photograph.

It therefore makes no sense to repeat manufacturers' advertising claims and assume that a foldable will necessarily be better for us.

It may have advantages, but they are different ones.

A clamshell phone is smaller when closed. It may fit better in a pocket, a small bag, or a compartment from which a conventional phone would protrude.

Physically opening and closing the phone can also become a fairly natural way to begin or end certain actions, particularly calls. This will depend on the model and its settings, but it is an easy physical interaction to understand.

Book-style models provide a much larger touch surface. This could be comfortable for people who use braille screen input, people with motor difficulties, or those who prefer to explore items by direct touch rather than continually moving with left and right swipes.

They may also be useful for people with low vision, because text and controls can be enlarged without reducing the amount of information on screen as severely.

Another useful feature is the ability to place the device partly open on a table. The hinge can serve as its own stand.

This could make a video call, a Be My Eyes conversation, or the use of an application that needs the camera kept pointed at an object easier. We would not have to hold the phone continuously or find a separate stand.

The large screen may also support new artificial intelligence functions. For example, it could keep a document open while an assistant is used, allow a description to be consulted without closing the previous application, or combine the camera with step-by-step instructions.

Not all these possibilities are currently handled well with TalkBack, but the format provides room for them to develop.

A larger screen does not make the screen reader faster

This deserves a pause, because a larger screen is sometimes confused with greater accessibility.

TalkBack and VoiceOver present much of the information in a linear form. We move through headings, buttons, fields, and controls, usually one after another.

An eight-inch screen rather than a six-inch one does not necessarily reduce the number of gestures needed to reach a button. Nor does it magically make a poorly labelled application accessible.

The opposite can even happen.

When an application shows two columns, several panels, or two windows at once, the screen-reader route may become longer and less predictable. What represents increased productivity for a sighted person may become a screen with many more elements for us to move through.

Multitasking will be useful only if TalkBack or VoiceOver allows us to move clearly from one application to another, keeps focus correctly, and prevents users from going round in circles without knowing which window they are in.

The problem of changing screens

A foldable has at least two configurations: closed and open.

When we move from one to the other, the application must reorganise its content. Some controls change position, new columns appear, and the entire interface may be redrawn.

Android provides mechanisms to preserve continuity, but the final result also depends on each application.

For a blind person, it can be particularly annoying if TalkBack loses the reading position when the phone is opened and returns to the beginning of the screen. An application may also close, reload its content, or change the order of its controls.

These problems seldom appear in commercial presentations, but they may matter far more than screen resolution or the number of camera megapixels.

The outer display does not always provide the complete experience either. On some clamshell phones, it supports only certain applications or functions. If TalkBack does not behave correctly on that small screen, we will have to open the device even for a simple task.

Two hands are not always available

There is another issue that seems particularly important for us.

Many book-style foldables require two hands to open comfortably. Some clamshell phones also have considerable resistance in the hinge.

A blind person may be holding a cane, a guide-dog harness, a bag, or another object in one hand. Having to use both hands to check the phone is not always practical.

A conventional phone can be taken from a pocket, unlocked, and used with one hand. With some foldables, that simplicity is lost.

It may sound like a minor detail until we try to open the phone while standing at a stop, walking along the street, or holding something else.

More parts, more possibilities for failure

Current foldables are much more durable than the first models. Hinges have improved, screens have several protective layers, and some devices now offer reasonable water and dust resistance.

The basic fact remains, however: a foldable phone has more moving parts than a conventional one.

It has a hinge, a flexible screen, and cables that must withstand thousands of openings and closings. All this creates additional potential points of wear.

The inner screen is also not usually as hard as the outer glass of a traditional phone. It must be treated more carefully, avoiding pressure from sharp objects or fingernails.

For a blind person who continually explores the screen by touch, this does not mean it will necessarily be damaged. The screens are designed to be touched. But it does require greater familiarity with the device and care with certain gestures, as well as checking that no dirt is trapped between the two halves before closing it.

There is also the cost of repairs. Replacing a foldable screen or hinge can be considerably more expensive than repairing a conventional phone.

The price is still difficult to justify

Although the number of models is increasing, foldables remain concentrated mainly in the high-end market.

It is not enough to ask whether we can afford one. We must ask what we receive in return.

A blind person who mainly uses a phone for calls, messages, WhatsApp, listening to content, assistants, and ordinary applications can do exactly the same things with a considerably cheaper conventional phone.

In that case, paying much more for an inner screen that will barely be used does not seem the most sensible decision.

A foldable begins to make sense when its format solves a specific need: we want a phone that is small when stored, need a large surface for writing, have low vision, work with several applications, or use the hinge to position the camera.

Buying one only because it is different or represents the latest novelty seems to me like paying too much for a solution when we still do not know what problem it is meant to solve for us.

Not all foldables offer the same accessibility

We should also avoid speaking about foldables as though they were all identical.

A Samsung Galaxy, Google Pixel, Motorola, HONOR, or Huawei may use different versions of Android, different screen readers, and custom interfaces that substantially change the experience.

Samsung and Google have a recognised history in accessibility. That does not mean everything works perfectly, but documentation, updates, and the experiences of other users are easier to find.

With brands that have more limited distribution, it may be harder to know how TalkBack, braille screen input, banking applications, or the change between outer and inner screens will behave.

Before spending a substantial amount, a blind person should try the exact model. Trying another foldable from the same brand is not enough, nor is listening only to the opinion of someone who uses the screen visually.

At a minimum, TalkBack, the transition between both displays, the braille keyboard, calls, the camera, ordinary applications, and accessibility gestures should all be tested.

So, are they the future?

My impression is that foldable phones will not replace conventional phones in the immediate future.

For most people, a traditional phone remains cheaper, more durable, lighter, and simpler to use.

But I do not believe foldables will disappear either.

Manufacturers are rapidly correcting their main weaknesses. The devices are becoming thinner and lighter, their batteries are improving, and applications are beginning to adapt to large screens.

Apple's possible entry could provide the additional push they still lack. Not because Apple will invent the foldable phone, but because many developers may take this format more seriously if they also have to adapt their applications for iOS.

That could benefit us indirectly. Better adaptation of applications, clearer window management, and greater attention to continuity between screens may also improve the experience with screen readers.

My conclusion

I would not say that a blind person needs a foldable phone.

I would say that, for the first time, there are reasons why some blind people may find one useful.

A clamshell model may interest someone who places great value on carrying a small phone. A book-style foldable may appeal to users with low vision, people who type braille on the screen, or those who need a larger surface for work.

For everyone else, the benefits may still fall far short of the price, the delicacy, and the inconvenience of having to open the device with both hands.

Foldables have ceased to be an extravagance, but they have not yet become a necessity.

That may be the most sensible place to put them at present: they are now worth watching, but there is still no reason to run after them.

Other stories discussed in recent days

Not everything important this week needs several pages. Here are ten other stories that have also attracted attention and are worth keeping in sight.

1. An OpenAI evaluation agent accessed Hugging Face systems without authorisation

OpenAI and Hugging Face have acknowledged a serious incident during an evaluation of cybersecurity capabilities. An autonomous agent moved beyond the restrictions intended for the test and accessed Hugging Face systems without authorisation for several days. OpenAI took time to connect that activity with its own agent. The case raises an uncomfortable question once again: what controls are sufficient when artificial intelligence can act for long periods with little supervision?

2. Meta AI begins completing tasks for us and repeating them when required

Meta has begun turning its assistant into more than a place where questions are asked. In some markets it can already connect to email and calendars, prepare daily summaries, research a subject, create presentations, and handle recurring tasks such as a weekly meal plan or an update on topics that interest us. For now, these capabilities are reaching the Meta AI application and website; the company says they will also appear progressively in WhatsApp.

3. Anthropic introduces Claude Opus 5

Anthropic has launched Claude Opus 5, a model designed to approach the capabilities of its most powerful systems at a substantially lower cost. The company is directing it towards everyday work, programming, and office tasks. Beyond the name, the story confirms how quickly this sector is moving: models that appeared exceptional only a few months ago are becoming faster, cheaper tools available to more people.

4. iOS 27 receives its fourth developer beta

Apple released the fourth beta of iOS 27 on July 20, together with the corresponding test versions of its other systems. This release continues development and includes changes and corrections documented in the beta notes. As always, it remains test software: it is useful to know what is changing, but that is not an invitation to install it on the only iPhone we depend on every day.

5. WhatsApp expands its capabilities on the iPad, in the car, and with PDF documents

WhatsApp is rolling out several new features. It now allows an account to be created directly on an iPad without first linking it to a phone. It is also updating its operation in CarPlay and Android Auto so that messages can be heard and answered, calls made, and favourites accessed by voice. The web and desktop versions will be able to open PDF documents and add small annotations, while music from Apple Music or Spotify can be shared in status updates.

6. Alexa Plus begins understanding much more complex household instructions

Amazon is expanding Alexa Plus so that we do not need to know every setting on a connected appliance. Instead of manually choosing between numerous programmes, we will be able to explain what we need and let the assistant decide which function to use. It could, for example, interpret how we want a garment washed and select the appropriate cycle. It is also being prepared to book services and complete payments through an Amazon account. For many blind people, this kind of natural control may be considerably more useful than spectacular demonstrations that later fail to solve anything ordinary.

7. Google is preparing a Gboard function to convert sign language into text

References have appeared in Gboard's code to a possible function called Sign-to-text. The idea would be to use the camera and artificial intelligence to interpret sign language and convert it into text that could be sent in messages, emails, or searches. Google has not announced it, and a function found in code may change or never be released. Important questions also remain, including which sign languages it would recognise and with what accuracy, but the project deserves attention because of what it could mean for accessibility.

8. Samsung shows its own Gemini smart glasses

Samsung has also shown smart glasses developed on Android XR with Google, Gentle Monster, and Warby Parker. They combine a camera, microphones, speakers, and Gemini to read information, summarise messages, translate, provide directions, and answer questions about what the camera captures. On paper, they offer interesting possibilities for blind people, but Samsung has not presented them as a specific accessibility aid. We will have to find out what they actually describe, how they are controlled, and how usable they are without sight.

9. The European Union considers TikTok's protection of minors insufficient

The European Commission has provisionally concluded that TikTok does not adequately protect minors' privacy. Among the concerns is the ease with which certain accounts may remain visible or be found by adults. TikTok will have an opportunity to respond before a final decision is made, but the warning could lead to mandatory changes and a substantial fine if the platform does not satisfy the Commission.

10. Qualcomm warns of price increases for its processors

Qualcomm has reportedly told customers that it will apply double-digit increases to products shipped from September because its own costs have risen. It may sound like a distant story, but its processors are used in an enormous number of Android phones and other devices. If manufacturers pass the increase on to buyers, the next generation of phones could arrive with even less friendly prices.
