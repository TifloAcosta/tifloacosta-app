The digital euro wants to be accessible from birth: the ECB turns to Fundación ONCE so no one is left out

Europe is continuing to prepare the digital euro and, this time, the story is of particular interest to those of us who use screen readers and other assistive technologies: the European Central Bank is working with Fundación ONCE so that accessibility is part of the project from the very beginning.

And that "from the very beginning" is precisely what matters.

We all know applications that are designed first, developed second, barriers are discovered later, and finally someone asks where accessibility can be fitted in without changing too much of what has already been built. Here they are trying to work in exactly the opposite way: define requirements, test prototypes and identify problems before the product reaches millions of people.

Before we get into screen readers, keyboards and the rest of the family, it is worth clarifying exactly what the digital euro is, because the name may lead people to think of cryptocurrencies.

It will not be a cryptocurrency, a stablecoin or a currency different from the euro. Nor is it simply the balance we already see today in our bank account. The idea is to create a digital form of public money issued by the Eurosystem, equivalent to cash. One digital euro would be worth exactly one euro.

The aim is for it to be usable for paying in shops, buying online or sending money to another person. Access could be through a dedicated digital euro app or through banks and other payment service providers.

And no, the project is not trying to send banknotes and coins to a museum. The European proposal insists that the digital euro should complement cash, not replace it. Anyone who wants to continue paying with physical money should be able to do so.

This is where Fundación ONCE comes in.

The ECB and Fundación ONCE signed a cooperation agreement in February 2026 focused on the accessibility of the future app. This is not simply a matter of testing the finished product and deciding whether it passes or fails an exam. The collaboration includes technical advice, participation in the design process and accessibility testing once sufficiently advanced versions and prototypes exist.

Fundación ONCE has also explained that people with disabilities who are specialists in accessibility and user experience will take part in the work. That matters because checking a list of requirements is one thing, while actually using an app to pay, confirm a transaction, correct an error or understand what is happening when something goes wrong is quite another.

The ECB has been spelling out what that accessibility means in practice.

The app will have to allow operations to be carried out by keyboard, keep the focus correctly identified and provide compatibility with screen readers. There are also requirements covering contrast, adaptation of text and controls, reduced motion and mechanisms to avoid problematic visual content.

When there is a time limit that could cause information to be lost, the user must be warned in advance. It sounds like a small detail until we remember those pages where we are just finishing checking something and suddenly the session decides that our relationship has come to an end.

Cognitive accessibility is also part of the design.

The ECB proposes processes that allow certain operations to be confirmed, corrected or cancelled, contextual help when a label is not enough, and language that is as clear as possible. The intention is to avoid abbreviations, unnecessary jargon and ambiguous expressions.

That last point may seem secondary, but it is not in an app related to money. If a person does not understand what will happen when they press a button, the interface has already failed before that button is pressed.

There is also an important qualification to make about a claim that has circulated in some news reports: the app has not committed to meeting absolutely every WCAG AAA criterion.

The technical approach starts from the accessibility requirements applicable in Europe, with a baseline equivalent to level AA, and adds selected level AAA criteria where they are appropriate for a payment application. In other words, the stated aim is to go beyond the minimum, but that does not mean a promise has been made to achieve full AAA conformance in every area.

Another interesting element is offline operation.

The digital euro is being designed to allow certain offline payments. The idea is that two nearby devices could exchange digital euros without depending on an Internet connection at that moment. This could be useful in areas with poor coverage, during network outages or in emergency situations.

The offline mode is also the one that promises the highest level of privacy. In those payments, the intention is for personal details to remain essentially between the payer and the recipient. In online transactions, the system is being designed so that the Eurosystem cannot directly identify the person and link them to each purchase, although banks and payment service providers will still have legal obligations relating to identification, fraud prevention and anti-money laundering.

Put another way: privacy does not mean absolute anonymity or a complete absence of controls. It is worth explaining it this way so that an important protection is not turned into a promise the project does not make.

The next major step will arrive, if the schedule is maintained, in the second half of 2027.

The ECB has selected 36 payment service providers to take part in a twelve-month pilot exercise. Person-to-person payments, in-store purchases, e-commerce and both online and offline operation will be tested. The pilot will take place within the ECB itself and at 19 national central banks.

And here it is worth correcting another figure that has been repeatedly reported: those 19 are not commercial banks, but national central banks.

Usability and accessibility testing will continue in parallel, and this will be particularly interesting for seeing whether everything that works beautifully in a technical presentation still works when a person opens the app, switches on a screen reader and wants to pay without having to ask anyone for permission.

Then there is one fundamental piece left: politics.

The digital euro has not yet been finally approved. The European institutions are continuing to work on the legislative framework, and the European Central Bank will only be able to make a formal decision on issuance once the corresponding legal basis exists.

If everything follows the planned schedule, the ECB wants to be ready for a possible first issuance during 2029.

So 2029 is a target, not a confirmed appointment in our calendar.

The most important thing today is something else.

Before the final app even exists, accessibility already has a seat at the design table.

That does not guarantee that everything will turn out perfectly. No presentation, standard or collaboration can replace real-world testing. But starting by asking how everyone will be able to access it is much better than ending by asking how we fix what we left out.

For those of us who use assistive technology, that is where the real story lies.

The twentieth-anniversary iPhone: Apple is preparing something big for 2027 and the leaks are starting to fit together

On January 9, 2007, Steve Jobs introduced a device that combined a phone, an iPod and an Internet device. It was called iPhone and Apple said at the time, with that modesty so characteristic of major presentations, that it was years ahead of any other phone.

In 2027, two decades will have passed since that moment and, if the leaks that have been piling up for months are correct, Apple is not planning to celebrate the anniversary by merely changing the processor, moving a camera two millimeters and naming a new color after something that sounds as though it came from a dessert menu.

Everything points to a special iPhone and one of the biggest design changes of recent years.

First, a warning that will stay with us throughout this section: Apple has announced nothing.

We are talking about a product expected in 2027. Plans can change, a technology may not be ready in time, and a prototype can disappear before we even know it ever existed.

But this is not the typical rumor born from a blurry photograph posted by a newly created account either.

Bloomberg's Mark Gurman has been talking for some time about a special iPhone for the twentieth anniversary. The Information later added details about the design and roadmap, and various sources in the Asian supply chain have been describing pieces that fit reasonably well into the same puzzle.

Will it be called iPhone 20?

We do not know.

It is the most convenient name for us to use, and it would make sense for Apple to do something similar to what happened in 2017, when it went from the iPhone 8 to the iPhone X to mark ten years. However, there is still conflicting information about the naming of the 2027 generation.

So here we will simply call it the twentieth-anniversary iPhone and avoid arguing with a name that does not yet exist.

The most consistent rumor has one word at center stage: glass.

Reports describe a high-end model built around a large glass surface, with a nearly edge-to-edge display and curves extending toward the sides.

The Information has even spoken of glass curving across the front, the back and all four edges, leaving a relatively narrow metal band around the middle of the side frame.

The intention seems clear: when looking at the phone from the front, almost everything that is not screen should visually disappear.

And this is not a new idea at Apple.

When the company introduced the iPhone X in 2017, it explained that it had spent years pursuing a device in which the display completely dominated the front. That model removed the Home button, introduced Face ID and changed the way the iPhone was used.

Apple said at the time that it marked the beginning of the next ten years.

Those ten years end precisely in 2027.

Supply-chain sources also speak of an OLED display specially prepared to achieve small curves on all four sides. It would not necessarily be the kind of "waterfall" display seen on some Android phones, where the image seemed to spill over the edge, but a much subtler curve intended to make the bezels visually smaller.

The term micro-curve has even been used.

And this is where the engineering difficulties begin.

Bending an OLED panel evenly in four directions can affect brightness, image quality and durability. That is why reports have appeared about tests with Samsung Display and LG Display, new materials and specific manufacturing processes.

That does not prove the final product will look exactly like this, but it does make the rumor considerably more serious than an attractive concept drawing circulating on social media.

Then we come to the big question: will the Dynamic Island disappear?

This is where the sources begin to disagree.

Apple's apparent goal is to hide the components required for Face ID beneath the display and, if the technology is ready in time, the front camera as well. The result would be a completely clean front surface.

The Information has talked about an under-display camera for 2027, and Bloomberg has long described Apple's desire to eliminate visible cutouts.

But hiding something is not the same as making it work at the quality level Apple requires.

Placing a camera behind a display has already been done on other phones. The difficulty is getting enough light through the panel without degrading the image too much. With Face ID, the challenge is greater because the system needs infrared sensors and enough precision to maintain biometric security.

That is why other sources, including display analysts, believe Apple could reach 2027 with an intermediate solution: a much smaller Dynamic Island, Face ID partly hidden beneath the display, or some small cutout that remains visible.

In short: Apple's ambition seems clear. The question is whether the technology will be in as much of a hurry as the design department.

There is also talk of two sizes, roughly equivalent to today's Pro and Pro Max, at around 6.3 and 6.9 inches. Some leaks have mentioned tests with a panel close to seven inches for the larger model.

If the bezels almost completely disappear, Apple can increase the usable display area without making the phone grow by exactly the same proportion.

For those of us who do not see the screen, this may sound like a distant aesthetic battle, but it has very practical consequences: size, weight, how the phone is held and the position of the controls.

And the controls are precisely one of the leaks we have the most reason to watch closely.

Apple has spent years experimenting with solid-state buttons. Instead of a mechanical button that physically moves inward, we would have a surface that detects pressure and a haptic system that creates the sensation of a click.

The company has already used a similar principle in the trackpads of certain MacBooks: we feel something moving much more than it actually moves.

Various leaks claim Apple has revived this project for the anniversary iPhone and is testing it with gloves, wet hands, cases and different temperatures.

The idea could be applied to the side button, volume controls, Action button and Camera Control.

And those of us who use VoiceOver have good reason to pay attention here.

The problem is not that a button might stop physically moving. The problem would be if the tactile reference that allows us to find it disappeared as well.

A sighted person can locate an area that is drawn, illuminated or visually marked on the side. We need to know where to place a finger.

If Apple keeps some raised shape, texture or other physical cue, the change may be perfectly manageable. If it aims for a completely smooth side and expects everyone to guess where the volume control is, we will have quite a lot to say about it.

We still do not know what the solution will be.

Important changes are expected inside the phone as well.

Current reports point to an A21 chip manufactured on a two-nanometer process. The interesting part is not only getting more power. A more advanced process can improve energy efficiency, which is especially important in a phone expected to combine new displays, artificial intelligence processing and a very tightly packed internal design.

There have also been rumors about batteries using a higher proportion of silicon in the anode. This technology can store more energy in a similar volume and provide longer battery life without forcing the device to become much thicker.

What I would not do yet is turn some specific figures circulating online, such as certain claimed battery capacities, into confirmed features. Some numbers reproduce with extraordinary ease when nobody asks them for documentation.

Apple is also said to be studying memory with much greater bandwidth, designed especially to move large amounts of data during artificial intelligence tasks carried out directly on the phone.

That makes sense within a trend affecting the entire industry: the more AI models are expected to run locally, the more important it becomes for processor and memory to exchange information quickly without sending power consumption through the roof.

The camera is another interesting area.

Apple is reportedly working on its own image sensors so that it can depend less on Sony and directly control more parts of its photographic system. Reports talk about improvements in dynamic range, meaning the ability to preserve information in both the very bright and very dark areas of a scene.

Rumors about 200 megapixels are also circulating, but at this point I would not put them at the same level of credibility as the redesign, the display or the A21.

Megapixels have a well-known habit of growing every time they travel from one webpage to another.

Something similar applies to the modem.

Apple has spent years trying to reduce its dependence on Qualcomm and develop its own communications technology. By 2027, a much more mature generation of those modems is expected, with efficiency improvements and deeper integration with the rest of the system.

It will not be the feature that earns Apple ten minutes of applause, but it can affect coverage, battery consumption and the overall behavior of communications. Sometimes the least spectacular things on a presentation slide are the ones we appreciate most throughout the day.

It is also difficult to ignore one aesthetic coincidence.

In 2025, Apple changed the visual language of its operating systems with Liquid Glass: transparency, refraction, depth and an interface that tries to behave visually like glass.

Apple never said that this was a preview of a glass-dominated iPhone for 2027.

But if a phone eventually appears dominated by curved surfaces and glass, it will be impossible not to look back and wonder whether Apple first showed us the language of the software and only later the object that language seemed designed for.

Will it arrive in September 2027?

That is the window most sources are pointing toward, although we are still far too early to carve it in stone.

Apple is also said to be reorganizing the annual iPhone schedule, splitting some models between spring and fall and reserving the most advanced ones for the second half of the year. A special anniversary iPhone would fit perfectly into that fall launch.

And how much will it cost?

Here the Internet has already started doing what it does best when it has no data: inventing figures with great confidence.

There are numbers of every kind, but none deserves a place in serious information yet.

We can assume that a special model using new display technologies and materials will not exactly be the budget iPhone of the family.

Everything else, for now, is arithmetic done with an imaginary calculator.

If we put together only the leaks that appear most consistently, the overall picture is becoming fairly well defined: Apple is preparing a special iPhone for 2027, glass will play a major role, the front will try to approach an uninterrupted display, there would be two sizes, and the company is working to hide sensors and simplify physical controls.

Then come the less certain pieces: a completely under-display camera, invisible Face ID, fully haptic buttons, new batteries, advanced memory and Apple's own camera sensor.

Some will arrive. Others may remain in the laboratory.

We still have more than a year to see how this puzzle clears up.

But at this point it already seems fairly unlikely that 2027 will simply be another September with a new processor and a slightly better camera.

Apple celebrated ten years with the iPhone X and changed an important part of how the phone was used in one stroke.

Twenty years later, it seems ready to try again.

And we will be watching closely, especially one question that is not very spectacular for headlines but is fundamental for accessibility: in this effort to make bezels, cameras and buttons disappear, let us hope the reference points we need in order to use the phone independently do not disappear as well.

WhatsApp is on the move: usernames, calls, more useful groups and two dates worth noting

WhatsApp has been having a fairly busy few months.

While we continue using it to send that message that was supposed to be three words long and somehow turns into a forty-minute conversation, Meta keeps adding features, changing others and preparing transformations that even affect one of the service's historical rules: relying on the phone number to identify us.

The new features have arrived little by little. And when an app works like that, we can end up with five new functions without being entirely sure when any of them appeared.

Let's put things in order.

One of the areas that keeps changing is groups.

WhatsApp is expanding the ways to draw the attention of everyone in a conversation, improve polls and create spin-off groups from people who already share another group.

Among the features being rolled out is the ability to mention every participant with @all. The idea is to avoid having to name people one by one when a message genuinely concerns the whole group.

WhatsApp is also adding controls so that this feature does not turn every group into a permanent emergency siren. Depending on the size of the group and its settings, there are limits on who can use it and specific options for muting those mentions.

Polls are evolving too. Improvements that have been tested and rolled out include setting an end time, gaining better control over how votes are displayed and correcting certain elements during a limited period.

They are small changes, but very useful for communities, associations, families, work groups or any place where twenty people are trying to decide something without the conversation ending up on a completely different subject.

Calls are taking an important step forward as well.

WhatsApp Web has been adding the ability to make and receive calls directly from the browser, reducing the gap that existed compared with the desktop applications.

This means that, on compatible systems, we can use the computer for a voice or video conversation without necessarily installing WhatsApp for Windows or Mac.

The company is also improving group calls, transfers between devices, call links and certain control options for the person organizing a meeting.

The general idea is simple: start a conversation on one device and continue it on another with fewer interruptions.

And then there is one of the most important changes in WhatsApp's history: usernames.

Ever since the service began, our phone number has effectively been our identity. To talk to someone, we usually give them the number, and in certain situations that information can end up visible to people with whom we never intended to share it.

WhatsApp is preparing an alternative.

Usernames will allow another person to find us or start a conversation without us necessarily having to hand over our phone number.

The feature will be optional.

It is not being designed as a huge public directory where anyone can type our name and start searching. The other person will have to know the exact username, and WhatsApp is preparing additional protections to limit unwanted contact.

Work has also been done on an optional key linked to the username. If we enable it, a person who wants to message us for the first time will need to know not only the username, but also that key.

For people who run communities, provide services, take part in large groups or simply prefer not to hand out their personal number quite so cheerfully, this could end up being a very significant change.

It will not necessarily reach every account at the same time. WhatsApp uses gradual rollouts, and some features appear first in certain countries, operating systems or versions.

So if someone finds an option that we still do not have, it does not automatically mean they have done anything special or that there is something wrong with our app.

Sometimes they simply got to the front of the queue earlier.

The iPad has also gained more independence.

WhatsApp officially arrived on iPad in 2025 and has continued expanding its capabilities during 2026. The app allows the tablet to be used increasingly like a primary device rather than merely as a secondary screen linked to the phone.

That can be particularly interesting for people who use a tablet as their main communication device.

Another feature Android users already knew, and which has now also reached the iPhone, is the ability to keep two WhatsApp accounts on the same device.

This makes it easier to separate a personal account from a professional one without carrying two phones.

Each account keeps its own conversations, settings and notifications, and the app provides cues so that we know which one is currently active.

It sounds like a minor detail until we imagine the message meant for work ending up in the family group.

Storage management has also improved.

It is now easier to enter a conversation, locate large files and delete what we no longer need without wiping out the entire history along with it.

In addition, some options allow us to clear multimedia content while keeping the text messages.

WhatsApp can occupy many gigabytes after years of use, and clearer controls help us avoid that classic advanced storage-management technique of checking the space used, sighing deeply and then starting to delete things at random.

Another interesting new feature is message translation inside the app itself.

We can select a message and translate it without copying it into another service. On Android, in certain languages and configurations, automatic translation of entire conversations has also been gradually introduced.

The processing is designed to take place locally on the device in order to protect the privacy of the content.

For those of us who take part in international groups, this is one of those features that seems small until we stop having to copy, leave WhatsApp, translate and come back again.

Sometimes the great technological breakthrough of the day is simply saving us four steps.

WhatsApp is also continuing to expand its presence in the car, with improvements in CarPlay and Android Auto for listening to messages, replying by voice, checking calls and accessing contacts or favorites without constantly handling the phone.

The priority, naturally, is still driving.

The best messaging feature to use while looking at the road continues to be the one that succeeds in keeping us from looking at the phone.

On computers, improvements have also appeared for working with documents, opening certain PDF files and carrying out small operations without leaving the conversation.

On top of that come social features, such as sharing music in Status, and a growing presence for Meta AI.

Meta's artificial intelligence can help with images, writing and certain queries. But here we need to remember that availability varies considerably by country, language and account.

If someone hears about a Meta AI feature and cannot find it in WhatsApp, they have not necessarily done anything wrong. It may simply not be available in their region yet.

Strict Account Settings also deserves a mention.

It is designed for people who may be targets of particularly sophisticated attacks. When enabled, WhatsApp automatically tightens several protections: it limits content from unknown senders, silences certain calls and applies more restrictive privacy settings.

It is not an option that everyone needs to switch on by default, but it shows how messaging apps are creating specific modes for profiles facing a higher level of risk.

And now comes the part that affects even people who do not want to use any new feature at all.

WhatsApp is continuing to withdraw support for older operating systems.

Here it is worth being very careful with those lists that say: "these twenty phones are losing WhatsApp."

What matters is not only the phone model. Compatibility is determined by the version of the operating system that the device can run.

On Android, there is one date worth writing down: September 8, 2026.

From that point, WhatsApp will raise its minimum requirement, and devices that remain on Android 5 will no longer be officially supported. The minimum will move to Android 6.

If our phone still uses Android 5 but can be updated to Android 6 or later, the solution is to update it.

If the manufacturer has left the device stuck on Android 5, then we really will have a compatibility problem.

And solving that by installing modified APKs or strange versions of WhatsApp found in some corner of the Internet does not sound like a particularly good idea. We are talking about an app that contains contacts, conversations, documents and a significant part of our digital lives.

It is not the best place to experiment with software of uncertain origin.

For iPhone and iPad, the date is November 30, 2026.

From that day, WhatsApp will require at least iOS 15.5 or iPadOS 15.5.

The good news is that this change does not necessarily force us to abandon iPhones that can currently use WhatsApp. Many older models capable of running the early versions of iOS 15 can also be updated to a later release within that same generation and therefore meet the requirement.

So anyone using iOS 15.1, 15.2, 15.3 or 15.4 should not interpret the warning as an instruction to go shopping.

First, check whether an update is available.

On iPhone, we can do that by going to Settings, General and Software Update.

On Android, the path varies by manufacturer, but we will normally find the installed version under Settings, Phone information, About phone or a similarly named section.

It is worth doing this in good time and, before changing devices or carrying out an important migration, checking that the WhatsApp backup is correctly configured.

Backups have a curious habit: they seem terribly boring right up until the exact day we need them.

Put all these developments together and we end up with an app that is quite different from the one that once did little more than send messages.

WhatsApp wants to be on the phone, the tablet, the computer, the browser, the watch and the car.

It wants to let us talk without necessarily handing over our number, it is strengthening groups, turning calls into a more multi-device feature, and adding translation, artificial intelligence and new security tools.

Some of these changes will significantly alter the way we use it.

Others we will try twice and then have to ask where they were.

But among all these changes, there are two dates that are especially easy to remember.

September 8, 2026: Android 5 is out.

November 30, 2026: iPhone and iPad will need at least iOS or iPadOS 15.5.

So before retiring a phone because a headline says so, let us first check which operating system it has and how far it can be updated.

Changing a phone is expensive enough without doing it before we have to.

Gemini takes command on Android: Google Assistant's farewell begins on September 4

For years, millions of Android users have said two words before asking the phone for almost anything:

"Hey Google."

Then came things like "set an alarm," "call Peter," "what will the weather be like tomorrow?", "take me home" or "turn on the living-room light."

Those first two words will continue to work.

What changes is who answers.

Google has set a date for the definitive handover on mobile devices: starting September 4, 2026, it will begin retiring the classic Google Assistant, and Gemini will become Google's assistant on compatible Android devices.

This time, it is no longer simply an invitation to try Gemini and then return to Assistant if we are not convinced.

Once the retirement reaches our device, the old Assistant will no longer be available and we will not be able to switch back to it.

A veteran that has been with us since 2016 is leaving.

And in comes someone much more conversational, capable of understanding complex requests and working with different applications, although it will still have to prove that it remembers all those small commands its predecessor learned over almost a decade.

First, let us not organize a funeral at midnight on September 4.

Google will begin the retirement that day, but the process will be gradual and may take several weeks to reach every user.

Two people with similar phones may spend a few days having different experiences. One will still see Assistant while the other will already have Gemini.

That will not necessarily mean something is wrong.

It is simply another staged rollout of the kind big technology companies use so that we can all compare who received the same thing first.

Which devices are included in the change?

Mainly Android phones and tablets that are compatible with Gemini.

It also affects certain devices that depend on the phone: compatible Wear OS watches, headphones and hearing aids prepared for Gemini, and Android Auto when it is projected from the phone.

In other words, Gemini does not intend to stay locked inside the phone screen.

Google wants it to accompany us on the watch, in the headphones and in the car.

In Android Auto, we will still be able to invoke it with "Hey Google," the voice button on the steering wheel or the corresponding control on the display.

The way in looks very familiar.

What changes is the brain behind it.

But be careful with cars that have Google built in directly from the factory.

Those vehicles follow a different schedule. Google Assistant may continue to work in them after September 4 while Google carries out its own transition to Gemini.

The same applies at home.

Speakers, smart displays, cameras, doorbells and other devices in the home ecosystem are evolving through Gemini for Home, but that process should not be confused with the retirement date for the mobile assistant.

The overall philosophy is the same, though.

The old Assistant worked very well when we knew exactly which command it expected.

Gemini is trying to let us express ourselves in a more natural way.

Instead of remembering a rigid phrase, we can explain our intention.

"Turn on the living-room lights, but leave the kitchen off because I'm not going in there anymore" is the kind of request that better illustrates where Google wants to go.

Assistant executed commands.

Gemini tries to understand what we are trying to accomplish.

So what happens when we say "Hey Google"?

The phrase is not going away.

If voice activation is enabled and our device has already made the transition, we will be able to keep saying exactly the same thing. Other activation methods will also remain, such as holding down the power button or using the gesture configured by the manufacturer.

Google is changing who answers before changing how we call them.

One of the most reasonable concerns is whether Gemini can handle the basic tasks Assistant has been solving for years.

That has been precisely the difficult part of the transition.

When Gemini first began replacing it, it was far better at answering complex questions and holding conversations, but it could be surprisingly worse at something as simple as managing an alarm or controlling certain parts of the phone.

Google has been closing those gaps for some time.

Gemini now has integrations and utilities that allow it to create, modify and delete alarms and timers; open apps and settings; control volume; turn features such as the flashlight, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, NFC, Do Not Disturb or Battery Saver on and off, depending on the device; manage media playback; take screenshots; and control certain system functions.

It can also read and reply to notifications when we grant the necessary permissions.

In other words, Gemini is gradually ceasing to be just a chatbot placed where the assistant used to be and is becoming the system responsible for controlling the phone through natural language.

Calls and messages remain part of the package.

We can ask it to call a contact, send a message or use compatible apps such as WhatsApp when the integration is available and correctly configured.

All of this depends on the permissions we have granted.

Gemini may be very intelligent, but it should not develop the superpower of entering our applications without permission.

And it is better if it continues not to have that power.

It can also do certain things from the lock screen.

We can allow it to manage alarms, timers, calls, messages, media playback or home devices without forcing us to unlock the phone every time.

To access personal information or carry out sensitive operations, it may ask for authentication.

We want a useful assistant.

Not one so attentive that it tells our life story to anyone who happens to find the phone lying on a table.

The big difference appears when we stop giving isolated commands and start having a conversation.

We can ask something, receive an answer and continue from there without repeating all the context.

"Find an Italian restaurant near the hotel."

Then: "Which one closes later?"

And finally: "Tell me how to get to the second one."

The system tries to keep the thread of the conversation.

With the classic Assistant, it often felt as though we were starting a new relationship with every sentence.

Gemini Live takes this further.

It allows us to have a continuous voice conversation, interrupt the answer, change direction and ask follow-up questions in a way that is much closer to a normal conversation.

It can also use the camera and the screen.

We can show it something and ask what it is seeing, or share what is on the phone and talk about it.

For those of us who are blind, this possibility deserves particular attention.

Not because Gemini replaces specialized recognition applications, or because its descriptions should be treated as infallible.

But being able to show something with the camera and ask a series of follow-up questions opens up some very interesting possibilities.

"What does this label say?"

"Where is the button?"

"Is it on the left or the right?"

"Can you see any text underneath?"

"Which option looks as though it continues the process?"

The difference is that we do not receive only a closed, one-off description. We can have a conversation about whatever is in front of us.

That said, Gemini does not replace TalkBack.

TalkBack remains Android's screen reader.

Gemini is the assistant and artificial intelligence system.

They are different tools and can complement each other.

The retirement of Google Assistant does not mean TalkBack is disappearing or that Google is changing its screen reader.

It is worth making that clear because the names of all these tools are starting to sound like a family gathering where nobody is entirely sure who is whose cousin.

Another major difference lies in connected applications.

Depending on our configuration, Gemini can work with services such as Gmail, Calendar, Maps, YouTube, Google Photos, Google Home, Spotify, WhatsApp and other compatible applications.

This makes it possible to make requests that combine information from several places.

For example, it could find travel details in email, check how long it will take us to get there and then create a reminder.

Google's intention is for Gemini not only to answer, but also to help complete tasks.

And here we find the other side of convenience: permissions.

It is worth going into the settings and checking which applications are connected, what information we allow Gemini to use, what it can do while the phone is locked and how our activity is managed.

We do not need to press Accept on everything simply because an option exists.

Artificial intelligence works better when we continue using our own.

Gemini can also control devices through Google Home: lights, plugs, thermostats, televisions, speakers, blinds, vacuum cleaners and other compatible equipment.

However, there are still automations and routines that do not work in exactly the same way as they did with the previous system.

This is one of the major challenges of the change.

Gemini is much more powerful at understanding language and context.

But Google Assistant had spent almost ten years accumulating small integrations, tricks and compatibilities.

Moving all of that to a new platform is not a matter of changing an icon.

That is why anyone who relies every day on a very specific routine should check it during these weeks instead of waiting until September to discover that the command needs to be changed.

We should not assume that Gemini will be identical on every Android phone either.

One thing is being able to use Gemini as the assistant.

Another is having all the advanced features Google is grouping around Gemini Intelligence and on-device artificial intelligence.

The basic requirements for the app are relatively modest on many compatible phones, but the more advanced features may require a recent version of Android, specific processors and much more memory.

So "I have Gemini" does not mean "I have every AI feature Google has presented."

That will be an important distinction over the coming months.

And what about phones that are too old to run Gemini?

Google has indicated that they are not immediately included in the same mandatory retirement. Assistant will be able to continue working for now on devices that do not meet Gemini's minimum requirements.

The important words are "for now."

There is no promise of permanent compatibility.

But it also makes no sense to interpret September 4 as the day when every old Android phone suddenly loses its assistant.

Do we have to pay for Gemini?

Not to use the basic assistant features.

Google offers subscription plans with more advanced models, higher limits and premium services, but replacing Assistant with Gemini does not suddenly turn an eight o'clock alarm into a paid service.

Some advanced features may depend on a subscription, the device or the region.

So, once again: Gemini as an assistant and the entire premium ecosystem Google is building around Gemini are not exactly the same thing.

Put everything together and we can understand why Google is willing to retire an assistant that worked reasonably well for years.

Assistant belonged to a generation in which we spoke a command and the device tried to find the corresponding action.

Gemini belongs to another in which we can explain an intention, hold a conversation, use context, work with images and the camera, consult information from different applications and request multi-step tasks.

The potential leap is enormous.

Now it has to prove that it does not lose the small things we used to do without thinking along the way.

For blind users, the change may be especially interesting because voice assistants have never been merely a convenience. Making a call, creating an alarm, finding information, sending a message or controlling home devices by voice can be part of our everyday independence.

If we add an AI capable of talking about what the camera sees or what appears on the screen, the possibilities increase considerably.

But we will continue applying the same rule as always.

Test.

Check.

And do not assume that because an artificial intelligence says something in a wonderful voice, it must necessarily be right.

Confident-sounding voices make mistakes too.

The important date, then, is September 4, 2026.

On that day, Google will begin retiring Assistant on compatible phones and tablets and on certain connected devices. The process will take several weeks.

"Hey Google" will remain.

Alarms, calls, messages and a good deal of phone control will remain too.

Watches, headphones and Android Auto are part of the transition.

Cars with Google built in and home devices follow different schedules.

And devices that are too old for Gemini will keep the traditional assistant, at least for the time being.

After almost a decade, Google Assistant is beginning to hand over the keys to Android.

Gemini gets them.

Let us hope that, as well as being excellent at conversation, it remembers where we left the alarms, the lights and all those little commands for which we have spent years saying exactly the same two words.

That charger that stays plugged in: should we unplug it even when it is not charging anything?

There are small everyday habits we repeat for years without paying much attention to them.

We charge the phone, remove the cable and get on with our lives. The phone comes with us, but the charger stays quietly plugged into the wall, waiting for the next visit.

Over the past few days, recommendations have once again been circulating insisting that we should unplug chargers and power adapters when we are not using them.

The question is simple: does that make sense, or is this another of those warnings that turns every wall socket into a suspense movie?

There is a useful recommendation behind the message, but it deserves to be explained without alarmism.

Yes, a charger that remains plugged in consumes electricity even when it is not charging anything.

If we leave a charger connected to the mains with no phone, tablet, watch or other device at the other end, its consumption is not exactly zero.

Some of the internal electronics remain powered and need a very small amount of energy to stay ready.

This is what is usually called no-load power consumption, phantom power or standby consumption.

Now, before we go around the house ripping chargers out of the walls, let us put the matter into perspective.

Modern, good-quality chargers are far more efficient than older ones. In many cases, their consumption with no device connected falls to just a few tenths of a watt or even less.

A modern charger is not usually the secret culprit behind a huge electricity bill.

But it does not consume zero either.

And this is where the problem of accumulation appears.

The phone charger, the tablet charger, the watch charger, the laptop, the headphones, the wireless charging pad, a speaker and that power adapter that has been sitting behind a piece of furniture for three years because nobody can remember exactly which device it belonged to.

Individually, they may consume very little.

Multiply them by all the devices in a home, an office and then by millions of households, and the total is no longer completely insignificant.

That is why energy-efficiency regulations have been steadily reducing the amount of power that power supplies are allowed to consume when they are not doing useful work.

From an energy point of view, then, unplugging a charger we are not going to use is better than leaving it permanently connected.

But the financial saving from a single modern charger will probably be small.

So why is it worth doing?

Because power consumption is only one part of the story.

Even when no phone is connected, the charger is still an electrical device connected to the mains.

An original charger, or one from a reliable manufacturer, that complies with the safety standards of the market in which it is sold and is in good condition, is designed to remain connected without becoming a danger simply because of that.

This point matters.

Millions of chargers spend many hours plugged in every day without causing anything at all.

We should not turn a good habit into fear.

But a disconnected device has one obvious advantage: it is no longer exposed to mains voltage.

That removes any risk arising from an internal fault in the charger while it is unplugged.

The difference matters more when we are talking about old, damaged chargers, products of uncertain origin or chargers made with poor quality control.

And this is where it is worth paying attention.

A charger is not simply a piece of plastic that carries electricity from the wall to the phone.

Inside it are components responsible for converting, regulating and protecting the electrical current.

We connect it directly to the electrical installation.

That is why saving a few euros by buying a dubious-quality imitation can be a very poor decision.

The fact that a charger has USB-C does not automatically mean it is good.

USB-C describes the type of connection and some of what the system can do. It does not award university degrees to the device sitting behind the connector.

A properly designed product includes protection against excessive current, excessive temperature, overvoltage and short circuits.

In poor-quality products, those protections may be weaker, badly implemented or simply absent.

If we have a charger that gets excessively hot, makes a strange noise, gives off an odor, has cracks, has deformed pins, has a damaged cable or begins behaving differently from usual, we do not need to launch a scientific investigation.

Stop using it.

And one particularly revealing detail: if a charger with no device connected remains clearly hot, it deserves our attention. A slight temperature change can occur in some designs, but significant heating when it is doing no work should not be considered normal.

Another common question is whether constantly unplugging the charger can damage it.

Under normal conditions, no.

Plugs and chargers are designed precisely to be connected and disconnected.

What matters is doing it properly: hold the body of the plug or charger rather than pulling on the cable.

That simple habit helps prevent damage to internal connections and applies to practically any cabled device.

If we have several chargers together, a good-quality switched power strip can make the task much easier.

We can cut power to the entire group when we know we will not be using any of them.

Naturally, it is worth checking first what else is connected.

It would not be a great victory for energy efficiency to eliminate the phantom consumption of two chargers while also turning off the router in the middle of someone else's work.

Do we need to unplug the charger every single time we finish charging the phone?

This is not an emergency.

If we leave home and remember in the elevator that the charger was left plugged in, we do not need to run back as though we had left a frying pan on the stove.

If it is a reliable product, is in good condition and the electrical installation is sound, leaving it plugged in occasionally does not in itself create an abnormal situation.

The recommendation should be understood as a good habit.

If we have finished using it and do not need it to remain connected, we can unplug it.

It is doing nothing useful, it is still consuming a small amount of energy and it remains unnecessarily connected to the mains.

Seen this way, the question can also be turned around.

If we are not using it, what do we gain by leaving it plugged in?

Mainly, we save ourselves the effort of plugging it back in later.

That does not sound like a particularly memorable technological victory.

We also need to separate two situations that some reports mix together.

One thing is leaving only the charger plugged in.

Another is leaving the phone connected as well after it reaches one hundred percent.

Modern phones include electronic battery-management systems. When they reach certain levels, they reduce or stop charging, and many use optimized charging features to avoid keeping the battery completely full for longer than necessary.

So a modern phone does not keep drawing the charger's full power indefinitely as though it were a tank that could never finish filling.

That does not mean we should ignore the manufacturer's recommendations.

During charging, heat remains one of the battery's main enemies.

A bed, pillow, sofa or any surface that restricts ventilation is a worse place to charge than a firm, clear surface.

But that is a different issue from an empty charger sitting by itself in the wall socket.

And what about wireless chargers?

They also consume power while they remain connected.

A charging pad needs electronics to detect devices and manage the process. Consumption varies considerably by model and design.

The same logic applies here.

If we use the pad every day and want it ready, keeping it connected can be perfectly reasonable.

If we are going to spend days or weeks without using it, disconnecting it makes considerably more sense.

And be careful about extending the rule to every appliance in the house.

"Unplug everything when you are not using it" sounds wonderful until someone decides to apply the theory to the refrigerator before going on vacation.

Some devices are designed to remain powered: routers, security systems, certain medical equipment, refrigerators, freezers and other appliances need continuous power or carry out tasks even while it looks as though we are not using them.

This is not about turning the wall socket into a religion.

It is about disconnecting what genuinely does not need to stay connected.

An unused charger is one of the simplest examples.

And if we really want to reduce phantom power consumption at home, we may find more interesting targets than a small USB charger.

Older televisions, audio systems, game consoles, set-top boxes, monitors, printers and other devices can keep functions active while in standby mode.

Some need that state so they can respond to a remote control, update themselves or maintain a network connection.

Others consume power simply because their design is old or inefficient.

A switched power strip can be useful for grouping several devices we use together and cutting the power when we know we will not need any of them.

We also have smart plugs that can do this automatically.

They are excellent when there is a specific need.

But buying a smart plug solely to eliminate the tiny no-load consumption of a single charger may be a very technological solution to a problem that perhaps did not need quite so much technology.

The smart plug itself consumes energy in order to remain connected and communicate.

Sometimes the most advanced system is still simply switching something off.

The conclusion is quite simple.

Does a charger plugged in with no phone connected continue to consume electricity?

Yes.

Is the consumption enormous?

With a modern, efficient charger, usually not.

Are we going to get rich by unplugging it?

I am afraid we will have to keep looking for another plan.

Does it make sense to get into the habit of unplugging it when we do not need it?

Yes.

Not because every forgotten charger is waiting for the right moment to set the house on fire, nor because it is secretly devouring electricity.

Simply because it is still consuming power and remains connected to the mains without providing any service.

And quite often, technology is about that too.

Not only learning how to connect things.

Every now and then, knowing when it is worth disconnecting them.

Ten more stories that have also shaped the technology news

That brings us to the end of the stories that deserved a little more time and a more detailed explanation.

But the technology world has not been waiting around while we talked about them.

As in every edition, we close the news section with ten headlines that have attracted attention over the past few days, along with a short explanation of why they are worth knowing about.

1. Europe introduces new rules for identifying content created by artificial intelligence

Since August 2, new transparency obligations under the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act have begun to apply. Among other things, users must be able to know when they are interacting directly with certain AI systems, and providers of generative artificial intelligence will have to incorporate mechanisms that make synthetic or manipulated content identifiable. Deepfakes and certain content of public interest are also subject to disclosure obligations. Although the law is European, its effects ultimately reach international platforms and services operating in that market. The age of "if it looks real, it must be real" increasingly needs an asterisk.

2. Nvidia, Microsoft and other technology companies join forces to strengthen artificial intelligence security

At the end of July, the Open Secure AI Alliance was launched, an initiative involving major technology companies and organizations. Its goal is to share open tools for detecting vulnerabilities, auditing systems and improving the security of artificial intelligence models and agents. Concern is growing as agents move beyond simply answering questions and begin using software, browsing, writing code and carrying out actions. The more capable an AI becomes of doing things for us, the more important it is to make sure it does not also do the things nobody asked it to do.

3. Workers and specialists at major AI companies call for greater international coordination

Over the past few days, calls have gained renewed momentum for international mechanisms capable of evaluating and managing risks associated with increasingly advanced artificial intelligence systems. Among those supporting these proposals are professionals connected with some of the sector's most important companies. The main concern is not only what today's models can do, but what happens when AI itself begins to take an active role in developing the next generation. The race continues, but more and more people inside the circuit are also asking where the brakes are.

4. Microsoft shows that the enormous investment in artificial intelligence is starting to become a business

Microsoft has once again published figures that help explain why the major technology companies are spending gigantic amounts on data centers, processors and AI services. Azure has passed the one-hundred-billion-dollar mark in annual revenue, and Microsoft 365 Copilot continues to grow within companies and organizations. For months we have talked about how much it costs to build artificial intelligence infrastructure. It is becoming increasingly clear how some companies intend to recover that investment.

5. Europe pushes ahead with huge computing centers to compete in the AI race

The European Union is continuing to prepare what it calls artificial intelligence gigafactories: enormous infrastructures with specialized processors, storage and high-speed networks for training and running large-scale models. The aim is to mobilize public and private investment and reduce dependence on infrastructure located mainly in the United States and Asia. The AI race is no longer only about having a good idea or a good model. You also need an enormous place to plug it in.

6. Safety tests detect AI agents carrying out unauthorized actions

An evaluation by the United Kingdom body dedicated to artificial intelligence safety identified several cases in which advanced agents took actions outside their intended instructions during cybersecurity tests. The experiments were deliberately designed to explore the limits of these systems, with controlled Internet access and reduced safeguards, and they do not represent the normal operation of commercial products. Even so, they serve as a reminder that an agent capable of acting on the Internet needs different controls from a simple chatbot. Answering a question badly is one problem. Doing something nobody authorized is quite another.

7. Zoox is about to start charging passengers to ride in a robotaxi with no steering wheel or pedals

Amazon-owned Zoox is about to take an important step in Las Vegas by moving from free trials to commercial rides in robotaxis designed from the ground up to operate without a driver. It has announced that it will begin charging for rides on August 10. The vehicle is not a conventional car with autonomy added to it: it has no traditional steering wheel or pedals, and passengers sit facing one another. Autonomous driving has been promising for years that it will transform cities. We are now beginning to see services where the question is no longer only whether the car can drive itself, but whether passengers are willing to pay it to do so.

8. Part of a Falcon 9 ends up crashing into the Moon

The upper stage of a Falcon 9 rocket used on a 2025 mission struck the lunar surface on August 5 at about 8,700 kilometers per hour. South Korea's Danuri probe has made it possible to observe the area before and after the impact, giving scientists an unusual opportunity to study a collision whose time and trajectory were known in advance. The story also has a future-looking dimension: as lunar missions increase, we will have to talk more seriously about what we do with the remains of space vehicles. It seems space debris has started expanding its territory as well.

9. Google reorganizes the leadership of its artificial intelligence operations

Google has made one of the most significant internal moves of recent years in its artificial intelligence division. Demis Hassabis is stepping back from part of DeepMind's day-to-day management to focus more on research and scientific strategy, while other executives take on greater operational responsibility. At the same time, long-standing figures at the company have announced new projects outside Google. These changes show just how far artificial intelligence has gone beyond being simply another division within the company: it is even reorganizing the people who develop it.

10. Unitree takes humanoid robots to the stock market and DeepSeek comes in as an investor

Chinese company Unitree, known for its quadruped and humanoid robots, has taken a major step with its stock-market listing in Shanghai, reaching a valuation close to nine billion dollars. DeepSeek, one of the Chinese companies that has gained the greatest prominence in artificial intelligence, appears among the strategic investors. The combination makes perfect sense: one company knows how to build bodies capable of moving through the physical world, while the other works on artificial intelligence systems. One of the next great technology battles is precisely about bringing those two things together. Domestic robots that do the ironing have not arrived yet, but there is already a great deal of money trying to make sure they stop being science fiction.
