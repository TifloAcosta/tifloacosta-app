Main story one. Free companion flights: what Europe has approved and how other countries compare

Imagine we have booked a flight and the airline tells us that, for safety reasons, we cannot board without another person. An obvious question follows: if the airline is imposing that requirement, why should we pay for a second ticket?

On October 20, 2026, the European Parliament backed a reform of air-passenger rights. Among the proposals is free travel and an adjacent seat for a companion when an airline requires one for a passenger with a disability or reduced mobility on safety grounds and cannot itself provide the necessary assistance. This is not a free ticket for any relative of any disabled traveler.

That distinction matters. A blind person may need directions to the boarding gate yet be perfectly able to fly alone. Needing airport assistance is not the same as needing a companion. Treating the two as interchangeable would confuse accessibility with someone else deciding what we can do.

There is still a legislative process to complete, including formal approval by the Council and official publication. The announced timetable points to October 23, 2027, but that is a projected date, not a guarantee. Until the new rule applies, passengers cannot claim this free ticket as an existing right.

Your passport is not the deciding factor

This matters wherever you live. European passenger rights depend on the territory and circumstances of the flight rather than the traveler’s nationality. A passenger from Argentina, Australia or Japan may be covered on certain European journeys. But the rules are not worldwide: the route and applicable legislation must be checked.

How the rules differ around the world

In the United States, airlines generally cannot require a safety assistant except in certain regulated circumstances. If the airline insists despite the passenger disagreeing, it may be prohibited from charging for that second person’s transportation. That does not make all personal assistance free.

Canada applies a “one person, one fare” principle on certain domestic flights. It can cover an additional seat required because of a disability, but it is a different system and should not be assumed to apply to international flights.

In Brazil, regulations provide situations in which an airline must supply the necessary assistance or accept a companion chosen by the passenger, with a fare of no more than 20% of the relevant reference price, subject to the applicable conditions. That can be a substantial discount, but it is not always free travel.

Australia has discounts and airline-specific policies, but no general rule making all companion tickets free. Argentina provides free travel on certain forms of ground transport for disabled people and, in some cases, their companions; that does not mean commercial flights operate under the same rules.

There is no single international companion-ticket policy. Before booking, ask who is requiring the companion, what assistance the carrier can provide and which rules apply to the particular journey.

Questions to ask before booking

If an airline requires a companion, ask for its safety justification and the financial conditions in writing. If airport assistance is needed, request it in advance; in Europe, at least 48 hours’ notice is recommended. Keep the replies: a written record will help far more with a complaint than a vague recollection of a telephone conversation.

The proposed reform is intended to prevent an airline-imposed requirement from becoming an extra cost for the passenger. Until it takes effect, the existing rules still apply.

Main story two. Can a chip restore sight? Promises, clinical trials and who might benefit

On October 30, Elon Musk announced that Blindsight, Neuralink’s artificial-vision project, was ready from an engineering perspective and awaiting regulatory permission. That is interesting news. But a device being technically ready is not the same as restoring useful vision to blind people.

Neuralink aims to bypass the eyes and optic nerve and stimulate the brain’s visual areas directly. A camera would capture the surroundings, and an implant would turn that information into signals for the brain to interpret. If effective, this could offer a route for people who cannot benefit from retinal technologies.

Blindsight received the US FDA’s Breakthrough Device designation in 2024. This supports interaction with regulators during development; it is neither commercial approval nor proof of effectiveness. As of October 31, 2026, published human clinical findings do not establish that Blindsight restores sight.

Seeing a light is not the same as reading a book

Experimental visual prostheses can generate flashes of light known as phosphenes. In some cases these may help users detect shapes, movement or position and assist orientation. That is a long way from recognizing faces, identifying colors or reading a newspaper. A few landmarks can be valuable without amounting to a complete picture.

Blindness from birth presents additional questions. The brain develops its ability to process visual input over time. If it has never received that input, it might have to learn to interpret entirely unfamiliar artificial signals. Activating electrodes alone is not enough. The cause of blindness and the condition of the brain’s visual pathways matter greatly.

PRIMA: results measured in people

PRIMA is a retinal implant developed through several stages by Pixium Vision and Science Corporation. It combines a device placed beneath the retina with special glasses that capture and project visual information. Unlike Blindsight, it relies on certain retinal cells and visual pathways continuing to function.

In a trial published in The New England Journal of Medicine, 26 of 32 participants assessed after one year achieved a clinically meaningful improvement in vision. Some could recognize letters and read. These are concrete results, but they concern people with a specific condition: advanced geographic atrophy associated with age-related macular degeneration, not every form of blindness. Adverse events were also reported and require follow-up.

PRIMA does not recreate natural vision. Users need training, and there are limitations in detail and color. The important difference is that outcomes have actually been measured in trial participants.

Genes and light-sensitive cells

Luxturna is an approved gene therapy for certain patients with changes in both copies of the RPE65 gene, provided they retain sufficient viable retinal cells. It does not treat arbitrary optic-nerve damage or every inherited eye condition.

Optogenetics aims to make surviving retinal cells sensitive to light through light-responsive proteins. Several studies have reported partial improvements in selected participants, but the approach remains experimental and is being explored for particular conditions. It is not a universal treatment.

Who might benefit from which approach?

Someone who retains certain retinal structures but has lost central vision to a specific disease may be considered for a retinal prosthesis. Where blindness results from a particular genetic mutation, molecular diagnosis may point toward therapies or clinical trials. If the eyes or optic nerve are absent, technologies that depend on them cannot help; a future cortical implant might be relevant, but only after safety and efficacy have been demonstrated. People blind from birth do not all share the same underlying condition.

Access is another major obstacle. Approval in one country does not make a treatment immediately available in every hospital. Cost, specialist teams, funding and long-term follow-up all matter. And a person may decide against invasive surgery without that decision detracting in any way from their independence.

These are different lines of research addressing different causes of sight loss. Some have published patient results; others still need to demonstrate that they are safe and effective.

Main story three. Screen readers in 2026: progress varies across the world

Those of us who have used screen readers for years have seen computers, phones and even the experience of fighting with online forms change dramatically. An international survey now puts some numbers to those developments.

WebAIM received 1,780 valid responses during July and August 2026, as part of a survey series that began in 2009. Participation was voluntary, so the results are not statistically representative of every screen-reader user worldwide. More than half of the respondents who supplied regional information were from North America. These are figures about respondents, not global market shares.

JAWS and NVDA: geography changes the picture

Of respondents, 55% chose JAWS as their primary desktop screen reader, 32.9% chose NVDA and 6.5% chose VoiceOver on Mac. Two years earlier the figures were 40.5% for JAWS and 37.7% for NVDA. Changes in the sample make a simple winner-versus-loser interpretation misleading.

In North America, JAWS reached 72.5% and NVDA 15.6%. In Europe they were almost level, at 42.8% and 42.3% respectively. NVDA reached 77.4% in Asia, 83% in Africa and the Middle East, and 67.2% in South America. The samples for those three regions were considerably smaller, so the figures need caution.

Price matters, but so do training, workplace agreements, the software we use and support in our language. NVDA has expanded access as a free, open-source reader. JAWS offers tools and an established professional ecosystem that some users depend on. There is no need to turn every choice into a competition.

We do not have to use just one reader

Some 70.3% regularly use more than one desktop screen reader. Not simultaneously, of course: that would make a noisy office even noisier. Many people use JAWS at work, NVDA to check a website, Narrator to set up Windows and VoiceOver when working on a Mac.

Only 0.4% selected Narrator as their primary reader, while 36.1% used it regularly. This is why primary choice and use for particular tasks are worth measuring separately.

The phone has become an essential tool

Some 91.8% of respondents use screen readers on mobile devices. Among those naming a main platform, Apple led, but not by the same margin everywhere. iPhone use was particularly strong in North America and Europe, while Android had greater relative importance elsewhere.

With VoiceOver and TalkBack, phones can handle tasks once reserved for computers: reading documents, identifying products, managing messages, switching between apps and describing images. Android offers a wider range of prices and devices, although differences between brands and operating-system versions can complicate the experience. A screen reader alone cannot make every app accessible.

Artificial intelligence helps, but websites still have work to do

Some 60.1% use AI to describe images. Others use it to analyze documents or complete online tasks. These capabilities are useful, but users must also learn to recognize errors. A confident-sounding description is not necessarily correct.

Some 83.9% believe improving websites would make a bigger difference than further improvements to assistive technology, while 85.6% consider it likely that they will encounter significant problems reading PDFs. An unlabeled checkout button can still block a purchase even with the latest screen reader.

Training, connectivity, synthetic voices and braille displays are not equally available in every country. Affordability, languages and public services must be part of any meaningful discussion of progress.

Choosing a screen reader depends on each person’s needs and circumstances. However sophisticated the tools become, developers remain responsible for making their websites, documents and services accessible.

Ten more stories worth knowing about

WhatsApp makes group conversations easier to navigate

Features announced in late October aim to help people locate important interactions and regain context in group conversations. Availability depends on the rollout and app version. For anyone taking part in several communities, finding a reply without reading hundreds of messages could be genuinely useful, provided the controls are accessible.

Siri AI gains languages, but not everywhere

Apple has expanded Siri AI language support, including Spanish. Supported language does not necessarily mean supported territory: regional restrictions remain on some mobile platforms in the European Union. Before changing region settings, check which functions your device and software version actually support.

Scribe for Documents comes to iPhone

Pneuma Solutions has announced live camera-based reading and document conversion into accessible formats. Live scanning is presented as free and processed on the device, while advanced conversion uses paid credits or plans. Being able to point a camera at a sign for free does not necessarily mean converting an entire book is free.

A new iPad mini updates the small-tablet range

Apple has introduced a refreshed iPad mini with display and performance improvements. Beyond processing power, real-world battery life, VoiceOver, braille support and reading apps will matter to many of us. Availability and some intelligent features vary by country.

Firefox continues improving its PDF viewer

Mozilla has reported performance and navigation changes. A faster PDF viewer is useful, but the file itself still needs actual text, proper tagging and a logical reading order. A badly prepared document remains difficult to use.

Microsoft rolls out a Start menu accessibility fix

According to a report circulated by NFB Access On, Microsoft has fixed a Start menu accessibility regression through a server-side update, without requiring a new Windows build. The rollout is gradual, so some people may still encounter the issue for a time.

Accessibility overlays are no magic fix

A study of US state-government websites found problems with some overlay tools that promise to fix accessibility without changing the underlying site. Buttons, navigation and forms can still fail. The lesson applies in any country: displaying an accessibility icon is not the same as developing an accessible site.

Automatic captions do not tell the whole story

WebAIM points out that useful captions need to include relevant sounds, identify speakers and synchronize the text correctly. An apparently accurate transcript can omit an alert, a speaker change or an essential sound. Pressing “generate captions” is only the beginning.

TurnOver becomes free and open source on Mac

Oliver Kennett has released the source code of TurnOver, a project offering an alternative experience in some macOS applications. It can now be downloaded and examined, although its author emphasizes that it remains a personal project without guaranteed support. It is best approached as an experimental alternative, not as a replacement for VoiceOver.

Gemini Live adds Guided Vision after testing with blind users

Google and Aira have worked together on Guided Vision, which describes the environment and helps users position the camera. More than a thousand blind or low-vision testers took part in its evaluation. Involving the people who will use a feature matters, although AI descriptions still need checking and established mobility aids remain important.
