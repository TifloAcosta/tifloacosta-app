Introduction

Hello, hello, lovely people.

Does any of this sound familiar? You type one word on your iPhone and another one appears. You update your screen reader and, weeks later, discover from a tutorial that it can now do something you could have used from day one. Or you look for an accessible book, know it exists, and find that getting hold of it takes more work than reading it.

I haven't invented these examples to pad out the issue. They're the starting points for our three main stories: what the iPhone keyboard does with the words we type; why we're still so poor at explaining what's new in screen readers; and what the Marrakesh Treaty has achieved ten years after it entered into force.

Then we'll look at ten shorter stories from the wider world of technology: security, artificial intelligence, energy and the latest moves by the big companies. A headline doesn't have to mention accessibility for the story to affect us.

Ten years after the Marrakesh Treaty: there are far more accessible books, but can you actually get them?

Tony Acosta - TifloAcosta

November 8, 2026

Some years ago I wrote about the Marrakesh Treaty, an agreement intended to put an end to something that was difficult to justify: an accessible book produced in one country couldn't simply be used in another. The work had to be repeated, as though the book lost its letters when it crossed a border.

Ten years after the treaty entered into force, it's time to see what has changed. I'm not going to give you a lesson in international copyright law. What interests me is whether you can find the book you're after today, whether your library can obtain it from another country, and what is still getting in the way.

I'll start with an impressive number: 1.2 million titles. According to the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), the Accessible Books Consortium (ABC) catalogue has passed that figure, covering around 80 languages and formats such as braille, audio, DAISY and accessible EPUB. The treaty has 106 contracting parties covering 132 WIPO member states, while 156 organizations participate in the international service.

Here's what that means in practice. If a library in Argentina needs a book that has already been made accessible in Australia, it can request a copy through the international exchange instead of producing another version. One of the system's first exchanges, in 2016, sent an audiobook from Canada to Vision Australia, saving the Australian organization an estimated US$2,000 in production costs. Repeat that many times and the savings can pay for more accessible books.

Does that mean you can open the catalogue and download whatever you fancy? No. A collection that size is not a public library with a download button for everyone. Access depends on participating organizations and their eligibility rules. ABC operates mainly through organizations serving people who cannot easily read print: they request books from other institutions and provide them to eligible readers.

There is, however, an interesting development: some participating organizations provide direct access to international titles. In those cases you can search for and download books through ABC's reader service or compatible apps such as Dolphin EasyReader. Your library must participate and enable that kind of access. Installing an app alone won't make the entire collection appear. So before filling in forms all over the internet, ask your library whether it takes part in the exchange and offers direct access.

The options differ quite a bit from country to country. Here's a quick tour.

If you live in the United States

One of the world's major accessible reading services is the National Library Service for the Blind and Print Disabled (NLS), part of the Library of Congress. Through BARD, eligible readers can download audiobooks and digital braille books and magazines free of charge. NLS also participates in the Marrakesh exchange. If you're looking for a book produced abroad, ask your NLS network library whether it has the title or can obtain it through that route.

The service also supports eligible US citizens living abroad. But that doesn't mean anyone living in Mexico, Argentina or Australia can sign up for BARD: ordinary eligibility covers people living in the United States and US citizens overseas, with certain exceptions.

There's also Bookshare, an international digital library of accessible books and reading tools. Eligible students in the United States can use it free of charge; other individual users have an annual subscription. People in many countries can join, although prices and access to particular titles vary depending on where they live.

If you live in Argentina

Tiflolibros, run by the nonprofit organization Tiflonexos, stands out. According to Argentina's National Congress Library, its collection contains more than 70,000 accessible digital books. Registration is free for people with visual impairments and others who cannot read conventional print. The National Congress Library also has a Sala Marrakech, developed with Tiflonexos, to help people access these resources.

Tiflolibros isn't limited to Argentina, either. In 2025 it announced a collection of human-narrated audiobooks available for download in eleven countries, including Argentina, Mexico, the United States, Spain and Uruguay, subject to eligibility requirements. That's cooperation between libraries producing a result you can actually use.

If you live in Mexico

Mexico has been part of the treaty for years and has continued to develop the rules governing exchange. In August 2025 it published new cooperation rules for participating organizations, allowing those that meet the requirements to obtain and distribute works from other countries. But the existence of the treaty doesn't mean every Mexican library already offers international downloads. Services vary.

Meanwhile, there are other options. Mexico is among the countries from which eligible readers can download certain international audiobook collections through Tiflolibros. Bookshare also accepts members outside the United States, subject to its own conditions.

If you live in Australia

Vision Australia has a free accessible library for eligible permanent residents who have difficulty reading print. Its collection includes more than 70,000 audio and braille titles, along with magazines and other materials. It also participates in ABC, so eligible users can benefit from books held by other libraries when the titles are available and access conditions are met.

That illustrates the point nicely: the aim isn't to build a single worldwide library, but to help the libraries we already have share their collections.

If you live somewhere else

Look for a library or organization serving people who are blind, have low vision or cannot read print, and ask whether it participates in the Marrakesh exchange and can request books produced abroad. ABC publishes a country-by-country list of participating organizations. It's also worth checking Bookshare and Tiflolibros, because not every route to accessible books requires a physical library nearby. Each service may, of course, ask for evidence that you qualify.

What has actually reached readers

The size of the catalogue matters, but so do actual deliveries. WIPO estimates that some 1.3 million accessible books have been delivered to eligible readers through participating organizations since the international exchange began. In 2025 alone, approximately 240,500 digital files were delivered. Ten years ago, obtaining material across borders was considerably harder.

Two qualifications, before we get carried away. More than a million catalogue entries doesn't mean a million distinct books are available to every reader: language, version, format and access rules all vary. Nor does the number of delivered files tell us how many different people received them.

What still needs fixing

First, far too many books still aren't available in accessible formats.

Second, countries are progressing at different speeds. Some have well-organized libraries, specialist staff and digital distribution. Elsewhere, the first hurdle is finding an organization able to supply the material at all.

Third, language. A work may be available in English or French but not in Spanish, Arabic or another language.

And fourth, format. A digitized book isn't necessarily usable with a screen reader. It may be a PDF made up entirely of images, a document with no useful structure, or a file in which moving between chapters, sections and pages is impossible. I've opened supposedly digital documents and found something close to a brick wall. Digital does not automatically mean accessible.

What might happen in the next year or two

Over the next year or two, realistic progress would mean easier direct access to collections, more libraries joining the exchange and better ways to find titles. Ideally, you'd search from your usual reading app, check which format is available and obtain the book without a long chain of emails and manual requests. Some ABC services and apps such as Dolphin EasyReader are starting to make that possible, but the option is far from universal.

Artificial intelligence is also being explored to help convert documents into accessible books. WIPO and the DAISY Consortium have been developing tools intended to assist organizations that produce accessible editions. Don't expect perfect conversions, particularly with tables, mathematical formulas or complex images, but such tools could save libraries a lot of work.

What could change over the next few years

The really important shift would be for publishers to release accessible books from day one, instead of relying on later adaptations: digital books that work properly with screen readers, refreshable braille displays and other assistive technologies. A well-made ebook lets you move between chapters, consult its table of contents, read with synthetic speech or braille, and save bookmarks and your reading position. The technology exists; widespread adoption is the challenge.

If that happens, specialist libraries will be better able to focus on older works, books needing particular adaptations and material that still isn't published accessibly. Catalogues will need improving, too. Finding a title described as accessible is of little use if its table of contents doesn't work or its tables make no sense.

And it isn't all about files. Millions of books are no help if the people who need them don't know where to look, how to register or which reading tool to use. Libraries and accessibility organizations have a job to do explaining those options properly.

What you can do today

If you already use a library for blind or print-disabled readers, ask about the Marrakesh Treaty. Can it obtain books from libraries in other countries? Does it offer direct access to the ABC catalogue?

If you don't use one yet, consult ABC's list of participating organizations and find those operating in your country. Take a look at Bookshare and Tiflolibros as well: check their joining conditions and whether their collections meet your needs.

And if you can't find a particular title, don't give up without asking. It may already have been made accessible somewhere else.

The shortage of accessible books is far from over, but the progress is real. There is now a network through which libraries can share works internationally, although some borders remain surprisingly difficult to cross when we're talking about digital files that could travel around the world in seconds.

For me, the real success of the Marrakesh Treaty won't come when the catalogue reaches two or three million titles, impressive though that would be. It will come when you can find the book you want, in your language, choose a format you can use, and start reading without having to worry about which country produced the accessible version.

When you pick up a detective novel, you've already got enough to worry about finding out who the murderer is. You shouldn't have to solve the mystery of why the book can't cross a border first.

When your iPhone writes its own version: VoiceOver, dictation and corrections you didn't ask for

Tony Acosta - TifloAcosta

You write a message, read it back and find a word you don't remember typing. Perhaps your iPhone decided to help. The trouble is that it doesn't always get things right, and sometimes you don't even notice that it has intervened.

A Reuters investigation reports that the iOS keyboard uses internal lists of expressions it avoids offering as suggestions. It's important to understand what that means: those words aren't banned, and you can type them manually. The restriction concerns certain predictions and text-entry methods.

That raises a practical question for those of us who write with VoiceOver: which settings affect the words we enter, and how can we check that the final text says exactly what we meant?

Auto-Correction, predictions and dictation aren't the same thing

Auto-Correction tries to fix spelling mistakes. Sometimes it succeeds; at other times it turns a name or unusual term into a completely different word.

Predictive text suggests words and phrases you might type next. You can ignore them. The restrictions described by Reuters concern what appears as a suggestion, not a system that removes words you have deliberately written from your messages.

Dictation turns speech into text. Asking Siri to send a message is a separate process. If speech recognition gets a word wrong, that doesn't necessarily mean Auto-Correction had anything to do with it. It's worth distinguishing the two before randomly switching off settings and ending up with two problems instead of one.

Settings worth checking

On the iPhone, open Settings, General, Keyboard. Several options deserve your attention.

Auto-Correction. If it regularly changes words you typed correctly, try turning it off and see whether things improve. This isn't a universal prescription: some people will prefer to keep it on because it prevents more mistakes than it causes.

Predictive Text. If the suggestions distract you or get in the way of your typing, you can turn them off. This is separate from Auto-Correction.

Text Replacement. You might have shortcuts here that turn a few letters into a word or entire sentence. If the same strange replacement keeps appearing, check this list before deciding the phone has developed an imagination of its own. There's also a useful trick for words the keyboard insists on correcting: put the correct word in the Phrase field and leave the Shortcut field empty. Apple documents this way of preventing particular unwanted corrections.

Auto-Punctuation. If you use dictation and the iPhone inserts punctuation you didn't expect, try turning it off and speaking the punctuation yourself. The exact location and wording of this setting may depend on your iOS version and language.

VoiceOver has a part to play, too

Under Settings, Accessibility, VoiceOver, look for the typing-feedback options. You can have characters, words, both or neither spoken as you type. Hearing each completed word often helps you catch mistakes early, but it doesn't guarantee you'll notice a replacement made afterwards.

That's why I like to review the entire message before sending it, especially when it contains names, numbers, addresses or details that don't leave room for creative interpretation. You can use the rotor to move through the text and, where the editing controls allow, inspect individual words and characters.

I take much the same precaution with dictation. A pause, an unusual name or a long sentence can produce a transcript quite different from what you intended, so listen to the result when you finish. If you ask Siri to compose or send a message, check what it understood before approving it, whenever that option is offered.

The point isn't to turn off every helpful feature. It's to understand what each one does and keep the ones that suit you. If my iPhone is going to write on my behalf, I'd rather it didn't change its mind without telling me.

Screen readers keep moving forward. Are their users keeping up?

Tony Acosta - TifloAcosta

Screen readers have changed enormously. JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver and TalkBack let you customize voices, navigation, how much information is spoken, gestures and behaviour in particular situations. The problem isn't that they offer too many features. It's that we can use them for years without discovering some that would genuinely help us.

You probably know the routine. You update your screen reader, check that email, the browser and your usual apps still work, and carry on as before. Weeks later, someone demonstrates a procedure in a tutorial that could save you time every day. You try it, it works, and you wonder: how long has it been able to do that?

Sometimes the answer is: two versions ago. And there you were, faithfully following twenty steps for something you can now do in five.

The new features exist, but are they explained well?

The people behind these products publish documentation and lists of changes, some of them very thorough. I'm not dismissing that effort. I'm asking who those explanations are really written for.

Someone who knows their screen reader inside out will immediately understand the significance of a new setting, command or internal change. A beginner or intermediate user can read precisely the same explanation and still not know what it would improve in everyday life. The information is technically correct, but it hasn't told that person anything useful.

Perhaps the explanation should start with the problem a new feature solves: “You can now do this more easily,” followed by an example and the steps. Leave the technical detail for those who want it.

Customization can make life harder, too

Being able to tailor a screen reader to our needs is an important achievement. I'm not suggesting cutting features or treating users as though they couldn't learn to use them.

What I question is why changing something simple can require knowing several menus, obscure setting names and exceptions that apply only in certain apps. Worse still, a setting can alter the screen reader's behaviour, and then finding the way back to the old behaviour becomes another project.

Advanced users may enjoy exploring. Others simply want to read, write, work or study. They shouldn't have to become screen-reader technicians just to get something out of an update.

Learning about features by accident

Much of what we learn comes from friends, mailing lists, videos and independent tutorials. All of that is tremendously valuable, and those of us who explain assistive technology will keep doing so. But it makes little sense for the main practical explanation of a new feature to reach people by chance. If a company develops a feature and its users discover it accidentally, the communication job isn't finished.

How could this improve? For example, group the changes by level: improvements to everyday tasks, customization options and specialist features. Short explanations, accessible demonstrations and a place that's easy to consult months later would go a long way. And when a familiar gesture or procedure changes, make that particularly clear.

I'm not proposing that every update should force us through a course. Remembering all those keyboard shortcuts is enough without adding an entrance exam.

After all these years using screen readers, I still come across features I've missed, and I'm not embarrassed to say so. That makes me wonder what happens to people who have less experience or less time to explore.

A new version shouldn't be judged solely by what it adds, but also by what its users actually learn about and use. If an improvement remains hidden and nobody knows it exists, there's still work to do.

Ten Technology Stories Worth A Look

1. Apple opens Siri AI to developers, with limitations still in place.

On November 3, Apple announced that developers could start testing integrations between their apps and Siri AI in the 27.3 betas. The idea is for the assistant to carry out actions inside apps, rather than simply answer questions. This is a developer preview, not a feature now available to all users, and availability differs between platforms and regions. In the European Union, Siri AI is currently unavailable on iOS, iPadOS and watchOS, although Apple offers it on compatible Macs and Vision Pro devices under the relevant conditions. That distinction matters whether you're an EU resident or simply using a device there.

2. Artificial intelligence helps uncover 81 serious Firefox vulnerabilities.

On November 5, OpenAI introduced GPT-6 Cyber, a model intended for authorized cybersecurity work. According to the company, its researchers used it to identify 81 previously unknown high-severity vulnerabilities in Firefox and to develop proof-of-exploit work involving Linux. Mozilla has already fixed many of the reported Firefox issues. AI can speed up vulnerability research, which makes safeguards essential. For the rest of us, there's a less glamorous but useful task: keep the browser updated.

3. OpenAI introduces new ways to create and edit images.

On November 4, OpenAI announced GPT Image 2.6 and new image-generation features. They include gradually rolling out the ability to make animated GIFs, along with improvements intended to preserve details that are already right when editing a picture. This could be useful to people creating content and to anyone who just wants to retouch a photo without ruining the rest of it.

4. JAWS 2027 is available to download.

The new version of JAWS is available from Freedom Scientific's official website. One practical detail: installing a new major version doesn't require removing the previous one. You can try the new features while keeping the version you know until you've checked that everything works as expected. Download from the official site, and check that your preferred language is available.

5. Artists' voices and songs are at the centre of another AI copyright dispute.

Rights holders connected to Elvis Presley and publishers of music associated with Eminem have brought legal action against Suno, the AI music-generation platform. They allege unauthorized imitation or use of protected creative material and artistic identities. Suno says its service is intended to create original music and that it has measures against misuse. The cases raise questions about copyright and the use of performers' voices and identities, and the courts will have to decide the particular claims.

6. Google extends AI video generation to fifteen seconds.

On November 5, Google introduced Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash, which can generate clips up to fifteen seconds long and allows further editing through written instructions, without starting the entire video again. The company also announced lower pricing than for the previous version. The results can still contain mistakes, but the range of editing options continues to grow.

7. AI data centres begin planning when to use less electricity.

On November 2, OpenAI announced Grid Protectors, a programme under which its future computing facilities could temporarily reduce demand when local electricity networks are under pressure. At a planned site in Georgia, in the United States, an agreement provides for up to 1,000 megawatts of flexible demand response. OpenAI says this is comparable to the average electricity demand of about 700,000 homes. These are plans for future facilities, not savings already achieved. AI needs electricity, and rather more than it takes to charge a phone.

8. Perplexity proposes AI agents that share out the work.

On November 6, Perplexity announced updates to its Computer service, including Teammates, a feature designed to divide work between AI agents. It also announced local computing on Mac and new voice options. The idea is to move beyond answering isolated questions and support longer processes. Delegating work doesn't remove the need to check permissions and results, of course.

9. Hockey fans can follow matches through touch using haptic tablets.

On November 5, the NHL and Ticketmaster announced plans to provide OneCourt devices at selected North American events. The tablets translate puck movement and other on-ice action into vibrations you can feel with your hands. They don't replace commentary, but they offer another way to follow a game. For now, availability is limited to particular venues and matches; it's not something you can simply use from your sofa at home.

10. Six gaming companies adopt shared accessibility labels.

On November 2, Electronic Arts, Google, Nintendo of America, Riot Games, Ubisoft and Xbox announced that they were adopting common labels to describe accessibility features in games. The scheme covers 24 features, including narrated menus, captions and remappable controls. It's a useful step towards knowing what a game offers before buying it, although a label is no guarantee that it will be fully accessible to everybody. For now, the information is provided in US English.
