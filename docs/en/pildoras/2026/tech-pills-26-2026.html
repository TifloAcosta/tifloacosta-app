INTRODUCTION

All I wanted was to find out what was new in technology. Nothing more. Instead, somewhere between glasses that promise to tell us what is in front of us and an assistant that claims it can sort out half our lives, I found myself reading about espionage, billionaires, resignations and governments arguing over computer components. At this rate, keeping up with a phone update will require a course in international economics.

Meanwhile, while people in boardrooms debate who will control the future, the rest of us still have fairly straightforward needs: a document that reads properly; an app that remains accessible after an update; a camera that helps us find something without inventing what it sees. These may not make the headlines, but they tell us far more about the value of technology than a presentation full of applause.

This issue begins with smart glasses. Google and Meta already have concrete products and services to discuss, while Apple appears in the leaks, as so often happens before it announces anything. Next comes the change in Apple's leadership and what it may, or may not, mean to VoiceOver users. Finally, we follow the money: why Nvidia matters so much, what Anthropic's stock market debut means, and what could happen if enthusiasm for AI proves more expensive to sustain than expected.

We finish with ten items from science and technology. They do not all have to affect our daily lives to deserve attention. Sometimes a discovery is worthwhile simply because it reveals how much we still have to learn.

You won't need to understand the vocabulary of the stock market or the inner workings of a laboratory to follow along. If a subject deserves space here, it also deserves a clear explanation. We have enough complicated settings menus in our lives already.

FEATURE ARTICLES

1. SMART GLASSES: A NEW OPPORTUNITY FOR BLIND PEOPLE?

There was a time when smart glasses seemed designed for people who wanted to put a camera on their face and display their enthusiasm for new technology. Those of us who are blind had little reason to get excited. If the main attraction was an image displayed before your eyes, what use would it be to us?

Artificial intelligence has changed the proposition. Today, we are talking about glasses that capture a scene, interpret it and answer spoken questions. We might ask them to describe an object, read a label or help find something. The difference is enormous: instead of merely wearing a camera, we can ask what it is looking at. We still have to make sure its answer bears some resemblance to reality.

Google and Samsung take visual assistance seriously

On November 12, Google opened preorders for its new glasses, developed with Samsung and eyewear companies Warby Parker and Gentle Monster. They use Gemini, Google's AI assistant, and are scheduled to arrive in selected markets on December 10, 2026. Prices begin at $395 for some Warby Parker models and $449 for Gentle Monster. The initial launch is limited to the United States and South Korea.

The particularly interesting feature is Guided Vision. Rather than accepting a general description, users can ask follow-up questions, request that text be read aloud or ask for help locating an object. The assistant can also explain how to reposition the camera when the object is not properly framed.

Google developed the feature with Aira, a human visual interpreting service, and blind and low-vision testers. According to the company, more than a thousand members of Aira's testing community took part. That is a welcome change from designing something first and only afterwards asking blind people whether they can use it. The glasses will also offer access to a human Aira interpreter when appropriate. AI can handle some everyday requests; a person is still valuable when careful confirmation matters.

There is a catch worth knowing before considering a purchase. Some iPhones will be compatible with the glasses, but Guided Vision and Aira will initially require a compatible Android phone. That leaves iPhone users without two of the most attractive features at launch. Nor has Google announced a release date for many countries, including Spain. We can follow the news without rushing to clear a space for the glasses at home.

Meta already has real-world experience

While Google prepares its launch, Meta's glasses are already being worn by many people. Ray-Ban Meta glasses can make calls, play audio, send messages, take pictures and answer questions through Meta AI. For us, the real attraction is visual assistance: being able to ask what is in front of us or obtain descriptions without holding a phone.

Meta has worked with Be My Eyes on visual assistance through certain glasses, although availability varies by model and country. It has also expanded descriptions and accessibility options, including audio adjustments. We do not all hear in the same way, and the ability to adapt sound can make the difference between something genuinely useful and something that remains in a drawer.

Not every advertised feature works everywhere. Before deciding whether a product is suitable, we must check what is actually available in our country, language and particular model.

Culture is another promising area. Meta has worked with institutions such as the Pinacoteca di Brera in Milan on new ways to experience art. Perhaps one day we will be able to walk through a museum and receive explanations tailored to the work in front of us, rather than rely entirely on a dedicated guided tour. As long as the assistant offers more insight than 'what a lovely painting'.

Apple keeps its cards close

Reports suggest that Apple is working on smart glasses too. Recent leaks point towards a possible announcement in 2027, with cameras, microphones, speakers and Siri working with the iPhone. For those familiar with VoiceOver and Apple's ecosystem, the idea is appealing.

But there is a distinction worth protecting: a leak is not an official announcement. Apple has not presented these glasses or confirmed their features, price or release date. Imagining what wearable iPhone accessibility might look like is one thing. Claiming a product can do something nobody has demonstrated is quite another. We can assess it when there is a real announcement.

What would we actually do with them?

Think of an ordinary kitchen. You need to identify an ingredient, check a use-by date or find something on the counter. With a phone, you have to hold it, aim it and hope the object is in view. Glasses could leave your hands free while you ask your questions and continue preparing a meal. The same applies to reading signs, identifying clothes or understanding how items are arranged on a table.

These are not cinematic achievements, and that is precisely why they matter. Independence is often built from small tasks we no longer have to ask someone else to perform.

But AI makes mistakes. Confusing a blue shirt with a green one may be annoying; misunderstanding an obstacle or a step may be dangerous. These glasses cannot replace a white cane, a guide dog or established orientation and mobility skills. Battery life, connectivity, subscriptions and privacy matter too. A wearable camera may record people nearby, and images sent to a company's servers raise questions that an exciting advert cannot answer.

The real test takes place at home

One issue remains fundamental: the accessibility of the device itself. Glasses that can describe images are of little use if we cannot configure them independently, operate their controls, check the battery or use all their features without sight. They must also work in the languages and places where people actually live.

Competition among Google, Samsung, Meta and perhaps Apple could bring major benefits. Involving blind people in product development is encouraging. But the verdict comes in daily use. Describing a well-lit demonstration kitchen is one thing. Finding the keys among papers, the television remote and everything else on the table is another.

If manufacturers understand that accessibility begins with the first design decision, rather than being bolted on at the end, these glasses could become far more than a passing fashion.

2. APPLE HAS A NEW CAPTAIN. WILL THE WAY WE USE ITS PRODUCTS CHANGE TOO?

On September 1, 2026, John Ternus became Apple's chief executive, succeeding Tim Cook after fifteen years. Cook has not retired to grow tomatoes. He remains executive chairman, with considerable influence. Even so, the change is real and comes at a sensitive time. Apple has to decide how to compete in AI, how new devices will fit into its plans and where to take an ecosystem used by hundreds of millions of people.

For VoiceOver users, these are not abstract boardroom matters. A small system change can save us countless steps or cost us an afternoon searching for a function that has moved for no apparent reason.

The Apple Tim Cook leaves behind

It would be unfair to reduce his tenure to ever more expensive phones. Under Cook, Apple strengthened the Apple Watch, AirPods, its own processors and a huge services business. He also placed considerable emphasis on privacy and kept accessibility among the brand's recognisable priorities. For many blind people, the iPhone became an essential tool for communication, reading and independence. That deserves recognition.

It does not mean ignoring problems. Every update introduces features, moves controls and rearranges menus. Some changes genuinely help. Others force us to relearn procedures we had already mastered. Occasionally an accessibility bug wastes more time than all the new features save. Nobody should have to celebrate the fact that an essential function still works after a software update. That ought to be normal.

John Ternus knows how the machinery works

The new chief executive comes from hardware engineering and has spent years developing Apple products. That could encourage closer integration among devices, processors and software. But knowing how to build an iPhone is not quite the same as knowing what a person using VoiceOver encounters on an ordinary Tuesday morning.

Leadership is largely about priorities. Will eye-catching design matter more than reliability? Will we get useful features or impressive demonstrations? Will our devices continue serving us for years, or will the most interesting functions always demand a new purchase? It is too soon to credit or blame Ternus for outcomes that are not yet his. We can, however, watch the direction he takes.

Siri needs more than a pleasant voice

AI is one of Apple's major priorities. With iOS 27, Siri and Apple Intelligence are moving towards understanding requests better and helping us perform tasks in more natural conversations. For sighted people, that may be convenient. For someone using a screen reader, it could eliminate many steps: finding information, preparing a document or completing a task without navigating a forest of controls.

I have one condition: I want to remain in charge. If the assistant misunderstands, I should be able to correct it. Before an important action, it should ask for confirmation. When it does not know, it should say so. I am not particularly impressed by a machine that sounds as though it is having coffee with me. I would rather it understood a request without requiring a twenty-minute conversation to do something that once took two taps. I already have family to talk to.

AI that does things, not just talks

The news often uses the cumbersome term 'agentic artificial intelligence'. In plain language, it means an assistant that can carry out a sequence of actions instead of merely answering a question: find information, prepare a task and ask permission before completing it. For those who encounter inaccessible apps, that could be a real relief.

But it raises questions. How will we know what it is doing? Can we stop it? Will it explain its actions? What happens when it makes a mistake? Independence is not a phone secretly doing things on our behalf. It means deciding what should happen and checking that it happens as intended. Apple has an opportunity here, and an equally substantial responsibility.

Products that do not yet exist

This month has brought more reports about possible Apple glasses, future iPhone designs and closer AI integration across apps. There is talk of cleaner screens and differently positioned cameras. It all sounds interesting, but these products have not been formally introduced.

A newer device is not automatically a better one. For a blind person, removing a visible element may make little difference; what matters is whether controls remain easy to find, VoiceOver announces them correctly and familiar tasks continue to work. Beautiful design that reduces independence is a poor bargain.

The cost of living in an ecosystem

Apple has built one of the most closely integrated technology environments. The iPhone, Mac, Apple Watch and AirPods exchange information and work together conveniently. This ease of use also creates dependence: once we are comfortable in one system, moving away can be difficult. And when new features need newer devices, we face the cost of keeping up.

Some advances genuinely require more powerful processors. Nobody expects an older phone to match every capability of the latest model. What we should expect is clarity about the difference between a genuine technical limit and a commercial decision.

Replacing a phone is not simply a financial matter. For someone using assistive technology, it means adapting routines, checking apps and learning new procedures. That effort does not appear in sales figures, but it is real.

The test for Apple's new leadership

I do not expect miracles. A company does not become a different company because someone else occupies the largest office. But priorities can change. Over the coming years we will learn whether AI brings practical improvements, whether redesigned interfaces remain accessible and whether the new features justify their price. We will also see whether Apple listens to people who use its products in ways rarely featured on stage.

The strongest evidence of Ternus's success will not necessarily be a keynote full of fireworks. It will be our ability to switch on an iPhone, use VoiceOver and get on with what we need to do without wondering what has stopped working this time. If he can manage that, no speech will be necessary to prove that a new chapter has begun.

3. THE DAY ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BEGAN TO FRIGHTEN THE PEOPLE FINANCING IT

While we ask AI to translate a document or describe a photo, other people are risking fortunes trying to guess which company will lead the future. Recent weeks have supplied plenty of reasons for sleepless nights: resignations, suspected espionage, political disputes, shares rising and falling sharply, and companies investing almost unimaginable sums. All this surrounds technology that has already become an ordinary part of our lives. How did we get here?

A machine for manufacturing expectations

Imagine someone invents a machine that bakes bread in five minutes. One company sells it. Another announces a machine that needs only three minutes. A third promises to make sandwiches as well. Before any of us can test them, someone else claims to be developing one that will cook an entire meal. Customers get excited. So do investors.

An investor puts money into a business in the hope of getting more back. When a company appears to have a bright future, more people want shares—small pieces of ownership—and the price can rise. So far, nothing unusual. Trouble begins when people pay a price based on millions of expected sales before the company has proved that it can build the machines at scale or make a profit.

That is what concerns some economists about AI. Major companies are spending fortunes on specialist computers, data centres and electricity. A data centre is essentially a building packed with equipment that keeps digital services running. Building and operating these facilities costs a great deal, and somebody expects the investment to pay off.

Nvidia: when a scandal shakes an industry

Nvidia has become one of the key companies of the AI era. Its components power many of the machines used by advanced AI systems. That means its fortunes matter to customers, investors, governments and competitors alike.

On November 12, Jensen Huang, Nvidia's co-founder and chief executive, stepped down. His departure followed reports about personal relationships and allegations involving a woman suspected of links to Chinese espionage. Chief financial officer Colette Kress took over on an interim basis. The company's share price fell sharply and its market valuation suffered. Investigations and political debate about technology security and the export of advanced chips are continuing.

We must separate what is confirmed from what remains to be established. The resignation and the market reaction are facts; the allegations are allegations, not established findings of wrongdoing.

When a single company occupies such a central position, problems at its headquarters can spread anxiety across an entire sector. A personal controversy becomes entangled with supply chains, investment and government decisions. Sometimes reality writes plots that would seem too far-fetched for television.

Claude makes its stock market debut

Against that background, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, began trading publicly on November 19 in a remarkably large initial public offering. Going public means allowing investors to buy and sell a company's shares on a public stock exchange.

The offer price was $83 per share. On the first day, the price rose above $115 before closing around $97. It fell the following day and ended close to $89.

That does not mean Claude stopped working or that Anthropic suddenly became a bad company. It means the amount investors are willing to pay can change very quickly. A technology may be impressive without every valuation attached to its developer being reasonable.

Why are governments getting involved?

AI is not used only for chatting with an assistant. It matters to scientific research, manufacturing, communications, defence and other strategic activities. Advanced computer chips have become particularly valuable. Those who make them have an advantage; those who must buy them depend on others. That helps explain export restrictions, international negotiations and disagreements over access.

Industrial espionage also matters in this environment. It means seeking confidential information from businesses, researchers or governments without permission. It does not mean every question we put to an AI assistant is being watched by a secret agent in a trench coat. It means that valuable technology attracts some unlawful attempts to gain an advantage. These are different issues, even when the headlines blend them together.

Are we in a bubble?

An economic bubble forms when prices are driven up by expectations that may be unrealistic. Imagine a home worth €200,000. Someone pays €300,000 because they expect to sell it for €400,000. The next buyer thinks the same way. As long as new buyers keep paying more, the party continues. The problem comes when buyers disappear and people discover they paid far more than the asset was worth.

Something similar happened with many internet companies at the beginning of this century. Some lost much of their value and others disappeared. Yet the internet itself continued to grow. This distinction matters today: AI can transform society even if some investments in it prove excessive.

The International Monetary Fund and the European Central Bank have warned about risks connected with profit expectations, borrowing and investments concentrated in a small number of companies. Borrowing is money that must be paid back. It may help a business grow; the problem is when the expected income does not arrive but the bills do.

Does this mean the bubble is certain to burst? No. There are real risks, and nobody yet knows whether every business can deliver what it promises.

What happens to us if things go wrong?

Imagine that expectations were exaggerated and some investments lose value. Companies might cut spending, lay off employees, cancel projects or close. Others might increase prices, reduce free services or look for buyers. If losses spread widely enough, banks and other parts of the economy could suffer too.

But a stock market crisis would not switch off the internet. Our phones would not become paperweights overnight. For many users the consequences might be quieter: an app loses features, a subscription becomes more expensive or a service closes. And that is where assistive technology users have particular reasons to pay attention.

When losing an app means losing independence

For blind people, some tools are more than entertainment. They help us read printed documents, recognise objects, interpret images and access information for which we might otherwise need another person's help. Many depend on outside providers, online AI systems and subscriptions. If a provider disappears, changes its terms or removes a function, the effect can be serious.

There is also a risk that features built for smaller groups of users receive less investment when companies cut costs. That outcome is not inevitable. It is, however, important enough to consider.

We need alternatives and, where practical, tools that can operate directly on our own devices. Manufacturers should treat accessibility as an essential part of a product, not an optional decoration to discard when budgets tighten.

We do not need to be sold the universe

AI offers possibilities that seemed like science fiction a few years ago. Denying that would be absurd. But treating every marketing promise as a proven fact would be equally unwise. We can enjoy these technologies while demanding reliability, accessibility and a sustainable business behind them.

A useful way to read the news is to distinguish three things: what a tool can do today, what its makers say it may do tomorrow, and what investors hope to earn from those promises. They are not the same.

I care far less about the movement of a company's share price than about whether I can still use its tools tomorrow, what they will cost and whether they will work with my screen reader. Progress should be measured not only by the fortunes being made, but also by the people who can now do something they could not do before. If we spend staggering amounts on machines that promise to solve every problem and still have to ask someone else to press an inaccessible button, we may need to reconsider what we mean by intelligence.

TEN SHORT NEWS STORIES

1. A STEP TOWARDS VISUAL PROSTHESES

November 12, 2026.

Scientists have used AI to interpret the visual signals produced by an experimental brain implant more effectively. The study, published in Neuron, examined the light-like perceptions reported by a blind participant.

This is not a cure for blindness, nor does it mean sight has been broadly restored. It is experimental work, but learning how the brain responds to such signals may support the development of future visual prostheses.

2. SOME LEUKAEMIAS BEGIN DEVELOPING DECADES BEFORE DIAGNOSIS

November 13, 2026.

Researchers have reconstructed stages in the development of acute myeloid leukaemia, finding cellular changes that began long before the disease appeared. The Nature study used blood samples collected over several years to follow particular mutations.

The findings may improve understanding of the disease and eventually help identify people at higher risk. There is still no general test that can predict every case, but the research opens another avenue for investigation.

3. A SPACECRAFT RETURNS FROM ORBIT AND TWO GIANT ROBOTIC ARMS CATCH IT

November 14, 2026.

SpaceX recovered the upper stage of Starship after an orbital mission, catching it with the giant arms on its launch tower. It was the first catch of this kind for that part of the vehicle. Reusing spacecraft could reduce costs and support future missions.

Not everything went to plan: the main booster did not complete its planned recovery. Even spectacular advances usually arrive with a few difficulties.

4. VOYAGER 1 REACHES AN ALMOST UNIMAGINABLE DISTANCE

November 18, 2026.

Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 reached approximately one light-day from Earth.

That is the distance light travels in twenty-four hours: around 26 billion kilometres. A radio signal from Earth takes about a day to reach the probe, so even an immediate reply would take a further day to get back.

And we still get impatient when a web page takes five seconds to load.

5. A SPANISH SPACE COMPANY SECURES ITS FIRST COMMERCIAL SATELLITE LAUNCH CUSTOMER

November 18, 2026.

Spain-based PLD Space signed its first commercial launch contract with Japan's Mitsubishi Electric, to carry a satellite on its MIURA 5 rocket. The agreement represents progress towards the international launch market.

The rocket has not yet completed its first orbital mission. But the contract shows that a smaller European company can begin attracting international customers in an industry long dominated by a few major players.

6. THE EUROPEAN UNION QUESTIONS WHETHER TEMU PROTECTS SHOPPERS ADEQUATELY

November 17, 2026.

The European Commission has issued further preliminary findings concerning Temu. It believes the platform may not be meeting certain obligations designed to reduce the risk of illegal products being sold. The proceedings are not a final ruling, and the company can respond.

The issue matters to millions of shoppers in the European Union. A low price is attractive; an unsafe product considerably less so.

7. FOSSIL TEETH COMPLICATE THE STORY OF OUR ANCESTORS

November 19, 2026.

Research on fossil teeth found in caves in China suggests that several ancient human groups may have adapted to tropical forest environments. The finds point to a more complex evolutionary history than previously thought.

One unusual detail: porcupines had carried some remains into the caves. Sometimes researchers need extraordinary excavations. At other times, they owe a small debt of gratitude to an animal that had no intention of assisting palaeontology.

8. FORD MAKES BATTERIES IN MICHIGAN WITH CHINESE TECHNOLOGY

November 18, 2026.

Ford has begun producing battery cells for electric vehicles at a facility in the US state of Michigan. The plant belongs to the American manufacturer, but uses technology licensed from CATL, a major Chinese battery company. The arrangement has prompted political controversy in the United States.

The story illustrates how much modern manufacturing depends on knowledge and components that cross borders, even when governments are engaged in serious trade disputes.

9. TWO SPACE PROBES PREPARE TO STUDY THE PLANET CLOSEST TO THE SUN

November 21, 2026.

BepiColombo, a joint mission of the European and Japanese space agencies, is scheduled to enter orbit around Mercury after a journey lasting about eight years. Its two scientific orbiters will study the planet, its surface, composition and magnetic environment.

The insertion manoeuvre is scheduled for November 21, with additional operations in the months ahead. At the time this issue was prepared, the outcome still required confirmation from official mission updates.

10. META ADDS MORE LANGUAGES TO ITS GLASSES ASSISTANT

November 11, 2026.

Meta says its AI assistant for certain smart-glasses models will support additional languages, including Portuguese, Polish, Turkish, Arabic and Mandarin Chinese, with broader availability in more countries.

This may not sound as dramatic as catching a spacecraft, but it has an important everyday consequence: technology becomes far more useful when people can speak their own language. Accessibility features should be offered on equal terms too, wherever users live.

CLOSING REMARKS

If you have reached this point, you will have seen just how much can fit under the heading of technology: glasses beginning to interpret images for us, corporate decisions that may change our habits and scientific discoveries that challenge what we thought we knew.

I will keep paying attention to how each new idea works away from the advertising. The possibilities interest me, but so does the button that fails, the feature that never reaches a particular country or the subscription that turns an everyday aid into a luxury.
