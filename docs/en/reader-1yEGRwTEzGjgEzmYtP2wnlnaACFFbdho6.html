When the Thermometer Gets Cocky

What heat really does to our bodies, for better and for worse

By Tony Acosta - TifloAcosta Channel

I do not like heat. At all.

Some people welcome summer as if a millionaire cousin from America had just arrived to share out the inheritance. I do not. Whenever I hear someone say, “What a gorgeous day, 35 degrees,” I always think we have rather different ideas about what the word gorgeous means.

That confession probably matters very little to medicine, but quite a lot to the person writing this study.

Because what I like is one thing and what science says is another. And since we are here to find out what heat really does to the human body, I am going to try to behave myself. I make no promises about managing it all the way through.

I am not a doctor either. We established that reasonably well when we wandered into that previous garden of sleep, melatonin and circadian rhythms. But I am curious. And you start out wondering whether heat is simply uncomfortable and end up reading about vasodilation, plasma volume, thermoregulation, kidney injury and heat-shock proteins, once again wondering exactly when the afternoon went off the rails.

So here we go, because heat is not just about sweating, sleeping with one leg outside the sheets and arguing with whoever decided that 27 degrees is an acceptable air-conditioning setting. Quite a lot more is happening inside the body.

HEAT IS NOT THE ENEMY. TOO MUCH HEAT CAN BE

It is worth starting by dismantling one oversimplification. Heat is not a poison. Our bodies produce heat constantly; in fact, being alive means producing it. Muscles work, organs function, we digest food and we move. All of that generates heat.

The problem begins when we produce or absorb more heat than we can get rid of. That is when the story changes.

The World Health Organization regards heat as a major environmental and occupational health hazard. Between 2000 and 2019, an estimated 489,000 heat-related deaths occurred worldwide each year. Heat-related mortality among people over 65 also rose by about 85% when the periods 2000–2004 and 2017–2021 were compared.

So we are not talking about July being a bit annoying. We are talking about something capable of making people seriously ill and of killing them.

WE COME WITH BUILT-IN AIR CONDITIONING

Our bodies try to keep internal temperature within a fairly narrow range, and they have several mechanisms for doing it.

When we begin to heat up, one of the first things the body does is send more blood towards the skin. The skin blood vessels widen. In plain language: the pipes near the surface open up so that more warm blood can pass through them and release heat to the outside.

That makes the cardiovascular system work differently. The heart has to maintain blood pressure, keep important organs supplied and, at the same time, send a substantial amount of blood towards the skin to help us lose heat.

Then our second major tool arrives: sweat.

We release water and electrolytes onto the skin. When that sweat evaporates, it carries heat away. There is one crucial word there: evaporates. Sweat running down your back, soaking your shirt and making you look as though you have just come out of a washing machine is not necessarily cooling you efficiently.

To cool us, it needs to evaporate. That is why humidity matters so much.

30 DEGREES IS NOT ALWAYS THE SAME 30 DEGREES

Two places can have exactly the same air temperature and produce very different sensations and risks. In dry air, sweat can evaporate more easily. In very humid air, evaporation becomes harder. And when sweat cannot evaporate properly, we lose one of our best cooling mechanisms.

That is why the number on the thermometer is not enough. Humidity, wind, sun, what we are doing and how long we have been exposed also matter.

Occupational health even uses a measure called the WBGT index to assess several of those factors together. Nobody needs to memorise the initials to survive summer. The useful point is that our bodies do not interact with one lonely number on a thermometer; they interact with the whole environment around them.

So “It is not even that hot” may be a first-class piece of terrace meteorology, but medically it does not tell us very much.

THE HEART STARTS WORKING OVERTIME

To cool us down, more blood has to reach the skin, and that blood does not appear by magic. The circulatory system has to redistribute it.

The heart works harder while we begin losing fluid through sweat. If those losses are not replaced, the volume available for circulation falls.

So now we are asking the heart to do two delightful things at once: send lots of blood to the skin so we can cool down, while working with progressively less fluid. Excellent plan.

In a healthy person under moderate conditions, the body can usually manage this reasonably well. When heat is intense, lasts a long time, is combined with exercise or dehydration, or occurs in someone with cardiovascular disease, the safety margin can shrink considerably.

Heatwaves have been associated with increased cardiovascular illness and mortality, particularly among older people and those with conditions such as coronary heart disease or heart failure.

That does not mean going outside on a hot day will cause a heart attack. It means something much more sensible: if the cardiovascular system is already under strain, adding a major heat load can make its job harder.

THE KIDNEYS GET THEIR SHARE OF THE FUN TOO

The kidneys have plenty to do in hot weather. They help regulate water, salts and circulating volume.

When we become very hot, more blood is directed towards the skin. If we also become dehydrated, the available circulating volume falls. The kidneys try to conserve water and electrolytes while they may also be receiving less blood flow.

Under some circumstances, that combination can contribute to acute kidney injury. The concern is particularly important when intense heat, heavy physical work and repeated dehydration occur together, as can happen in demanding jobs carried out for long hours in hot environments.

That also explains why “just drink something when you feel thirsty and you will be fine” is a rather thin answer when the exposure is intense and prolonged.

THE BRAIN IS NOT PARTICULARLY KEEN ON BEING COOKED EITHER

In substantial heat, attention, decision-making and some cognitive functions can worsen. Several things are happening at once: body temperature rises, dehydration may develop, the cardiovascular system is under strain and fatigue builds up.

Add to that the wonderful experience of trying to think while your body is mainly occupied with preventing you from turning into a radiator.

Research in heat-stress conditions shows that some mental tasks can deteriorate and that acclimatisation, cooling and appropriate hydration can reduce part of that effect.

In everyday language: in heavy heat we may think less clearly, react more slowly and make more mistakes. Not necessarily because we have suddenly become stupid. The body has some rather urgent business to deal with.

This matters beyond medicine. It matters when driving, operating machinery or doing any job where a mistake has consequences.

HEAT AND SLEEP: ANOTHER TROUBLED RELATIONSHIP

This is where the subject crosses neatly into our previous study.

Sleep and body-temperature regulation are closely linked. To fall asleep and stay asleep properly, the body needs to make certain thermal adjustments, and a bedroom that is too hot can interfere with that process.

Scientific reviews have linked higher ambient temperatures with poorer sleep quality, shorter sleep, greater difficulty falling asleep, more awakenings and less restorative rest. The effect is especially noticeable during very hot nights and when the body has little opportunity to cool down.

So yes: that night when you turn over forty times, change position another thirty, flip the pillow to the supposedly cold side and eventually consider moving into the refrigerator has a physiological explanation.

The problem does not necessarily end when we get up. Poor sleep means starting the next day more tired and less able to concentrate, and if the heat continues the body goes straight back into the same cycle.

Heat, poor sleep, fatigue, more heat.

Lovely.

HEAT CAN AFFECT MENTAL HEALTH TOO

This part needs some care.

Epidemiological evidence links extreme temperatures with increases in some mental-health problems and with greater use of health services for mood disorders.

But knowing that an association exists is not the same as fully understanding every mechanism behind it.

Heat can increase physical stress, worsen sleep, cause fatigue and make ordinary daily activities harder. All of that can affect psychological wellbeing. Some mental illnesses, and some medicines used to treat them, can also make a person more vulnerable to heat.

So a sentence such as “heat causes depression” would be far too simplistic.

The more accurate conclusion is that extreme temperatures can harm psychological wellbeing and have been associated with adverse mental-health outcomes, while researchers are still working out how much is due to direct physiological effects and how much is mediated through sleep, isolation and social conditions.

WITH DIABETES, HEAT DESERVES A LITTLE MORE RESPECT

Diabetes can alter some mechanisms involved in skin circulation and temperature regulation. Heat also changes hydration needs and can make the condition harder to manage.

Research has found increases in diabetes-related hospital admissions and emergency visits during episodes of extreme heat, particularly among older adults.

That does not mean a person with diabetes has to lock themselves indoors every time the thermometer rises. It means their safety margin may be different and hydration, medication and disease management deserve extra attention during very hot periods.

PREGNANCY: TWO ORGANISMS AND A COOLING SYSTEM WITH MORE WORK TO DO

Pregnancy also deserves special attention.

A large review bringing together almost two hundred studies from many countries found associations between high heat exposure and a range of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including a higher risk of preterm birth.

We also know that heat exposure can increase maternal heart rate and body temperature and can raise fetal heart rate.

That does not mean a pregnant woman cannot go outside when it is hot. It means pregnancy is physiologically one of the situations in which intense heat deserves particular caution.

As with almost everything in this study, the right message is not panic. It is awareness.

MEDICINES: THERE IS A TRAP HERE THAT MANY PEOPLE DO NOT KNOW ABOUT

Some medicines can change the way we respond to heat. Some reduce thirst, some affect sweating, and others can promote dehydration or alter the way the body handles certain drugs.

Examples include some diuretics, beta-blockers, antihistamines, antipsychotics and antidepressants, although the effect depends on the specific medicine and the individual.

But this matters: appearing on a list of medicines that can interact with heat does not mean a medicine should be stopped.

Nobody should reduce, stop or change a prescribed treatment because they have read this study. That decision belongs in a conversation with the appropriate healthcare professional.

Let us not solve the heat problem by creating a bigger one. That would be a remarkably inventive way to make summer worse.

WORKING IN HEAT IS NOT A TEST OF MANHOOD, OR OF ANYTHING ELSE

Some people can choose whether to go for a walk at three in the afternoon. Others are working at exactly that time because that is how somebody organised the shift.

Agriculture, construction, delivery work and some industrial jobs are obvious examples, but the problem is much wider. You do not even need to be under direct sun: kitchens, industrial buildings and greenhouses can become ovens in their own right.

The World Health Organization estimates that more than 2.4 billion workers worldwide are exposed to excessive heat. This is not simply about feeling uncomfortable: dehydration, fainting, kidney injury, exhaustion, accidents and heatstroke all become concerns.

Serious prevention includes adjusting workload, moving the hardest tasks to cooler hours where possible, providing water, breaks, shade or cooled spaces, and allowing time for acclimatisation.

Labour law is not identical everywhere, so there is little point pretending there is one universal heat regulation for the entire planet.

But the health principle is fairly universal: a heat plan cannot simply be telling the worker, “Put up with it; we have always worked like this.”

It sounds even less convincing when the person saying it is sitting in an air-conditioned office.

THE SKIN COMPLAINS TOO, BUT NOT EVERYTHING THAT HAPPENS IN SUMMER IS CAUSED BY HEAT

Heat rash, also called miliaria, can develop when heavy sweating irritates the skin and interferes with normal sweat flow. It is more common in hot, humid conditions and often appears as small bumps or blisters, especially where moisture collects or skin rubs.

It usually improves by keeping the skin cool and dry and reducing heat exposure.

Then there is sunburn.

This is where two different things are often thrown into the same summer bag. Sunburn is not caused by heat. It is caused by excessive ultraviolet radiation.

You can get sunburnt on a day that does not feel particularly hot, and you can become seriously ill from heat without receiving direct sunlight at all.

Ultraviolet radiation can damage the skin and increase the risk of skin cancer, so sun protection still matters even though this study is mainly about heat.

Sun and heat often turn up in the same place.

But they are not the same thing.

FROM FEELING HOT TO BEING IN DANGER

Heat-related illnesses are not all the same.

Heat rash, cramps or dizziness can occur. Heat exhaustion can develop and, at the most serious end of the scale, there is heatstroke.

Heat exhaustion is commonly associated with heavy sweating, weakness, thirst, headache, dizziness, nausea and reduced urine output. The body is still trying to cool itself, but it is beginning to run out of room to manoeuvre.

At that point the activity should stop, the person should move to a cooler place, cooling should begin, and fluids can be given if the person is conscious and able to drink safely.

But there is a much more serious level.

A PARKED CAR CAN BECOME A TRAP VERY QUICKLY

I am going to pause here because this point deserves not to be buried between two ordinary paragraphs.

A child should never be left inside a parked vehicle on a hot day. Not “just for a minute”, not “I left the window slightly open”, and not “I am only going into the shop and coming straight back”.

Cars can heat up astonishingly quickly. Health authorities warn that the temperature inside can rise by roughly 11°C in the first ten minutes, even with a window partly open.

Children also regulate heat differently from adults and can reach a dangerous situation more quickly.

And while this study is about human beings, the same rule belongs here: do not leave a dog, cat or any other animal inside a parked vehicle in hot weather. They can also develop life-threatening hyperthermia.

This is not a question of calculating how long we think they can tolerate it.

Do not leave them inside.

Full stop.

HEATSTROKE IS NOT JUST “A BIT TOO MUCH HEAT”

Heatstroke is a medical emergency.

We are talking about severe failure of thermoregulation accompanied by effects on the central nervous system. Confusion, unusual behaviour, difficulty speaking, seizures, loss of consciousness and a very high body temperature can occur.

And one detail matters: it is not true that every person with heatstroke must have dry skin and must have stopped sweating. In some forms of heatstroke, sweating can still be present.

So do not wait to decide whether somebody is sweating as if you were solving a quiz question.

If significant heat exposure is followed by confusion, disorientation, speech problems, seizures or loss of consciousness, think medical emergency.

Heatstroke can cause cellular injury, coagulation abnormalities and failure of several organs. At this point we have left “a bit overheated” a very long way behind.

SO WHAT DO WE DO?

Call for urgent medical help and start cooling the person while help is on the way.

Move them to a cooler place, remove unnecessary clothing and use active cooling methods: cold water, wet cloths, air movement and, when circumstances allow, more intensive cooling techniques.

Heatstroke is not treated as though it were an ordinary fever.

Medicines such as paracetamol or aspirin do not correct the mechanism causing this hyperthermia and should not be used as treatment for heatstroke.

Fever and heat-induced hyperthermia are not the same thing. With a fever, the body has deliberately changed its temperature set point. With heatstroke, the body is being overwhelmed in its attempt to get rid of heat.

Same high thermometer reading.

Completely different film.

WHO HAS A HARDER TIME WITH HEAT?

Anyone can develop a heat-related illness. “Anyone” includes the fit young athlete who is absolutely convinced nothing ever happens to them.

But some people have less room for error: older adults, young children, pregnant people, those with chronic illness, workers exposed to heat and people taking certain medicines.

There is another kind of vulnerability that does not show up in a blood test: not having access to a cool place.

Someone can understand every recommendation in this study and still live in housing that becomes unbearably hot, be unable to afford air conditioning or work in a job where they have very little control over when they can take a break.

Heat also has a social dimension.

We do not all face the same thermometer with the same tools.

THE BODY CAN LEARN TO GET ALONG WITH HEAT A LITTLE BETTER

This is the part that personally annoys me, because after spending pages collecting arguments to justify my dislike of heat, science decides to spoil the speech.

Yes: the body can adapt to some extent.

When a person is repeatedly exposed to heat in a gradual and controlled way, acclimatisation develops. The body learns to respond more efficiently: sweating can begin earlier, blood distribution can improve, and heart rate and core temperature may rise less during the same level of work.

A large quantitative review published in 2025, drawing on more than two hundred studies, confirmed many of these adaptations and also found improvements in physical performance in hot environments.

Acclimatisation usually develops over several days of repeated exposure. It can also be partly lost after time away from the heat.

But nobody should translate that into “the more heat I endure, the stronger I become”.

Acclimatisation increases our ability to cope with certain conditions. It does not make us immune, and it does not turn dangerous heat into healthy heat.

Somebody used to hot conditions may tolerate them better than a newcomer, but they can still develop heatstroke.

The body learns.

It does not perform miracles.

SAUNAS, HOT BATHS AND HEAT THERAPIES: NOW IT GETS INTERESTING

There is research into so-called passive heating: saunas, hot baths and other ways of raising body temperature in a controlled manner without exercise.

Some studies have found small improvements in cardiovascular measures, particularly certain blood-pressure and vascular-function outcomes. Other analyses find no clear benefit in most of the markers studied.

The conclusion?

The results are interesting and worth continuing to investigate.

What we cannot responsibly do is turn the sauna into a pharmacy and claim it prevents heart attacks, cures disease or replaces exercise.

That sounds terrific on the internet.

Medicine needs a little more.

AND NO, SWEATING IS NOT A FULL-BODY CLEANING SERVICE

Sweat contains water, salts and small amounts of other substances, but the liver and kidneys are among the body’s main systems for processing and eliminating waste products.

The primary job of sweat in hot conditions is to cool us. It is not running an internal deep-cleaning service.

After a sauna we may weigh a little less. Wonderful. We have mainly lost water. Once we replace it, the scales recover much of their sense of humour.

Sweating heavily does not necessarily mean we are getting a magnificent workout either.

Sometimes it means exactly what it looks like: we are very hot.

HOW MUCH WATER SHOULD WE DRINK?

It would be wonderfully convenient to write: two and a half litres. End of chapter.

Unfortunately, the human body has the irritating habit of not being manufactured to one standard specification.

Needs vary with the person, the activity, the environment and how much sweat is being lost.

During hot periods it is sensible to drink regularly rather than waiting for intense thirst. When sweating is heavy for many hours or physical work is intense, salts are also lost and in some situations electrolytes may need to be replaced.

But drinking enormous amounts of water without need is not a brilliant idea either. Extreme overconsumption can dangerously lower the sodium concentration in the blood.

And some people with heart failure, kidney disease or other conditions have specific instructions about fluid intake. For them, the generic advice to “drink loads” can actually be inappropriate.

So: stay hydrated, yes.

Turn it into a five-litre water-drinking competition, no.

ALCOHOL DOES NOT COOL THE BODY EITHER

It may cool your throat. That is not quite the same thing.

Alcohol can make hydration more difficult and can also impair perception and decision-making. That is why health authorities recommend limiting or avoiding it during periods of intense heat.

An ice-cold beer may feel excellent.

That does not make it intravenous fluid.

WHAT ABOUT A FAN?

Even this comes with small print.

A fan moves air and can help sweat evaporate, which often improves comfort considerably. But a fan does not manufacture cold air.

When ambient temperatures become extremely high, moving very hot air across the body may no longer be enough and can sometimes add to heat gain.

Advice also depends on humidity and on the person’s condition. The important idea is not to rely on a fan as the only protection when the environment is dangerously hot.

There is no magical switch at one exact temperature.

In extreme heat we need methods that truly reduce heat load: moving to a cooler environment, wetting the skin, taking a cool shower or using air conditioning.

AIR CONDITIONING: THE APPLIANCE I WOULD BUILD A STATUE TO

My medical objectivity may be under considerable strain here, so let us separate the two things.

Science says that access to cooler environments during periods of intense heat reduces heat exposure and is one of the most effective ways of preventing heat-related illness.

Tony says it is also one of the great achievements of modern civilisation.

With science and opinion safely separated, we may continue.

HEAT DOES NOT WORK ALONE

There is another point we sometimes forget. A heatwave can coincide with air pollution, cause power cuts, make some medicines harder to store and make work or travel more hazardous.

It can also hit especially hard those who live alone, have mobility difficulties or cannot easily reach a cooler place.

And extremely hot nights can prevent the body from recovering from the heat load accumulated during the day.

That is why medicine and public health no longer treat extreme heat as merely a weather issue.

It is a health issue.

SO WHERE IS THE LINE BETWEEN USEFUL HEAT AND DANGEROUS HEAT?

There is no single magic temperature.

We cannot say, “Up to 34 degrees, wonderful; at 35, disaster.”

Temperature, humidity, duration of exposure, what we are doing and our own health all matter.

Thirty-five degrees for a few minutes in the shade is not the same situation as several hours of heavy work in direct sun. Nor is 35 degrees in dry air the same as 35 degrees with very high humidity.

And a temperature that a young acclimatised person may tolerate reasonably well can become a serious problem for an 85-year-old with heart failure.

That is why public-health heat alerts try to estimate risk for a population rather than relying on one isolated thermometer reading.

A FEW SIGNALS YOU SHOULD NOT NEGOTIATE WITH YOUR BODY

Intense thirst, unusual weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps or feeling faint are signs that we should stop asking the body to carry on as though nothing were happening.

And if major heat exposure is followed by confusion, disorientation, speech problems, seizures or loss of consciousness, we have entered a different category.

That is not the moment to wait and see whether it “passes”.

Get help and start cooling.

POCKET SUMMARY FOR WHEN THE THERMOMETER IS NOT IN THE MOOD FOR AN ESSAY

  • Reduce activity during the hottest hours.
  • Find shade or a cooler place.
  • Drink regularly.
  • If you need to cool down, use water, a cool shower or wet cloths.
  • Never leave children or animals inside a parked car.
  • Pay extra attention to older people, those with chronic illness and people who live alone.
  • If someone exposed to heat becomes confused, has a seizure or loses consciousness, this is not simply “being very hot”: it may be a medical emergency.

You do not need to turn summer into an entrance exam for medical school to remember that.

AFTER ALL THIS, IS SUMMER STILL A GOOD THING?

That is no longer a medical question. Some people love it. I remain firmly on the other team.

But after looking at the evidence, I do have to give heat some credit. The human body has an extraordinary capacity to deal with it. It can acclimatise, improve its cooling responses and learn to function more efficiently in a hot environment.

There is also interesting research into possible cardiovascular benefits from certain controlled forms of heat exposure.

That is considerably more praise than I expected to give heat when I started.

But there is a difference between using heat and allowing heat to use us.

WHAT I AM LEFT WITH AFTER THIS WHOLE JOURNEY

Before starting this study, my relationship with heat was simple: I do not like it.

Now that I have finished it, I can report that my relationship with heat remains simple: I do not like it.

The difference is that I now have a scientifically unnecessary number of reasons to explain why.

But I also learned something worth keeping. The human body is not a passive thermometer. It is a remarkable machine that detects change, redistributes blood, produces sweat, conserves water, adjusts cardiovascular function and, given time, can even learn to respond better to a hot environment.

All of that happens without us opening an app or going into Settings, which, coming from the technology world, I find deeply suspicious.

The problem begins when we ask that system to do more than it can.

Extreme heat is not merely uncomfortable. It can affect the heart, kidneys, brain, sleep, mental health, pregnancy and illnesses that were already present. It can impair our ability to work or make decisions and, when thermoregulation is overwhelmed, it can become a life-threatening medical emergency.

But we should not swing to the opposite extreme either. Not all heat is harmful. Acclimatisation is real, and controlled heat exposure is producing medically interesting results that deserve further investigation.

So the conclusion is rather less dramatic than the internet might prefer.

Heat is neither a miracle medicine nor a poison we must flee the moment the thermometer passes 20 degrees.

It depends on how much heat, for how long, who is exposed, what that person is doing and whether the body can continue to shed the heat it is accumulating.

As so often happens in medicine, the correct answer ends up being: it depends.

What an ungrateful phrase for a headline.

And what a useful one for avoiding nonsense.

FINAL NOTE

This is a medical-education and public-information study based on international health-agency guidance and scientific literature. It does not replace diagnosis, treatment or individual advice from a healthcare professional.

People with cardiovascular, kidney, respiratory or metabolic disease, pregnant people and those taking regular medication may need specific advice during periods of intense heat.

And if heatstroke is suspected, this is not something to leave for a calm consultation tomorrow.

That is the moment to stop reading studies and act.
