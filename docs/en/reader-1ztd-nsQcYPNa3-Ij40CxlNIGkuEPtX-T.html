Technology Pills No. 21 of 2026

Tony Acosta. TifloAcosta Channel.

September 6, 2026

Before we start

Hello hello, lovely people.

Today I am bringing you a batch of Technology Pills while the table is still not cleared. IFA is still open in Berlin, Apple already has the stage ready for Wednesday, September 9, and around the AirPods there are people adding cameras, sensors and almost a license plate before Tim Cook has even said a word.

We are right in that entertaining moment when technology mixes three things at once: what already exists, what is about to arrive, and what somebody has decided to turn into a headline because it sounded wonderful. If we do not tidy the drawer a little, we end up looking for a feature our phone does not have, waiting for headphones that are not coming this year, or assuming that any gadget with artificial intelligence has somehow solved accessibility as part of the deal.

So this edition is about putting things in order. Without killing the excitement, because technology would be terribly dull if nothing ever made us curious, but also without buying the motorbike before checking whether it actually has wheels.

What we have in this edition

We will start with iOS 27. Not to repeat an endless list of new features, but to make clear which iPhones can install it, which features depend on Apple Intelligence, what is happening with Siri AI, what changes in the European Union, what we know about languages, and which accessibility improvements Apple has already confirmed.

Then we will head to IFA 2026, the big technology fair in Berlin. There is a bit of everything there: devices that may open interesting possibilities for us, and others that prove once again that putting cameras, artificial intelligence and voice control inside a box does not automatically make the box accessible.

Our third stop will be the AirPods. Everything points to two new AirPods 5 models at Apple's presentation, while the famous camera-equipped AirPods seem to have moved house to 2027. We will look at what is expected from each one and why, when those cameras finally arrive, they may be far more interesting to a blind person than for taking a photo with an ear.

And at the end, as in the previous edition, ten short news items to round things out without turning each one into an encyclopedia.

iOS 27: updating will be easy; knowing exactly what you get, not so much

iOS 27 is getting close, and this year there is something worth doing before happily throwing ourselves at the Update button: finding out what on earth we are actually going to receive.

Because we will see videos saying that iOS 27 does this, that and everything in between, we will update quite happily, and somebody will eventually wonder where half the new features have gone. And no, the iPhone will not have eaten them during installation.

Which iPhones can install iOS 27

Apple is keeping compatibility fairly broad. Every iPhone 11, 12, 13 and 14 in its different versions can update; so can iPhone 15, 15 Plus, 15 Pro and 15 Pro Max; the entire iPhone 16 family; the iPhone 17 family, including iPhone Air and 17e; and iPhone SE second generation or later.

So far, so good. The trouble begins when we mix up two sentences that do not mean the same thing: "my iPhone can install iOS 27" and "my iPhone will receive every iOS 27 feature".

Two leagues of iPhone inside the same operating system

For Apple Intelligence and Siri AI, the dividing line is still the iPhone 15 Pro and 15 Pro Max. Every iPhone 16 or later is also compatible. The standard iPhone 15 and 15 Plus can install iOS 27, but they do not enter this generation of artificial intelligence features. Naturally, the same applies to the iPhone 11, 12, 13 and 14.

That explains a good part of the confusion we are going to see. Two people can quite truthfully say "I have iOS 27" and end up with phones that can do rather different things.

What still comes to the system without Apple Intelligence

iOS 27 is not just artificial intelligence. Apple has refined Liquid Glass to make it more consistent, with greater contrast and a control that lets us decide how transparent or tinted we want the interface to be. Apple also promises performance improvements for tasks such as opening apps, loading photos and using AirDrop, and it has worked on smoother handoffs between Wi-Fi and the mobile network.

Mail improves search and the relevance of its results. iCloud Shared Albums make it easier for people using Android or Windows to take part through iCloud.com, allow full-resolution photo uploads, and add new ways to filter, react and invite.

There are also changes to child controls and family safety, improvements in Maps, new possibilities with hearing devices, updates in Home and other features that depend on the accessory or service we use. In other words, an iPhone without Apple Intelligence is not frozen in the past. It will receive iOS 27, its fixes, interface changes and many general features. It simply will not receive the part that needs Apple Intelligence hardware.

Apple Intelligence and Siri AI are not exactly the same thing either

Here is another future source of confusion. Apple Intelligence is the collection of artificial intelligence features Apple distributes across the system. Siri AI is the new generation of the assistant and belongs to that collection, but it has its own timetable and its own limitations.

Apple Intelligence already supports English, Danish, Dutch, French, German, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Spanish, Swedish, Turkish, Vietnamese, Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean. Even so, the fact that the system supports our language does not mean every single feature will do so from day one.

Siri AI, for example, will initially arrive in beta on compatible devices configured in English. Apple says it will expand languages quickly, but as of this edition it has not given a specific date for Spanish.

The new Siri is meant to be much more conversational, understand better what appears on screen, use personal context from messages, email and photos, keep track of follow-up conversations and carry out actions inside apps. It will also have its own app where we can return to previous conversations.

European Union: iOS 27 yes; Siri AI on iPhone, not yet

For anyone living in one of the 27 European Union countries, there is an important difference. iOS 27 will arrive normally, and so will the general system features. What Apple has announced will not be available at first is Siri AI on iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch.

Apple blames the delay on requirements under the Digital Markets Act and says it has not yet found an arrangement with regulators that it considers sufficiently secure. The European Commission sees the matter differently. We do not need to turn the Technology Pills into a legal debate: the practical fact is that when iOS 27 arrives, a compatible iPhone located in the European Union will not initially have Siri AI.

There is an important curiosity here: Apple will allow Siri AI on Mac and Apple Vision Pro inside the European Union when the device is configured in a supported language. So the restriction does not affect every Apple product in the same way.

Changing an iPhone to English does not magically turn a European phone into one located outside the European Union either. The restriction is not based only on language.

Outside the European Union there will not be an all-you-can-eat buffet from minute one either

In other compatible markets, having an iPhone 15 Pro, 15 Pro Max, 16 or later opens the door to Apple Intelligence and Siri AI, but there will still be differences by language, region and feature. China, for example, is initially excluded from both Siri AI and the new Apple Intelligence features while Apple works through the country's regulatory requirements.

Some features will also appear first in English. Smart suggestions in Mail and Messages, Call Context and intelligent naming for files and folders are among the examples Apple has specifically mentioned.

Accessibility: here we do have quite a few things worth watching closely

For those of us who use VoiceOver, one of the most interesting additions is Image Explorer. Apple Intelligence will be able to provide much more detailed image descriptions across the system, including photos, scanned bills, personal documents and other visual content.

Live Recognition is getting deeper as well. On compatible iPhones we will be able to use the Action button to ask what appears in the camera view, receive a description and continue asking questions in our own words. This feels much more like having a conversation about what is in front of us than requesting one isolated description.

Magnifier gets similar capabilities for users with low vision and adds spoken commands for controlling features inside the app. Voice Control, meanwhile, will be able to understand natural descriptions of controls instead of forcing us to remember exact labels or numbers. This last feature will begin in English only in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia.

Accessibility Reader will work better with complex documents containing columns, images and tables, offer summaries, and translate content while preserving the formatting chosen by the user.

On-device generated captions are also coming for videos that do not already have them. They will work on iPhone, iPad, Mac, Apple TV and Apple Vision Pro, although initially they will be available in English in the United States and Canada.

There are also less spectacular but equally important changes: new ways to personalize Touch Accommodations, more reliable pairing and switching between devices for Made for iPhone hearing aids, spoken name recognition in more than 50 languages, an API that lets sign-language interpreting apps bring a human interpreter into a FaceTime call, and support for the Sony Access controller on iPhone, iPad and Mac.

And an Apple warning that is best left exactly where it belongs: VoiceOver and Magnifier descriptions should not be used as a navigation system, in high-risk situations, or for medical diagnosis or treatment. A camera becoming smarter does not turn an iPhone into a cane, a guide dog or a doctor.

Before looking for a button that does not exist

When iOS 27 arrives, somebody will update an iPhone 13 and ask where Siri AI is. Somebody else will have an iPhone 15 and fail to find features shown on a 15 Pro. European users will watch demonstrations recorded in the United States, and people using their phone in Spanish will be looking at a feature Apple launched first in English.

None of that means the update has gone wrong. It means that this year every feature list needs a surname: model, region and language.

Updating is still simple. What has become more complicated is knowing exactly which package of features is travelling inside your iPhone.

IFA 2026: the house thinks, the headphones talk, and we are still checking the controls

IFA 2026 is taking place in Berlin from September 4 to 8, and it has arrived loaded with artificial intelligence. There is AI in headphones, televisions, ovens, dishwashers, refrigerators, glasses and mobility systems. If you skim the headlines quickly, it looks as though by 2027 we will be able to retire from our own homes and let the appliances organize themselves.

The fair itself has strengthened its message around inclusion and accessibility this year. It even uses an idea I quite like: technology is not finished until everyone can use it. Fine. Let us take them at their word.

Plaud One: here the front door is somewhere familiar

Plaud One is one of the products worth keeping an eye on. It consists of headphones and a case that can also work as an independent device. We can hold down the agent button or say "Hey Plaud" and talk to a system capable of listening, transcribing, summarizing and working with services such as Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive, Slack, Notion and other compatible tools.

Responses can be heard directly through the headphones or through the speaker in the case. The case includes eSIM and 4G LTE, so some functions can remain available without the phone nearby. There are also physical controls on both the headphones and the case.

Plaud supports transcription and summaries in more than 112 languages, including Spanish variants for Spain, the United States and Latin America. The company does warn, however, that some beta and agent features may be optimized for English first. The initial Plaud One edition is also limited to 2,000 units and only a handful of markets: the United States, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, Spain, Canada and the Netherlands.

So this is not yet a product I can recommend internationally without qualification. But I do like the basic approach: buttons, voice, audio responses, and less need to chase a visual interface around. At least they have put the front door somewhere we can find it.

LG ThinQ Claw: a house that understands intentions, but still in writing

LG has presented ThinQ Claw, an agent designed to coordinate appliances and home services based on what we want to achieve. Instead of programming twenty rules, we could say that guests are coming over or that we are heading home and let the system combine calendar information, climate control and connected devices.

The idea makes a great deal of sense for anyone who would rather express an intention in natural language than navigate a collection of control panels. The curious detail is that, for now, LG presents ThinQ Claw as a text-chat experience.

A house capable of understanding a complicated request but still making us type it is one of those situations technology knows how to produce with remarkable elegance. If the chat works perfectly with VoiceOver and TalkBack, there is a road forward. If voice is added, there is a much wider one. Today we cannot assume either.

Miele CulinaryCoach and G8 Diamond: removing decisions can remove barriers too

Miele has brought CulinaryCoach to IFA, a cooking assistant integrated into its app. We can tell it what we want to prepare, and the system can turn that idea into the right settings for a connected oven and send them directly to the appliance.

That interests me more than another AI that simply recites a recipe. If it saves us from moving around the oven's physical control panel to choose temperature, mode and other settings, it is taking work away from a place that can be troublesome for a blind person.

The drawback is that CulinaryCoach does not yet have broad international availability. Miele has launched it in Germany and Austria, so for now we are talking more about an interesting direction than a solution available to everyone.

The company has also presented the G8 Diamond dishwasher. A camera recognizes how much we have loaded and what kinds of items are inside, adjusts the process and combines that information with automatic dosing and other systems. Fewer questions, fewer settings and fewer manual decisions can be an advantage. Whether the app and the appliance controls are equally well designed for us is a different matter.

Haier: plenty of intelligence inside and several doubts at the door

The news item that led us to IFA in the first place was Haier: a washer and dryer with four drums, ovens that recognize food through cameras, and a refrigerator capable of identifying some of what we store inside.

All of that can be very interesting for a blind person. A refrigerator that identifies products, an oven that manages cooking, or a washing machine that doses automatically and detects problems can save us real checking and guesswork.

The problem appears when we reach the controls. Many of these appliances still rely on large touchscreens. We also tried hOn, the app Haier uses for its connected ecosystem. It is available in Spanish and lets you create an account with Apple, but registration becomes a little treacherous and VoiceOver navigation is not particularly comfortable. Without a compatible appliance linked to the account, much of the app also has too little useful content for us to test properly.

Haier offers Alexa and Google Assistant control on certain appliances and supports Spanish, so I am not closing the door. But I am not going to call a system accessible because there happens to be a microphone somewhere in it. First we need to find out what can be done by voice and what sends us straight back to the screen.

RayNeo: smart glasses can be accessible without being designed for us

IFA is also putting smart glasses back on the table. Some RayNeo models offer transparent displays, captions, translation and artificial intelligence features. For a deaf or hard-of-hearing person, having captions directly in front of the eyes can be an extraordinary help.

For a completely blind person, a screen placed in front of the eyes produces rather less excitement. Other models include cameras, microphones, speakers and assistants capable of interpreting the environment, which sounds much closer to our needs. Yet we still find important functions where the answer ends up being shown visually or the system depends on touch menus.

And there is no need to get angry with a product for not being designed for us. Accessibility does not mean accessibility for every disability at the same time. What matters is knowing before we buy and not assuming that "AI glasses" automatically means "glasses for blind people".

Strutt ev1c: this time the manufacturer warns us before we start making a film in our heads

The Strutt ev1c power chair includes sensors, LiDAR, obstacle detection and intelligent driving functions. Reading only the technology list, it would be easy to think immediately of blind users.

The company itself stops that enthusiasm at the root, and rightly so. It explicitly warns that the ev1c is not specifically designed for blind or low-vision people and that certain features and menus require visual interaction. It also explains the limitations of its sensors in detecting every possible obstacle.

I find that kind of clarity much more useful than advertising stuffed with words such as autonomous, intelligent and accessible, only for us to discover later that the main button exists only on a screen.

One habit worth bringing from home

Whenever a presentation says "voice control", I would check what percentage of the device can actually be controlled by voice. When it says "visual recognition", I would look for where the result appears. When it says "automatic", I would check whether we can start and stop the process without help. And when "artificial intelligence" appears, I would breathe exactly as I did before: AI is a tool, not an accessibility certificate.

IFA 2026 is showing some magnificent things. Some remove steps that used to be a barrier. Others are one good design decision away from doing so. And some have learned to see everything while nobody has yet remembered to teach them how to tell us about it.

AirPods 5, two models and some cameras that will have to wait

On September 9 Apple opens the curtain again and, besides the new iPhones and Apple Watches, everything points to movement in the AirPods family.

A small mess has built up because for months we have been hearing about AirPods with cameras, AirPods Pro with cameras, possible AirPods Ultra, and now two AirPods 5 models have appeared as well. So before we end up waiting for headphones capable of recognizing the world, measuring our eyelashes and making the coffee, let us separate the boxes.

What has the best chance of appearing on September 9

The most recent reports point to two AirPods 5 models. One would keep the basic formula and the other would include active noise cancellation. It would be a fairly logical continuation of the strategy Apple currently uses with AirPods 4.

Alongside the rumor, references to two unreleased devices have appeared inside macOS 26.7, which adds considerable weight to the idea that two variants are ready to launch.

What we know much less about is what will change inside. The H3 chip is being mentioned as a candidate to improve sound and latency, but that has not been confirmed. There are also expectations around sensors and health, but this is where we should avoid automatically moving everything Apple has added to the Pro line into AirPods 5.

The camera-equipped AirPods are a different story

Apple is seriously working on AirPods equipped with cameras or visual sensors. Software references have already appeared, along with demonstration material connected to Visual Intelligence. So this is not an idea somebody invented yesterday afternoon.

What has changed is the date. Sources following the development of these products now place camera-equipped AirPods in 2027 rather than among the main candidates for the September 9 presentation. So unless Apple has decided to hide an enormous surprise, on September 9 we will see AirPods 5, but not the headphones that can look around.

And no, the cameras are not there so we can take photos with an ear

Apple's idea seems to be focused on Visual Intelligence. Those cameras could give Siri and Apple Intelligence information about what is in front of us: recognize objects, interpret text, remember something we have seen, or answer questions about the environment.

That is where the story changes quite a bit for us. Today we can use the iPhone camera to recognize text, objects or scenes, but first we have to take out the phone, open the right tool and manage to point the camera in the right direction.

AirPods that can capture the environment from roughly the position of our head could remove some of that gymnastics. The important question will be what information we can receive directly through audio and how far we can keep a conversation going about what the cameras are seeing.

We will also need to look at privacy, operation without the iPhone, response speed, languages and, above all, whether Apple understands that a visual sensor placed on a pair of headphones can be especially interesting when the answer ends up directly in the ear.

AirPods Ultra and AirPods Pro 4: better not invent a place for them yet

The name AirPods Ultra has appeared several times in connection with a possible higher-end camera model, but Apple has not confirmed that name. If the project once expected for 2026 has changed direction, the final name can change as well.

There are no strong reasons to expect conventional AirPods Pro 4 right now either. AirPods Pro 3 remain the current Pro generation. The next major evolution of that family could be tied precisely to visual sensors, but that takes us back to 2027.

How the family could look after the keynote

If the forecasts are right, Apple will have basic AirPods 5, AirPods 5 with active noise cancellation, AirPods Pro 3 as the Pro line, and AirPods Max 2 at the top. Models capable of "seeing" would remain for later.

It is curious to see where headphones have gone after starting out as little more than a way to put music in our ears. First came Siri, then spatial audio, then features connected with hearing, adaptation to the environment, translation and health. The next step may be for them to receive visual information as well.

When that day arrives, if those new eyes end up speaking directly into our ears, Apple may have something particularly interesting for blind people. If the important information ends up back on a screen, they will have built a fine pair of eyes and forgotten to give them a mouth.

For now, September 9 is about something rather more down to earth: two AirPods 5 models. And we will see what Apple has managed to keep hidden until the music starts.

Ten news items to round out the week

1. Android starts helping us point the camera

Google is rolling out Guided Vision inside Gemini Live, a feature designed with blind and low-vision people. It does not merely describe what the camera sees: if we are pointing badly, it can ask us to move the phone, scan around or center the object. It can be opened from accessibility shortcuts, from the TalkBack menu or from Gemini settings, and will come to phones running Android 9 or later wherever Gemini is available.

2. WhatsApp replaces the PIN with a proper password

WhatsApp is strengthening two-step verification. The old six-digit PIN can be replaced by a longer password using letters, numbers and special characters. It also allows more than one passkey and, on Android, will show more context before we answer calls from unknown numbers, such as whether they come from another country or whether we share groups with that person. The scammers are finding a few more pieces of furniture in the hallway.

3. Firefox lets us decide how much AI we actually want

Mozilla keeps a single panel in Firefox where we can block current and future artificial intelligence features or leave only the ones we want. Translations, generated alt text for images in PDFs, tab-group suggestions and chatbots can be managed separately. It may not sound like a technological triumph, but software asking how much we want instead of deciding for us is starting to feel almost exotic.

4. Firefox gets a built-in ad blocker on iPhone

Firefox for iOS includes its own blocker based on EasyList and WebKit content-blocking technology. It is off by default, and each user decides whether to enable it. It does not block every single ad, but it does stop many ads and third-party trackers without needing an extension, which is particularly useful on iPhone, where browsers have rather more limitations than they do on a computer.

5. Chrome fixes a vulnerability that was already being exploited

On September 3 Google released Chrome 152.0.7977.82 and .83 for Windows and Mac, 152.0.7977.82 for Linux, and the equivalent Android update. It fixes twelve security flaws, and one of them, in the V8 engine, already has an exploit being used in the wild. This is one of those updates worth installing when it appears rather than saving for the day we have nothing better to do.

6. Microsoft gives options back to the Copilot key

Some new computers replaced the right Control key or the Context Menu key with a dedicated Copilot key, which affected shortcuts and certain assistive technologies. Microsoft is announcing a Windows 11 update that will let users remap it so it can work again as Right Control or Context Menu. The key arrived first and the question of what each user wanted it for came later, but at least the question eventually turned up.

7. Meta introduces transcription that can separate more than twenty voices

Muse Voice Transcribe is Meta's new model for real-time audio transcription. It can distinguish more than twenty speakers, detect when each person starts and stops speaking, and switch languages within a conversation. It was trained on more than seventy languages, although Meta has especially validated a smaller group for launch. It is already used in Meta AI for Mac, Muse Code and Meta's model API.

8. OpenAI introduces GPT-6 Astra

OpenAI has introduced GPT-6 Astra, aimed particularly at long, multi-step work, programming, research and computer use. It can create documents, spreadsheets and presentations following templates and adapt when instructions change. The launch begins on a limited basis, and the company has added extra safety measures, especially because of the level the model has reached in cybersecurity capabilities.

9. Samsung takes its conversational TV assistant to more Latin American countries

Samsung is expanding Vision AI Companion to Central America, the Caribbean, Ecuador and Venezuela after rolling it out in other markets such as Brazil and Mexico. On compatible televisions it lets users ask voice questions about what they are watching and combines services such as Bixby, Gemini, Perplexity and Copilot. For us, the job will be to find out how far the spoken experience really goes and which parts of the interface still depend on the screen.

10. Artificial intelligence starts making the memory in our own devices more expensive

Demand for memory in artificial intelligence data centers is squeezing the supply intended for computers, phones, consoles and other consumer products. The Financial Times says DRAM prices have increased by roughly five times in a year and that some manufacturers are already raising prices or cutting specifications. AI may help us choose the next computer; at this rate, it may also have to help us explain the price to the credit card.

Farewell

And that is it for these Technology Pills.

This has been a curious edition because a good part of the technology still has one foot inside the box. iOS 27 is about to arrive, Apple has a presentation just around the corner, IFA is still showing things in Berlin, and some of the most striking products we have discussed still need months, or years, to show what they are actually going to become.

That is also part of following technology news. It is not all about reporting what we can already buy. Sometimes it is useful to watch where the pieces are moving, provided we make absolutely clear what is confirmed, what is still in development and what remains only an expectation.
