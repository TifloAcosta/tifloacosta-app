How Am I Supposed to Live My Life Now?

Rehabilitation and independence after sight loss

Tony Acosta · TifloAcosta

Hello, hello, lovely people.

A person loses their sight.

Sometimes it happens suddenly. An illness, an accident, a medical complication and, within hours or days, things they had done all their life without a second thought suddenly need a different way of being done.

Other times sight fades gradually. Reading becomes difficult first, then recognising a face, then walking in low light, until one day the curb you could have sworn was there yesterday seems to have conspired with the pavement and disappeared.

At some point a white cane may enter the picture.

Very often it is needed. And it can change a life.

But if we think rehabilitation after sight loss means putting a cane in someone's hand, teaching them to sweep it from side to side and wishing them the best of luck, we may as well pack up and go home, because we have understood very little.

Sight loss can affect the way we travel, of course, but also how we cook, choose clothes, identify medication, use a phone, return to work, study, shop, handle money, travel or simply enter an unfamiliar room without feeling that everything inside has arranged an ambush.

That is what this study is about.

What happens afterwards.

How independence is rebuilt when sight stops providing the information we used to rely on. What techniques exist, why they are used, what they are meant to achieve, and what changes when someone lives in a city with every imaginable service or in a place where finding an orientation and mobility instructor is already a small adventure.

And before we begin, there is one thing I want to make clear.

There is no such thing as “the standard blind person”.

A thirty-year-old woman who suddenly loses her sight and wants to return to work does not need exactly the same things as an eighty-five-year-old man with low vision, arthritis and balance problems. Nor does someone whose sight has declined over several years start from the same place as someone who wakes up after a medical procedure and discovers that they can no longer see.

Then there is deafblindness, neuropathy, motor impairment, cognitive difficulties, children who have never seen, people who live alone, others surrounded by family, and people with the latest smartphone compared with someone who has little more than a cane and a rehabilitation service several hundred kilometres away.

So we are not going to take a European model, a North American model or any other single model and then explain how “everyone else” is supposed to manage.

The person is our starting point.

And rehabilitation does not necessarily mean recovering sight that has been lost. It means learning to make the best use of the sight that remains, when there is any, and finding other ways to get information and do things when vision can no longer be relied upon.

The question is what that person needs in order to regain as much control as possible over their own life. From there, we look at the resources available.

The need may be fairly universal.

The resources, I can assure you, are not.

Before the cane, there is a person

Serious rehabilitation should begin by getting to know the person in front of us.

Knowing how much they can see is not enough.

What matters is how they use that vision, if they have any. Can they distinguish shapes, contrast or movement? Can they manage reasonably well during the day but find that everything changes after dark? We need to know how they walk, how they balance, what they can hear, how much sensation they have in their hands and feet, whether there are other health conditions, and how they are currently managing at home.

We also need to know what they did before.

And, above all, what they want to do again.

The priority might be going out independently. Or reading. Returning to work. Preparing food. Using a phone. Looking after a child. Going shopping without having to organise a full family expedition.

You cannot learn everything by Tuesday.

That is why setting priorities makes sense.

Someone who lives alone and has just lost their sight may first need to master basic movement at home, medication, phone use and food preparation. Someone who needs to return to work quickly may need to focus earlier on technology and travel.

Rehabilitation looks much more like that than like following the contents page of a manual from page one to the very end.

And then there is fear.

It does not show up on an eye chart, but it is there.

A person may be physically capable of walking down a street and still not be ready to do it alone. Until a week ago, visual information told them where the pavement ended, who was approaching or where a doorway was. Now they have to learn to obtain much of that information in other ways.

Telling them “don't be afraid” is not terribly useful.

Confidence usually grows when techniques begin to work and, after a few successful experiences, the person sees that they can actually handle the situation.

Orientation is not simply walking

Orientation and mobility are often mentioned together, but they are not the same thing.

Mobility is moving from place to place.

Orientation is knowing where we are, where the thing we want is, and how one relates to the other.

We can walk beautifully and still be completely lost.

That, incidentally, is hardly exclusive to blind people.

When sight disappears, we have to learn to notice information that was always present but that we did not previously need to analyse so carefully.

The sound of a busy road can become a landmark. So can the change from paving to grass. A slope, the echo in a space, a wall, a distinctive smell, the sound of a particular activity or even our own sense of having made a turn can all provide information.

It is not that the ears suddenly turn into a secret radar system.

That version works rather better in films.

What really happens is that we learn to make better use of the information already available.

And using it means interpreting it.

Hearing traffic on our right does not automatically mean that we should move to the right. We might be walking parallel to the road. We might be approaching it. We might be crossing a square with traffic running along one side.

The sound gives us a clue.

Then we need to know what to do with it.

Useful remaining vision also belongs in the picture. A person can use a cane and vision at the same time without tearing a hole in the fabric of the universe.

They might see a well-lit doorway but not a curb. They might detect strong contrast during the day and have almost no useful visual reference once it gets dark.

The point is not to prove that we can do without whatever vision remains.

If it helps, we use it.

What matters is knowing how far we can trust it in each situation.

When some vision remains: learning to use it better too

Low vision is not simply an incomplete version of blindness. It has its own needs and, very often, its own techniques.

A person may be able to read with magnification yet not recognise a face at a distance. They may travel well during the day and lose almost all visual reference in low light. They may detect movement but not detail. A clinical measurement of vision does not, by itself, tell us how that person uses sight in everyday life.

That is why functional assessment matters: what can the person locate, how long can they sustain a visual task, how are they affected by contrast, size, distance, lighting or glare, and at what point does visual information become unreliable?

Training the use of remaining vision may include learning to search systematically, locate objects, follow a line of text, find a landmark in a large space, or discover which position, distance or lighting makes a task less tiring.

Magnifiers, telescopes, filters, electronic magnification, cameras, screens and accessibility settings may all help. But the device is not the goal. If a well-placed lamp and good contrast solve the problem, we have already won without turning the table into a technology exhibition.

And if a task works better with a non-visual strategy, use it. A cane, speech, Braille, magnification and remaining vision can coexist perfectly well. Mixing tools is not cheating; it is solving the problem properly.

The cane: what it actually does

Now we arrive at the famous white cane.

The first thing to understand is very simple: the cane gets there before we do.

That is a large part of its job.

The tip finds an obstacle, a surface change or a drop before our feet do. It gives us information a little in advance and gives us time to respond.

That is why simply carrying it in one hand is not enough.

It needs to explore the area we are about to move through.

When a person moves the cane from side to side in front of the body, that arc is intended to cover the space needed so that the next steps land on ground that has already been checked.

It is not a decorative movement, nor some ancient tradition passed down so that blind people can all do the same thing in the street.

It has a purpose.

If the arc is too narrow, part of the path may remain unexplored. If enthusiasm takes over and we sweep half the neighbourhood on every stroke, that does not necessarily make us safer either. We can lose precision, tire ourselves out and, as a bonus, cover a surprisingly respectable amount of territory with stray cane swipes.

The important thing is to cover, usefully, the space we are actually going to occupy.

Cane and steps need to work together

In the classic two-point touch technique, walking and cane movement are coordinated.

The idea is that the area into which a foot is about to move has already been explored.

At first, there seems to be far too much to think about.

Cane over here.

Foot over there.

Rhythm.

Posture.

And meanwhile it would be helpful to remember where we were going.

With practice, much of that coordination becomes automatic.

That is exactly what we want.

The aim is to reach a point where the person does not have to think consciously about every centimetre of movement, because their attention is needed for traffic, conversation, orientation or wondering why they chose to leave home just as it started raining.

Two-point touch or constant contact

With two-point touch, the cane tip alternately contacts the ground near the ends of the arc.

With constant contact, the tip stays on the surface as it sweeps rather than lifting from one side to the other.

Why use one rather than the other?

Because the information is different.

Constant contact provides continuous information about changes in texture and can make certain drops easier to detect. On very uneven surfaces it may catch more often and become tiring.

Two-point touch is light and works extremely well in many situations.

We do not need to crown a world champion.

We need to understand what we are trying to detect and what kind of ground is in front of us.

Length matters, but there is no magic number

A person's height is a useful starting reference when choosing a cane, but it does not explain everything.

Stride length, walking speed, technique, reaction time, terrain and personal preference all matter.

A cane that is too short may warn us too late.

A dramatically longer cane does not automatically become better simply because it occupies half the street.

This is where an orientation and mobility professional can be particularly useful. Not because anyone needs a licence to buy a cane, but because watching how someone actually walks tells us far more than measuring them from head to toe and handing over whatever a chart says should fit.

Rigid or folding

A rigid cane transmits what it encounters very directly because the shaft is one continuous piece.

A folding cane has one quality that is wonderfully easy to understand: it folds.

That may sound minor until you need to put it under a table, store it in a vehicle or carry a spare.

Some people prefer the feedback from a rigid cane; others use folding canes all their lives.

There is no need to start a civil war over it.

The right cane is the one that does its job for that person and fits reasonably well into their life.

A wonderful cane that never leaves the cupboard does not detect many curbs.

The tip has something to say too

Cane tips come in smaller and larger sizes, rolling designs and different shapes and materials.

A small tip can give very precise feedback and, at the same time, display an uncanny attraction to every crack in the pavement.

A larger tip may travel more easily over certain surfaces.

Sand, soil, grass, gravel, mud and a smooth pavement are not remotely the same thing.

This is where we begin to see why a global document cannot pretend that everyone lives on the same ground.

In some places, everyday travel means paved streets.

In others, paths and tracks.

And in still others, you may encounter both before reaching the next corner.

Curbs, stairs and obstacles

When the cane tip detects that the ground drops before our foot gets there, it has just given us some of the most valuable information it can provide.

We stop.

We check.

Then we continue as appropriate.

On an upward staircase, the cane will encounter the first step. On a downward staircase, we need it to detect the change before we take the step.

There are specific techniques for going up and down stairs using the cane and a handrail where one is available.

Again, the purpose matters.

The cane must keep giving us information before the body arrives.

Discovering that a step was missing after the foot has already committed is certainly a very fast learning experience, but one we are better off avoiding.

Some things the cane does not detect

A sign at head height.

A branch.

The corner of an open door.

Certain suspended objects.

The cane explores a particular zone.

That matters for anyone watching from the outside and wondering how a person with a cane can still bump into something.

Because we are not surrounded by a force field.

Sadly.

There are protective techniques for particular situations, and experience, knowledge of the environment and some modern electronic aids can add useful information.

But a traditional cane does not wrap its user in an invisible bubble.

Following a route is not memorising a password

In mobility, we use landmarks and reference points.

A wall can lead us to an entrance. A change in the surface can tell us we are near a particular point. The sound of traffic can help us maintain direction.

All of that works.

Until it does not.

The wall ends.

The door is closed.

There are roadworks.

The bus leaves us somewhere we were not expecting.

That is why teaching a route should involve more than a sequence of instructions.

What references do we expect to find?

How will we know when something does not fit?

What can we do if one of those references disappears?

An independent person is not someone who never gets lost.

If we ever find such a person, we should probably study them, because the discovery would interest the whole human race.

What matters is knowing how to stop, check, look for another reference, return to a known point, ask someone or use a navigation tool.

Learning how to recover from an error is part of mobility too.

Helping without taking over

If a blind person appears to need help, ask.

They might need it.

They might not.

Perhaps they only want to know the bus number.

If they accept human guide assistance, normally the blind person takes the guide's arm. That lets them feel changes in direction, stops, narrow spaces and other movements.

Grabbing someone by the shoulders and pushing them from behind provides far less information and creates the slightly curious sensation of being abducted in broad daylight.

It is also a good idea not to grab the cane.

Do not move it.

And please do not step on it to indicate where something is.

If you need to warn someone, say what is happening.

And while we are here, allow me to continue a campaign I have been losing for years:

“Watch out!” is not a description.

Watch out for what?

A step?

A bicycle?

A door?

A piano falling from a window?

“There is a branch at head height” is considerably more useful.

Back home, there is work to do there as well

Knowing how to cross a street is excellent.

But afterwards you still need breakfast.

You need to get dressed.

Take medication.

Find the keys.

Prepare food.

Clean.

Pay for things.

And locate whatever somebody decided to put away “somewhere better”.

Activities of daily living are often overshadowed by the cane because they are less dramatic. Yet a huge part of independence is built right there.

A person who has just lost their sight has not forgotten how their kitchen works.

What has changed is that part of the information they used to manage it no longer arrives visually.

The system has to be rebuilt.

Organisation starts to become a map

If the keys always have a known place, we do not need to search for them visually.

If products follow a clear order, we greatly reduce the number of checks we need to make.

If medication is clearly identified, we avoid playing a lottery that is not remotely amusing in this case.

That does not mean freezing the house in time.

Things can move.

But when an important object has an established place and someone moves it, it helps to say so.

A sighted person may take a few seconds to spot the sugar after it has migrated to another shelf.

Someone using a non-visual system may have to inspect half the cupboard.

That is not an obsession with tidiness.

The order itself carried information.

We also need to cope when the system breaks

Memorising where everything lives is not enough either.

Life changes.

We buy a different brand. We move house. Someone uses something and leaves it somewhere else.

So it is useful to learn ways to classify, label and verify.

A rubber band around a container.

A raised mark.

Braille.

Electronic labels.

Recognition with a phone.

Different containers.

There is no prize for choosing the most technological option.

If a rubber band solves the problem perfectly, wonderful.

But if we are distinguishing between similar medicines, it may not be wise to rely on remembering six months later exactly what each rubber band was supposed to mean.

The kitchen

Families often worry about this one.

And, to be fair, they have some reason to.

There are knives, flames, boiling water, hot oil and several other things that deserve respect.

That does not mean a blind person cannot cook.

It means we use methods.

When cutting, we need to know where the food is, where the fingers are and where the blade will travel. Hand position should protect the fingers while providing tactile reference.

We are not trying to prove bravery.

A knife cuts just as effectively no matter how determined we feel.

Pouring liquids

How do we know how much is in a glass?

There are several possibilities.

The sound changes as the container fills.

An electronic liquid-level indicator can be used.

Known container sizes and measurements can help.

With suitable containers and cold liquids, some people use tactile reference on the outside of the glass. This is not a technique for every situation or every liquid.

The right method depends on the situation.

Putting a finger into a freshly made cup of coffee will also provide information, but probably not the information we were hoping for.

Heat and appliances

Keeping the work area clear, positioning handles safely and returning utensils to known places prevents a fair number of problems.

Physical controls can be marked.

Fully touch-screen appliances are a different story.

If setting the oven to a particular temperature requires someone else to come over and read the display, perhaps the problem is not that the blind person needs better rehabilitation.

Sometimes the appliance could do with a little rehabilitation of its own.

If useful vision remains, make it easier to use

Good lighting.

Contrast.

Reducing glare.

Changing the colour of a surface.

Enlarging a label.

Positioning a lamp better.

Sometimes a small change provides more practical benefit than a device costing several hundred euros or dollars.

First we look at the need.

Then, if necessary, we look at the catalogue.

Finding, cleaning, checking

When visual checking is no longer available, doing tasks systematically becomes more important.

A table can be cleaned using an organised pattern that covers the whole surface.

The same applies when looking for something that has fallen.

If we wave a hand randomly over the same area twenty times, we can spend a very pleasant afternoon finding absolutely nothing.

We need to search methodically.

And before bending down, it is worth checking what is in front of us. Table corners have spent decades proving that they know how to wait.

Shopping and handling money

Here techniques begin to vary considerably from one country to another.

Some coins and banknotes are easy to distinguish by touch; others are rather less cooperative.

People may use an organised wallet, templates, apps, permitted markings or other methods.

Shopping varies just as much.

In one place, a supermarket may provide assistance.

Elsewhere, accessible apps may help.

Someone may use a small local shop whose layout they know well.

In another setting, asking at a market may remain the simplest and most practical solution.

Independence does not require doing everything without speaking to another human being.

If I need someone to tell me where the cereal is, that does not mean they need to choose my cereal for me.

Medication: this is not the place for experiments

Identification needs to be reliable enough.

Braille.

Large print.

Audio labels.

Organisers.

Accessible systems provided by a pharmacy.

Apps as a supporting tool.

Whatever is available in that particular place.

If we are not sure which medicine is in front of us, we check.

Independence also means knowing when being heroic is a very poor idea.

Family and the famous “I'll do it for you”

That sentence is often full of love.

It can also end up creating a surprising amount of dependence.

The person takes a long time.

The family does it instead.

Tomorrow the person is still slow.

The family does it again.

Months later, they are still slow because they have barely had a chance to practise.

This is not about leaving someone alone in a dangerous situation so that they can “learn”.

Nor is it about rushing in to solve everything before they have had the chance to try.

There is a middle ground.

At first, a great deal of support may be needed.

Later, less.

And for some tasks, eventually none at all.

Good help needs to know when to step in and when to step back out.

Sight loss does not happen only to the hands and feet

There is another part that no technique can fix on its own.

A person may have to stop driving.

Change profession.

Depend on other people for a while.

Wonder what will happen to their children, partner, home or job.

There is no single correct reaction.

Some people start looking for solutions immediately; others need more time.

Good days.

Bad days.

Days when three new things go well and we feel delighted with ourselves.

And another day when an impossible food packet makes us question forty years of good manners.

That, too, falls within the range of normal human experience.

Grief without turning life into a funeral

There are real losses.

Pretending otherwise would be absurd.

Someone may miss driving.

Reading visually.

Recognising a face.

Seeing a landscape.

Rehabilitation does not require declaring that none of this matters.

But it does not mean that loss has to occupy every room in life forever either.

A person can mourn something they can no longer do and, at exactly the same time, take enormous pleasure in something they have regained.

There is no contradiction in that.

Fear does not disappear because of speeches

Confidence usually grows when we discover that techniques actually work.

First one simple route.

Then another.

One day something unexpected happens.

We solve it.

That is worth far more than hearing “you can do it” twenty times.

And there will be setbacks.

A fall can bring fear back.

A new problem can make us doubt ourselves again.

That does not mean we are starting from zero.

When blindness also changes the way people look at us

Someone starts speaking to the person beside us.

“What would they like to drink?”

I have no idea.

Ask me. I am still here.

Someone else raises their voice.

Another person congratulates us for knowing how to use a fork.

From the outside these things can look trivial, but repeated often enough they become exhausting.

Part of adapting is also learning how to correct other people without having to deliver a lecture every single time.

And there will be days when we do not feel like correcting anyone at all.

That is allowed too.

Partners, intimacy and family

If we are serious about whole-person rehabilitation, we cannot pretend this part does not exist.

Sight loss can change the way someone sees themselves.

It can alter the dynamics of a relationship.

Sometimes a partner gradually becomes a full-time carer.

That deserves attention, because it can change the relationship far more than either person intended.

Not everyone will need professional support.

But anyone who does should be able to access it without feeling that they are asking something strange or inappropriate.

Regaining access to information

Technology has changed the landscape dramatically here.

A person can listen to a message, use a refreshable Braille display, photograph a document and recognise the text, or ask artificial intelligence what appears in an image.

Not so many years ago, some of these things necessarily depended on another person.

That matters a great deal.

Because accessing information independently is not just a matter of convenience.

It is also privacy.

Braille

Let us begin by rescuing Braille from its own funeral.

No, Braille did not disappear when phones learned to talk.

Nor does every blind person have to read five-hundred-page novels with their fingers.

It is a reading and writing system.

It can be used intensively for study and work, or much more functionally to identify medication, label folders, jot down a note or distinguish objects.

Age alone should not decide whether someone can learn it.

An older person may have reduced tactile sensitivity and find Braille unsuitable.

Another person of the same age may learn enough Braille to solve exactly the problems that matter to them.

It is worth trying before we deliver the verdict.

Learning Braille takes time

At first, dots are simply dots.

Then they begin to become letters.

Later, with enough practice, they become words recognised without counting every raised point.

One common beginner habit is pressing the fingers down hard, as though the character will confess under interrogation.

It will not.

Tactile reading also requires sensitivity, movement and practice.

Refreshable Braille

Refreshable Braille displays use moving pins to show text from a phone or computer.

They can provide access to messages, documents, web pages and much more.

For some kinds of work or study, they are extraordinarily useful.

They also cost money.

Sometimes a great deal of it.

So we will not write about them as though every newly blind person is handed one on the way out of the clinic.

In one part of the world they may be a realistic option.

In another, they may not be.

Screen readers

The first time someone turns on a screen reader, it can feel as though the phone has suddenly decided to comment on absolutely everything.

Button.

Heading.

Link.

Selected.

Not selected.

Text field.

And you think it is very nice that everyone has introduced themselves, but now you need to learn who is who.

With training, all that information begins to acquire structure.

We learn which element has focus and how to move, activate, type and review.

The same thing happens on a computer.

Commands and gestures are not there specifically to make blind people's lives difficult, although there are days when one might reasonably suspect otherwise.

They replace actions that a sighted person performs by looking and pointing.

Sometimes the problem is the application

Families, teachers and colleagues need to understand this too.

If a blind person says they cannot use something, it may be because they have not yet learned how.

And it may be because the thing is badly designed.

An unnamed button.

A window the screen reader cannot enter.

Information that exists only as an image.

No matter how many courses the user takes, an inaccessible application does not become accessible through sheer persistence.

Audio, OCR and artificial intelligence

Audio provides quick access to books and documents.

OCR turns photographs into text and can unlock a huge amount of printed information.

Artificial intelligence adds another layer: we can ask questions about images, objects, documents and scenes.

It is remarkable.

It also gets things wrong.

Both ideas need to fit inside our heads at the same time.

If we only want to know which shirt seems to be blue, an occasional mistake is unlikely to be disastrous.

If we are checking a medication dose or making a safety decision, we need a much higher level of reliability.

Modern technology training should teach people how to use these tools and also when not to trust them without verification.

That is no small thing.

Studying and returning to work

Sooner or later, the question appears:

“Will I be able to do what I did before?”

Sometimes, yes.

Sometimes, yes, but in a different way.

And there will be specific activities that genuinely cannot continue without sight.

There is nothing wrong with saying so.

What matters is not turning one real limitation into twenty imaginary ones.

Analyse the tasks, not the job title

Before deciding that someone can no longer work, find out what they actually do.

Read documents.

Work with people.

Write reports.

Use an application.

Move around a building.

Operate machinery.

Teach.

Each activity can be considered separately.

Some may need no change at all.

Others may be solved through accessibility or assistive technology.

A particular task may not be adaptable.

Then we make decisions based on information, which generally works rather better than looking at the cane and drawing conclusions.

An accommodation does not do the job for you

A screen reader, an accessible format or a reorganisation of tasks does not write the report for us.

It gives us access to the work.

After that, the report can be excellent or terrible, exactly like anyone else's.

Which is strangely reassuring.

School and university

A PDF does not become accessible simply because its file name ends in PDF.

It may be a beautifully structured document or a photograph of forty pages.

A graph needs an accessible representation.

An equation may require appropriate mathematical notation.

A map will need another solution.

The goal is not to hand everyone exactly the same object.

The goal is to provide access to the same content.

No favours out of pity, no barriers by design

A blind student still has to demonstrate what they know.

But if the examination itself is inaccessible, we are measuring something else.

Adjustments should remove the barrier, not answer the questions for the student.

That sounds like a fairly simple distinction.

In practice, it can be surprisingly difficult to maintain.

Informal work, agriculture and other realities

When we talk about employment, we cannot always picture an office with a human resources department.

In many parts of the world, work is informal.

Agriculture.

Trade.

Craft work.

Family businesses.

Vocational rehabilitation may mean modifying tools or redistributing tasks within work that already existed.

A study that understands only office jobs leaves out a very large part of the world.

The guide dog

It is not a GPS with fur.

My apologies to anyone hoping for a more spectacular explanation.

The person still needs to orient themselves and decide where they want to go.

A guide dog can maintain a line of travel, avoid obstacles, locate certain features and stop at important changes in the environment.

Through the harness, the handler feels the dog's movements and learns to interpret them.

It is another form of mobility and it requires training.

And it can disobey

If a command would take the team into immediate danger, a properly trained guide dog may refuse it.

In that situation, disobedience is exactly what good work looks like.

That does not mean the dog is infallible.

It is still an animal.

It can become distracted, make mistakes or encounter a situation it does not understand.

The handler still has their own responsibilities for orientation and decision-making.

It does not read traffic lights by colour

The person assesses the crossing and decides when to begin moving.

The dog contributes to safety.

It does not look at a green light and think, “Right, off we go.”

This myth has extraordinary survival skills and will probably outlive this document, but we can at least make the attempt.

And it is not for everyone

A guide dog needs care, food, veterinary attention, exercise and daily life as part of a team.

Some people love this way of travelling; others would not want a dog even as a gift.

Both decisions are completely respectable.

GPS and electronic aids

A GPS can give us information about a route.

Where we are.

Which streets are nearby.

Where to turn.

What businesses or places are around us.

But it does not necessarily know that there is a box, a parked motorbike or a hole directly in front of us.

It works at a different scale.

GPS helps with “where am I and where am I going?”

The cane or guide dog deals much more directly with “what is happening right in front of me now?”

Together, they can complement each other very well.

Smart canes and other sensors

Systems have been developed using cameras, ultrasound, depth sensors, vibration and audio.

Their aim is to detect things a traditional cane may miss, especially obstacles above ground level.

That sounds excellent.

Then we need to ask how all that information is communicated.

While we are walking, the GPS may be talking, traffic may be roaring past, the device may be vibrating, a bus may arrive and the watch may decide that this is the perfect moment to remind us to stand up and move.

More information does not always make life simpler.

Useful technology also needs to know when to keep quiet.

When there are other disabilities as well

So far, our imaginary blind person has been in fairly good health.

Real life is not always so accommodating.

Hearing loss.

Diabetes.

Neuropathy.

Arthritis.

Parkinson's disease.

Balance problems.

Brain injury.

Cognitive impairment.

All of these can change the techniques we use. The real human body has rarely read the manual.

Deafblindness

Many strategies used by blind people rely on hearing.

Many strategies used by deaf people rely on vision.

When both channels are limited, the situation changes profoundly.

Before teaching anything, we first need to establish effective communication with the person.

That may involve Braille, tactile systems, tactile or supported sign communication, or other methods depending on the person's history, abilities and usual way of communicating.

If the instruction is not reaching the person properly, it is hardly fair to judge later how much they have learned.

Age and pace

An older person may need more time.

They may walk slowly.

That may be exactly the right speed.

Speed is not the goal.

Arriving safely is.

And “they are too old” should not replace an assessment.

A particular skill may genuinely not be worth pursuing.

Or it may simply need to be taught differently.

First, we find out.

Mobility difficulties

Some blind people use walkers, crutches or wheelchairs.

They need orientation too.

The techniques change; the needs do not disappear.

The same applies to neuropathy that reduces sensation in the fingers. If a small tactile mark no longer works, we look for another cue.

A technique exists to fulfil a function.

The body does not exist to obey the technique.

Children need a different approach

A child who has never seen is not trying to recover an earlier way of doing things.

They are developing those skills from the beginning.

Spatial concepts, movement, exploration, Braille where appropriate, technology, play and independence all matter.

Sighted children learn an enormous amount simply by watching what happens around them.

A blind child needs other opportunities to build those same concepts.

And that child needs to move.

Protecting them so thoroughly that they can hardly explore may keep them very safe in childhood and leave them badly prepared for what comes next.

Where we live changes the rehabilitation process

This is one of the ideas I wanted to run through the whole study rather than appear as a footnote at the end.

Having a service twenty minutes away is not the same as having one four hundred kilometres away.

Even if the statistics say that both people live somewhere where “rehabilitation is available”.

Attending may require paying for transport and accommodation, taking time off work or finding someone to care for children.

The service exists.

Getting to it is another matter.

Cities do not represent an entire country

Specialists may be concentrated in one capital or major city, with almost none in rural areas.

Funding may differ between regions.

There may be waiting lists.

Public services in one place and private services in another.

So we cannot colour a map according to national income and declare the problem solved.

Nor should we talk about Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas or Oceania as though each continent were one single rehabilitation system.

They are not.

We need to look at context, not just at the colour of a map.

Community-based rehabilitation

Where there is no extensive network of specialists, some training can be brought closer through local workers, mobile services, community organisations, relatives who have received guidance and support from other blind people.

That does not mean anyone becomes a specialist instructor after watching three videos online.

Some situations require professional expertise.

The point is different: solve locally what can be solved well locally, and have a route for referral when more specialised knowledge is needed.

Low-cost can be excellent

A simple tactile mark.

A rubber band.

A different container.

A cane without electronics.

Good organisation.

These can solve a great deal.

Not everything needs an app attached to it.

But we should not confuse low-cost solutions with accepting poor solutions simply because someone lives in a place with fewer resources.

A bad cane is still a bad cane.

An unsafe technique is still unsafe.

Limited money means finding sustainable solutions, not lowering the dignity or quality of the service.

Technology that cannot be maintained

Before handing over a device anywhere in the world, it is worth asking a few deeply unglamorous questions.

Can it be charged?

Is there connectivity?

Can it be repaired?

Are spare parts available?

Does it work in the person's language?

Is there someone who can teach them to use it?

These questions are much less photogenic than posing for a picture at the handover, but they are probably more useful six months later.

Poverty multiplies the problems

Poverty is not a disability, but it can magnify almost every consequence of disability.

More travel.

More devices.

More training.

And, precisely where all of that is needed, fewer resources.

That is why the ideal solution and the possible solution do not always match.

A serious study needs to explain both without pretending they are equivalent.

Emergencies and conflict

A blind person can lose their home, familiar routes, devices and reference points overnight.

Someone who has been independent for years may suddenly need support again.

They have not forgotten how to orient themselves.

The world they knew has disappeared.

In those circumstances, a spare cane, a hearing aid, a pair of glasses, a charger or accessible information can be enormously important, even if none of those is the first image that comes to mind when we think about humanitarian assistance.

Rehabilitation does not follow a perfect timetable

At first, safety and immediate needs often come first.

Then more complex skills are added.

Over time, everything begins to mix together.

Going alone to a medical appointment may require mobility, phone use, transport, documents, communication and the ability to deal with a last-minute change.

We are no longer doing exercises.

We are living.

That is the interesting part.

Practice

We need it.

A skill used once a week during a lesson will take longer to become natural.

If we already know how to prepare a drink, we need to do it.

If we have learned a simple route and it is safe, we need to use it.

But early on, we also need enough review to avoid practising a misunderstood technique for months.

Practising the wrong thing very diligently has the unfortunate habit of making us extremely good at doing the wrong thing.

Making mistakes

We need that too.

Within safe limits, naturally.

If an instructor corrects every deviation before the learner has even noticed it, the learner picks up one very specific lesson: someone will always be behind them correcting things.

Then Thursday arrives, there is no instructor and the door is not where it was supposed to be.

We need to learn to recognise when something does not fit and work out the situation again.

Things bad rehabilitation can be surprisingly good at

Creating dependence.

There are several excellent ways to achieve that.

Make decisions for the person.

Solve every difficulty before they have a chance to try.

Hand out devices nobody asked for.

Never explain why a technique is being used.

Constantly correct details that make no functional difference.

Use fear in the name of teaching safety.

Always speak to the relative instead of the person.

Treat every difficulty as a bad attitude.

Or keep the person in courses forever because there is always one more thing they could learn a little better.

Rehabilitation should not manufacture the perfect student.

It should help create the day when that student no longer needs us for most of these things.

Technology: neither saviour nor enemy

Sometimes we fall in love with whatever is new.

A new app, a pair of glasses or a device appears and, for about twenty minutes, it feels as though blindness has just been solved.

Then we use it in real life.

The excitement usually settles down a little.

There is an opposite extreme too:

“I've managed for thirty years without that.”

Excellent.

Someone else may still need it.

Age does not automatically make a technique better, and novelty does not automatically make one better either.

We ask what problem it solves.

It is not very glamorous, but it produces better decisions.

What a good service should provide

We do not need to begin with an enormous building or an endless collection of professionals.

We need to listen.

Assess properly.

Explain.

Set goals with the person.

Teach safe techniques.

Let people try products where possible.

Teach them how to use those products.

Follow up on what actually happened afterwards.

And know when to refer someone because a need falls outside the service's expertise.

A good service should also respect privacy, speak directly to the user and allow them to say that a recommendation is not working for them.

That last point is particularly healthy.

The professional can be wrong.

So can the user.

We try something else and the planet keeps turning.

A good specialist also knows how to say “I don't know”

I find that sentence rather reassuring.

Especially compared with an invented answer delivered with tremendous confidence.

Someone may know a great deal about orientation and mobility and very little about a particular technology.

Another professional may know Braille inside out and have limited experience of deafblindness.

Finding the person who does know is part of doing the job properly.

Coordination stops the user becoming the messenger

When several professionals are involved, they should share the information they genuinely need.

There is little sense in one professional being unaware of a balance problem another already knows about, or in making the person spend their life saying:

“The mobility instructor told me to tell you...”

There is usually enough paperwork already.

Success is not measured only by counting sessions

Twenty hours of rehabilitation tells us how much service someone received.

It does not tell us what they achieved.

We need to know whether they can now travel more effectively, manage medication, use a phone, return to study, regain activities or depend less on help they do not actually need.

Handing out fifty canes looks excellent in an annual report.

Discovering that forty-eight of them are still sitting in cupboards is less impressive, but perhaps that is the figure we ought to be looking at.

The future is already here, although it has arrived rather untidily

Artificial intelligence, camera-equipped glasses, sensors, accessible maps, indoor navigation, advanced refreshable Braille, telerehabilitation.

A lot is moving at once, each technology at its own pace.

Some of it already works well.

Some is beginning to mature.

And some looks absolutely wonderful in a demonstration where, by extraordinary coincidence, nobody has poor connectivity and the device has not chosen that exact moment to install an update.

Artificial intelligence can provide visual information in a different way

There is no need to repeat everything we have already said about AI. The interesting shift is that we can now have a conversation about an image or a scene: ask a question, follow it up, request attention to one detail or change the goal without starting from the beginning.

The potential is enormous. So is the need to understand when an answer is good enough and when it must be checked.

Smart glasses

Putting a camera at eye level can keep the hands free and reduce the need to take out a phone constantly.

These systems can help with reading, describing and identifying.

But battery life, cost, comfort, privacy and accuracy all matter.

A camera in front of our eyes does not automatically turn everything it sees into reliable information.

Sensors and navigation

Systems can detect more and more things.

The challenge is deciding which of those things they should tell us about.

Technology that announces absolutely everything it detects can become a remarkably irritating companion.

Mobility already gives us plenty of information to process.

The future will probably require machines that are better at detecting obstacles and equally better at ignoring what does not matter at that moment.

Telerehabilitation

This may be much less spectacular and far more useful on a global scale.

A specialist working at a distance can help with technology, organisation, follow-up and some areas of training.

Not everything can be taught through a video call.

Some mobility and safety situations require someone to be physically present.

But a hybrid model can bring expertise to places it previously could not reach at all.

That may change more lives than a very attractive pair of glasses unveiled at a trade show.

The biggest innovation still needed does not require a battery

Making rehabilitation actually reach people.

We already know how to do many things.

The problem is that too many people still do not have access to what we already know.

We can invent an excellent sensor for detecting branches.

Wonderful.

At the same time, someone else may not even have access to a suitable basic cane.

Both things can be true.

The future has to move on both fronts: better tools and better ways of delivering the tools and knowledge that already work.

And after all of that, we return to the beginning

A person loses their sight.

For a while, blindness can occupy almost everything.

The first trip outside.

The kitchen.

The phone.

Work.

Every task demands conscious thought about something that used to happen automatically.

Then methods begin to appear.

Some take longer.

Others click almost immediately.

Some things will never be done exactly as they were before, while others become so ordinary that, a few months later, we barely remember how difficult they felt at the beginning.

The cane ends up beside the door.

Not as a symbol of tragedy and not as a trophy of triumph.

It is there because we need it when we go out.

The screen reader stops being “that technology for blind people” we had to learn and becomes simply the way we use the phone.

Organising the kitchen stops being a rehabilitation technique.

It is our kitchen, and we know where things are. Assuming nobody has decided to be helpful by reorganising it, of course.

We may use Braille.

We may use a guide dog.

A magnifier.

An app.

Artificial intelligence.

Or a bit of everything, which is often what happens after enough years of solving real problems and no longer worrying which philosophical school each tool belongs to.

There will still be bad days.

An inaccessible web page.

Roadworks that were not there yesterday.

An app that worked perfectly until somebody had the brilliant idea of improving it.

A stranger who grabs us in the street without asking.

Those things do not disappear either.

But rehabilitation will have done its job when they stop constantly deciding what we can and cannot do with our lives.

Because the goal was never to manufacture the perfect blind person.

Nor a person who never needs help.

Nor one of those inspirational success stories who, before breakfast, have already climbed a mountain, founded two companies and delivered a motivational speech.

If we can get up, make our plans, do whatever the day requires and spend at least part of our time dealing with completely ordinary problems, we are doing fairly well.

At the beginning, someone may ask:

“How am I supposed to live my life now?”

Later, they may no longer have time to ask the question.

They are running late.

Something is cooking.

The phone is ringing.

They are meeting someone.

Or they simply want to sit quietly and have nobody explain another rehabilitation technique to them for the rest of the day.

And that is probably when we are quite close to where we wanted to be: rehabilitation is no longer the main subject, and life is.

A big hug.
