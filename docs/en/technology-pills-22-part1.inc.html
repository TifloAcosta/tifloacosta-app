First main story: there is no longer such a thing as “the Meta glasses”

There was a time when this was simple. You said “the Ray-Ban Meta glasses” and that was that: a pair of glasses, a camera, some microphones, speakers and an artificial intelligence that was gradually learning how to make use of all of it. That time is over. Meta and EssilorLuxottica have decided that, now that we had finally learned to tell generations apart, we could entertain ourselves with families, frames, sports versions, optical models, glasses with a display, glasses without a camera and the odd additional creature still warming up on the touchline.

So the cupboard needs sorting, and from a slightly different point of view. A blind person may care whether a pair of glasses looks good, of course. Nobody has declared war on elegance. But if I am going to spend a respectable amount of money to carry a camera, several microphones, speakers and an artificial intelligence on the bridge of my nose, I would quite like all that to do something more than match my shirt. What I want to know is what each family does, how they differ, when upgrading makes sense if we already own an older model, and what those of us who use the glasses as a practical extension of the phone—and sometimes as help interpreting what is in front of us—can actually get from them.

From Stories to glasses that can tell us what they are looking at

Ray-Ban Stories arrived in 2021 with two 5-megapixel cameras, three microphones and open-ear speakers. They could take photos, record short videos, play music and handle calls without putting earbuds in our ears, which was not a small thing at the time. But if you could not see, they left one fairly obvious problem behind: the camera could look for us, but it could not tell us anything about what it was looking at. We could record the door—wonderful—and still have no idea whether the thing in front of us actually was the door. As a first experiment they were fine; as an everyday tool for blind people, not yet.

The jump came in 2023 with Ray-Ban Meta. The Stories name disappeared, the camera, sound and microphones improved and, above all, Meta AI began to be integrated. That changed what the product was for. The camera was no longer there only to take pictures; it could become part of a conversation: we could ask what was in front of us, request information about an object, ask about visible text or put questions about what the camera was seeing. No magic tricks here, of course. AI makes mistakes, can misread an image and should not be the only source when something important depends on the answer. But there is a substantial difference between a camera that takes a photograph and one that also tries to explain what it sees. That is where much of the interest among blind users began.

Gen 1 and Gen 2: battery life changed everything

When we talk about Ray-Ban Meta Gen 1 today, we mean the 2023 generation, not Stories. It established the formula we have since watched evolve: a 12-megapixel camera, five microphones, open-ear speakers, touch controls, voice commands, a charging case and Meta AI. Battery life was around four hours with moderate use. On a specifications page that can look like a minor number, right up until you leave home, because four hours disappear quickly. If we make heavy use of Meta AI, take calls, listen to audio or use the camera, battery life stops being a figure on the manufacturer's website and starts organising our day for us.

Even so, Gen 1 still does almost everything that made these glasses popular, so a new or refurbished pair at a very good price should not be dismissed automatically. The important thing is to know what you are buying. Someone entering this world for the first time who sees only a small price difference to a later generation has good reason to look further up the range. Someone who already owns Gen 1 can ask whether the improvement in battery life, camera and comfort justifies the move. Not because the older glasses have stopped being useful, but because quite a lot has happened since 2023, which in technology is roughly equivalent to two marriages, three house moves and a kitchen renovation.

The second generation lifted battery life to around eight hours of moderate use, roughly twice as much, and for anyone who wants the glasses on for much of the day that matters far more than some of the shinier additions in the advertising. Video also moved up to 3K, while the basic platform remained the same: camera, open-ear audio, microphones, touch controls and Meta AI. This is where many of the familiar frames come in: Wayfarer, Headliner and Skyler. Nobody should go looking for three different brains, because the core technology is shared; what changes is the shape, fit and comfort. Wayfarer remains the most recognisable look, Headliner changes the design and may suit other face shapes better, while Skyler offers another option for fit and style.

I would not send a blind person with no particular interest in fashion on a search for which of these “does more”, because that is not where the difference lies. I would pay attention to which one sits properly, which is most comfortable and which leaves the camera in a natural position when we move our head. If we are going to wear them for five, six or eight hours, a frame that does not fit is excellent technology that will be sitting on the table by mid-morning.

Optics: the everyday pair

Within Gen 2, Blayzer Optics and Scriber Optics have gained prominence. They are designed for everyday optical use and for people who need prescription lenses. They include adjustable temple tips, interchangeable nose pads and a design intended for long hours of wear, with battery life slightly beyond eight hours. A totally blind person may not care about the prescription side of things, although plenty of users with residual vision certainly do. What matters to all of us is the concept: glasses designed to stay on for most of the day, rather than something we put on only to take a picture or go for a walk. The closer these devices get to behaving like ordinary glasses, the less attention we have to give them, and the device that asks the least from us is often the better one.

Gen 3, and whether Gen 2 owners should jump

In September 2026 Meta introduced the third generation, and it is worth distinguishing between “announced” and “available at the optician around the corner tomorrow”. Availability will expand by market and model, so before taking the card out it is worth checking what is actually sold in each market. Gen 3 keeps the 12-megapixel ultra-wide camera and video up to 3K, makes the camera-and-LED assembly less conspicuous, slims the arms a little and improves battery life, with more than eight hours and up to roughly nine depending on configuration and use.

It also brings one detail that interests me in particular: an assignable action button. When you cannot see, the fewer unnecessary trips around an interface, the better. Being able to dedicate a physical button to a function we use constantly is more valuable than it may sound on paper. If Meta keeps expanding the actions that can be assigned to it, this could become one of those small details we end up using without thinking. The designs expand too: alongside Wayfarer come Aviator and Zena. Once again, these are different ways of putting essentially the same technology on different faces, which sounds less dramatic than talking about artificial intelligence but matters just as much.

Is it worth moving from Gen 2 to Gen 3? That will be the question for people who already own recent Ray-Ban Meta glasses, and there is no single answer. If Gen 2 works well, the battery covers the day and we are comfortable with it, there is no mysterious obligation to retire it because a younger sister has arrived. Gen 3 improves battery life, design and controls, but it does not turn Gen 2 into a museum piece. If, on the other hand, we use the glasses constantly, routinely scrape the bottom of the battery, miss a quicker physical route to certain functions or are thinking of buying a second pair in a different frame, then Gen 3 starts to make more sense. The upgrade may be worthwhile without being urgent, and what a relief it is to be able to say that about technology occasionally.

Meta Glasses: Ray-Ban is no longer the only surname

Another piece arrived in June 2026: Meta and EssilorLuxottica launched a collection simply called Meta Glasses. It began with 26 styles and has continued to grow, and the company expects more than a hundred AI-glasses options across Ray-Ban, Oakley and Meta Glasses by the end of the year. That says quite a lot about where this is going: this is no longer one product, it is a category. Meta Glasses share much of the familiar philosophy—camera, open-ear audio, AI and different frames—and they are sold in a growing list of markets. During September they reached places such as Mexico, Singapore and South Korea, with Brazil and the United Arab Emirates among those announced next. Capri, Nova, Adventurer and Fury have appeared in the collection, alongside collaborations and special colours. Adventurer is particularly interesting because Meta positions it as a less expensive option, which may suit somebody who wants to enter the ecosystem without beginning with the most expensive pair in the window.

The warning I am going to repeat several times in this section applies to the whole family: it is not enough for the glasses to be sold in your country. You also need to check that Meta AI works there and, more importantly, that the particular feature you want is enabled in your language and region. A box can travel far faster than a software feature.

Oakley: HSTN and Vanguard

Oakley Meta HSTN is the more sports-oriented option for people who want a different design: a camera with 3K video, open-ear audio, Meta AI, IPX4 resistance and up to eight hours of typical use. They may appeal to blind people who are physically active, not because Meta AI becomes cleverer under a different logo—it does not—but because of the frame, fit and the use they were designed for. Someone who walks a great deal, trains, exercises outdoors or needs a different kind of grip may find them more suitable than a traditional Ray-Ban frame.

Oakley Meta Vanguard pushes that idea much further because it is designed for more intensive sport. The camera is centred, with a 122-degree field of view; it records 3K video with stabilisation, adds modes such as slow motion and Hyperlapse and reaches around nine hours of mixed use. Here there can be a practical difference for some blind users: a centred camera may provide a more natural perspective in certain situations because it sits closer to the direction in which we point our head. It will not interpret the world better by magic, but it changes the point from which the scene is captured. If our main uses are walking, checking objects and dealing with everyday tasks, Vanguard is probably more hardware than we need. If we also run, hike or want a frame designed for movement, then we are talking about something else.

Ray-Ban Meta Audio: read the small print in the name

At Connect 2026, Meta introduced Ray-Ban Meta Audio in Clubmaster and Burbank styles. They are thinner and lighter, with open-ear audio, calls, access to AI and up to around twelve hours of battery life. Twelve hours—lovely—until we remember why they last so long: they do not have a camera. For a blind person, that is not exactly a footnote. If we want music, podcasts, calls, messages and a voice assistant built into a lightweight pair of glasses, they could be very attractive. If we want to ask what is in front of us, show an object to the AI, read something with the camera or use services that depend on remote vision, this is not the model. Very good glasses for the ears; for borrowed eyes, we need another shelf.

Meta Ray-Ban Display: a lot of technology, but how much of it is for us?

Meta Ray-Ban Display plays in a different league. It puts a screen in the lens and comes with the Meta Neural Band, a wristband that interprets certain movements and muscle signals to control the interface. It adds visual navigation, notifications, overlaid information, apps and other features that depend on that display. For somebody with usable vision it could be fascinating. For somebody who cannot see at all, there is a simple calculation to make: how much of what I am paying for depends on a screen I will not perceive?

That does not mean it has nothing to offer us. The camera, audio, AI and additional controls are still there, and the Neural Band may have possibilities of its own. But more technology does not automatically mean more usefulness to me. At the moment, Display is available in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, with expansion to France, Italy and Germany planned for October 2026. Other markets still have to wait. Some functions also remain limited to certain languages and regions, so before turning these into an object of desire it is worth finding out which parts of the experience we could actually use.

Be My Eyes, when AI is not enough

For a blind person, the integration that catches my attention most in all of this is Be My Eyes. Compatible glasses can start a hands-free voice connection with volunteers. During 2026, that expanded to include trusted contacts, family members and some company support services as well. It changes the whole flow: we can use AI first for a quick question and, if we need human confirmation, move to a person without taking out the phone, opening an app, hunting for buttons and then trying to hold the camera at a sensible angle. The glasses are already in place, the other person sees what is in front of us and our hands remain free. That is useful when we are carrying a cane or working with a guide dog, loaded down with shopping, or simply unwilling to set up a portable office in the middle of the street.

Be My Eyes on Meta glasses is already available in a broad list of countries, including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, much of Europe, India, Brazil, Mexico and the United Arab Emirates. There is one qualification: the command that starts Be My Eyes goes through Meta AI. So it is not enough for Be My Eyes itself to be available in the country; the language configured in Meta AI also has to support that command. If it does not, the glasses should tell us.

Languages and regions: the small print

I have written this section mainly with Spanish- and English-speaking users in mind. Both languages have a strong presence in the Meta ecosystem, but that does not mean every feature arrives in both at the same time. Live translation on Meta Glasses currently supports twenty languages, including Spanish and English, as well as French, Italian, German, Portuguese, Arabic, Chinese, Dutch, Finnish, Greek, Hindi, Indonesian, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Swedish, Thai, Turkish and Vietnamese. But translation is one thing and Meta AI features are another: some tools launch first in English, others begin in the United States or Canada and later spread to Europe, Latin America or Asia, and some features can spend months crossing that invisible line between “announced” and “available to me”.

Meta has also been adding accessibility settings, and one of the most useful allows more detailed visual responses. If we ask what is in front of us, a blind person may need more than “there is a room”: how it is laid out, what objects are there, where they are and what details help us understand the scene. It began rolling out in a few markets and has continued to expand, but the same warning applies again, because two physically identical pairs of glasses can provide different experiences depending on the account, language and country.

So if somebody is considering buying the glasses for one specific purpose, my advice is to check that exact feature, not “Meta AI” or “the glasses”. If they want Be My Eyes, check Be My Eyes. If they want Spanish-English translation, check that language pair. If they want a certain kind of visual description, check whether it has rolled out in their region and language. Five minutes spent checking can save a lot of explanations later, once the card statement is already sitting in front of us.

So, which ones should I buy?

Perhaps somebody was waiting for a trumpet to sound while I declared one frame the winner. That is not going to happen. But we can at least point people in the right direction depending on where they are starting from.

If you have never owned a pair and want a camera, Meta AI, audio and features that help interpret the environment, I would start by looking at models with a camera. Gen 2 can be a very good choice if the price is right, while Gen 3 makes more sense for somebody who wants to start with the newest generation and values battery life, the slimmer design and the action button. Meta Glasses are also worth a look where they are sold, especially if one frame fits better or Adventurer offers a cheaper route in.

If you already have Gen 1, the case is easy to understand: moving from roughly four hours of battery life to around eight changes how the glasses can be used, the camera improves and you benefit from everything that has matured around the ecosystem. It is not compulsory, but there are practical reasons. With Gen 2, I would think more carefully. If it works well, is comfortable and the battery lasts through normal use, Gen 3 does not force anyone to rush out. If we want a second pair, need more battery life or are drawn to the assignable button, then it becomes more compelling. And if not, we can do something revolutionary: calmly carry on using what we already own.

People who exercise often should look at Oakley: HSTN if they want something sporty but close to everyday use, Vanguard if movement and more intensive sport will be central. If audio matters most, Ray-Ban Meta Audio makes a lot of sense: light, much longer battery life, AI and sound features, but with the clear understanding that there is no camera. If the camera is one of the reasons we want smart glasses, we can stop doing the maths. Someone with useful vision who wants a display should study the Display model, while a totally blind person should think carefully about how much the non-visual functions add before paying the premium.

For all-day wear, battery, comfort and fit matter more than the model name. A Gen 3, properly fitted Optics or one of the Meta Glasses frames may make more sense than a frame chosen simply because it looked good in the advertisement.

Two things not to forget

One detail sometimes disappears beneath all the AI: this entire contraption still rests on your face. Weight, pressure from the arms, nose fit, frame width and stability matter, especially if we are going to wear the glasses for hours. Anyone buying them mainly for their technology should try the frame physically whenever possible. We do not need a mirror to know whether a pair of glasses is punishing us behind the ears. That becomes clear rather quickly, and it is usually a surprisingly objective assessment.
