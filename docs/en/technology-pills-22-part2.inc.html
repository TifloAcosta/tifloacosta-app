The other point concerns everybody around us. Camera-equipped models have a capture LED that indicates when a photo is being taken or a recording is under way, and Meta normally prevents the camera from operating if that indicator is covered or disabled. For us, that has a practical consequence: using the camera as an assistive tool does not remove the need to respect the people around us. We may need to ask what is in front of us while also being in a place where it is sensible to explain what we are doing. This is not unique to these glasses, but because the camera is on our face it can be easier for the wearer to forget that it is there.

And one more clarification while we are here. These glasses can describe, recognise, read, help us look up information and connect us with another person. They do not replace mobility techniques, a cane or a guide dog. An AI can tell us there is a chair; we should not cheerfully ask it to guide us around that chair while crossing a road. These are different tools, and the clearer we are about their roles, the more useful they become.

One hundred models, a handful of questions

Meta says there will be more than one hundred AI-glasses options by the end of 2026. The number is impressive, but the important story to me is something else: the era in which one smart-glasses design tried to serve everybody in exactly the same way is ending. We are starting to see models specialised for audio, sport, battery life, display, price or prescription use, and that lets us buy with a little more judgement. If I had to reduce the whole collection to a few questions, they would be these: does it have a camera, how long does the battery last, can I wear it comfortably for many hours, what can I do with Meta AI in my language, which features are available where I live, can I use Be My Eyes, do I need a display, am I going to exercise in them, and do I also want them to replace my headphones? And ahead of all of those: what do I want them for? There is no frame that wins every comparison. There is a frame that fits a particular need better, and that is more useful than knowing which one comes first in a table.

When Stories first appeared, putting a camera in a pair of Ray-Bans was the novelty. Today that almost feels like the easy part. What matters comes after pressing a button or speaking a command: a camera that helps us understand an object, the ability to ask something without taking out the phone, a Be My Eyes call that begins hands-free, directions, music or messages without sealing ourselves off from the sounds around us. Those of us who cannot see also have one curious advantage in this huge catalogue: we can skip quite a few pages devoted to whether one colour goes with another. What matters to us is that they are comfortable, the battery lasts, the camera points where it should, the AI understands what we ask and, when we need help, it does not answer that the feature is not available in our region yet. And if they happen to look good as well, all the better. We should leave the mirror at least one pleasure.

Before we put the glasses away

The Meta glasses family changes quickly. Models, AI functions, supported languages and countries can change through software updates or new launches. What you have just read reflects the situation known as of September 30, 2026, so if you are buying a model for one particular feature, check with Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley or Be My Eyes first to make sure that feature is available in your country and language. Spanish and English are generally well supported, although some new features still arrive first in English or in selected markets.

Second main story: Europe wants to open Android's front door

From glasses that want to look on our behalf, we move to a less visible fight, but one that could change the phone in our pocket more than it first appears. There are things you buy knowing perfectly well who is in charge inside. Buy an iPhone and you know Apple has arranged the furniture. Buy an Android phone and the house feels more open, right up until you reach certain rooms and discover that Google is carrying quite a few keys in its pocket too.

One of those keys belongs to the assistant. We can install ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Mistral and whatever other intelligent creature happens to appear, put the icon wherever we like, open it, talk to it and grant permissions. But when it comes to occupying the privileged position from which Gemini can wake by voice, understand what is happening on the phone, act inside other apps and use deeper system functions, the situation changes. Google is playing on home ground there, and the European Union has decided to ask for the keys again.

This is not the first time. Apple has spent several years learning that when Brussels knocks on the door it often arrives carrying a fairly thick folder, and now Google is beginning to get the same treatment. The culprit has a decidedly unromantic name: the Digital Markets Act, or DMA. It may sound like another European regulation designed to require three coffees before you finish the first page, but it is changing very concrete things on our phones. Some may help users. Others may make life more complicated. And for quite a few, we still do not know which side of the line they will end up on.

What Europe wants to do with Android

The European Commission starts from a simple idea: if Google controls Android and also has its own AI assistant, it can give Gemini capabilities that its rivals do not have. The Commission estimates that around 60 percent of mobile users in the European Union use Android. That makes Google's operating system an enormous gateway to artificial intelligence, and whoever controls the gate has a certain advantage in deciding who gets through and how far they can go. That is why the Commission opened proceedings in January 2026 to spell out what access Google should provide to other assistants, and the final measures arrived in July.

We are not talking simply about changing an icon. The Commission has identified eleven Android capabilities that matter to AI services, including the ability to activate an assistant with its own word or phrase. In other words, one day we might have something like “Hey, ChatGPT” or “Hey, Claude”, or whatever each company comes up with, provided we do not end up living in a house where five artificial intelligences all answer at once because somebody said good morning.

The opening goes much further. An alternative assistant could receive contextual information from the phone, interact with apps and carry out actions for us: compose and send an email using our preferred app, add items to a list, share a photograph, order food, book a taxi, suggest replies in a conversation or use information related to what is currently on screen. That is no longer simply installing ChatGPT. It is allowing it to behave like a system assistant. And that is where the dispute begins.

Google says: careful, this is not just permission to look at your photos

Google does not deny that competition is good. Its argument goes in another direction. An assistant with this level of integration needs extraordinarily sensitive permissions. An AI agent that understands the context of the screen, accesses information stored on the phone and acts inside other apps has tremendous possibilities. It also has tremendous possibilities for getting things wrong—or doing something rather worse if the service is malicious or its security fails. Google says Android has spent years building walls between apps precisely so that nobody gets indiscriminate access to the system: apps usually live in their own kind of compartment. They may request permissions, but they do not automatically receive the keys to the building. Giving assistants downloaded by the user deep access to certain functions requires changes to part of that model, and Google believes the European proposal goes too far. Nor is this only a corporate complaint, because several cybersecurity specialists have also expressed concern about opening certain capabilities without extremely strict controls.

So we have one of those uncomfortable arguments in which both sides can make a reasonable point. Europe says that if only Google's assistant can use Android's best functions, the competition will always be running with one leg tied. Google replies that if we open very sensitive functions to more applications in order to level the race, we may end up paying for that equality with security and privacy. And in the middle, as usual, there we are.

Europe is not asking Android to let just anybody in

There is an important qualification here. The measures do not say that any app fresh out of the oven will automatically receive every permission. Especially sensitive capabilities may be subject to security, privacy and integrity conditions. These include screen automation, certain system integrations, centralised access to app data and functions that can understand device context. There will be certification processes. Google will take part in them, but independent bodies may also do so. The first conditions for the programme are due to be published in February 2027, and applications should begin to be accepted in May. The Commission also says that the user will always decide which service to use and which permissions to grant. In theory, then, nobody should wake up one morning to discover that an unknown chatbot is reading half their phone because Brussels ordered it.

There is still a perfectly reasonable question: how many of us know exactly what we are authorising when an app drops six permission screens in front of us? More freedom also means more decisions, and more decisions do not automatically produce better decisions.

The other front: search

While everybody was looking at the assistant, Europe opened another door: search. Google has spent decades accumulating an enormous amount of information about how we search. Not just the words we type, but which results appear, which ones we click, which ones we ignore and how millions of queries behave. That information helps improve a search engine, and it is difficult to compete with somebody who has been collecting it on a gigantic scale since many of us were still listening to a modem sing before the internet appeared.

The DMA requires Google to share certain search data with competitors. The Commission believes that without enough information those rivals will always struggle to build a comparable search engine. It has also decided that chatbots offering search functions may request that data, which potentially brings in AI services that want to answer questions using current information from the web.

But they are not handing over our personal search history with our name attached

The phrase “Google will have to share search data” can produce a small shiver, and for good reason. Searching the internet is surprisingly intimate. You can ask for a recipe and, five minutes later, look up something about an illness, debt, a relationship problem or something you would rather not bring up over Sunday lunch. Google uses exactly this argument in its opposition. It says the anonymisation proposed by Europe does not provide sufficient guarantees and that sharing certain data could create a risk of people being reidentified.

The Commission responds that it has designed a multi-layer anonymisation system, backed by contractual obligations, independent audits and regular controls. Recipients will not be able to use the data for anything they like either. They will not, for example, be allowed to take it and train a general-purpose large AI model with it. Its use must be related to improving search services. Nor may it be used for advertising, building consumer profiles or simply copying Google's results wholesale.

The Commission also says Google will be able to raise objections when it believes a particular company represents a serious cybersecurity or data-protection risk. DuckDuckGo, for its part, argues that the safeguards are sufficient and accuses Google of using legal challenges to delay opening the system. So once again this is not a film with a hero, a villain and closing music. It is a clash of interests around a technically delicate question.

Google is going to court

At the end of September, Google filed appeals with the General Court of the European Union against both decisions. The fight is no longer only technical or political; it is now judicial as well. Google says the measures could cause harm that would be difficult to reverse, particularly in privacy and security. The Commission maintains that its decisions already include the necessary protections and has said it will defend them in court. Google has also asked for at least some of the obligations to be suspended as an interim measure while the case is decided.

There is one legal detail worth keeping in mind: filing an appeal does not make a rule disappear. Unless the court decides otherwise, the deadlines keep running. The first major Android date arrives with Android 18 and, as a broad limit for many of these functions, August 1, 2027. Simultaneous activation of several assistants using their own voice phrases may wait until Android 19 and August 2028. On the search side, several milestones come earlier: documentation, test data, anonymisation mechanisms and pricing are due to be prepared between 2026 and January 2027.

We have already seen this with Apple

Anyone who follows the iPhone world will recognise the pattern. The DMA has also forced Apple to open functions previously reserved for its own services. It has had to make it easier to change the default browser, allow alternative app-distribution systems, open certain connectivity functions and give third parties access to iOS capabilities that previously benefited mainly Apple's own products. In some cases the result is visible: there are more choices for default apps, third-party devices can reach more functions and developers have new ways to integrate with iPhone and iPad. The Commission points to precisely these cases as evidence that interoperability can improve competition.

Apple tells the story differently. It has repeatedly warned that some obligations can affect privacy, security and the integrated experience of its products. It has also delayed certain features in Europe, citing difficulties related to the DMA. The Commission replies that the law does not prohibit Apple from launching those features; it requires that, when regulated capabilities are involved, competitors can obtain equivalent access. And so it goes: the company says it needs to protect the product it built, Europe says protecting the product cannot become a way of protecting a commercial advantage, and European users gradually discover that they are sometimes using versions of services that differ from those available elsewhere.

Is the DMA helping us or hurting us?

Probably both, and I suspect that answer will not fit comfortably into some headlines. One obvious advantage is choice. If somebody decides tomorrow that ChatGPT works better for them as an assistant than Gemini, they should not have to accept it as a second-class app. They could eventually place it at the centre of Android and let it carry out tasks with far deeper integration. That forces companies to compete to become our preferred assistant, rather than winning simply because they arrived preinstalled.

For those of us who use accessibility technology, that could be especially relevant. Imagine an assistant that provides better image descriptions, understands certain spoken commands more reliably or offers a more accessible experience. Deep system integration could make a substantial difference. It may also encourage specialised services that currently have no realistic way to compete with an assistant placed in the front row by the operating-system maker.

Search is similar. Giving other companies access to data that only Google could previously accumulate may help create better search engines. And if serious alternatives emerge, Google itself has more reason to keep improving. So far, pleasant music.

Now the other side. The first consequence is fairly obvious: Europe is becoming a technologically different market. Companies have to build region-specific functions, choice screens, extra permission systems, alternative distribution mechanisms and interoperability tools they do not necessarily need elsewhere. That costs money and time. Some products reach other markets first. Some features arrive later. And in certain cases a company may decide not to launch something until it understands how it fits the regulation. So the same law designed to give us more options can also cause some new features to reach us more slowly.

Complexity grows as well. Choosing a browser is fine. Choosing an app store may be fine. Choosing a payment system may be fine. Choosing a search engine, voice assistant, AI service, password manager, calling app and another half-dozen things is still fine—right up until the day we set up a new phone and it starts feeling like a civil-service exam.

Then there is security. A closed ecosystem can control more tightly who touches certain parts of the system. Opening those parts helps competition but increases the number of actors who can make mistakes or abuse their access. Opening is not automatically unsafe. It simply takes more work than a button labelled “Allow” might suggest.

And then there is privacy, which becomes especially delicate with assistants. We are moving from apps that answer questions to agents that can act. An agent that reads context, understands our screen, opens apps and carries out actions needs to know a great deal about us. So in a few years the question may no longer be only, “Which artificial intelligence is smartest?” It may be, “Which one am I willing to hand the keys to?”

Who gets to keep the keys?

The easy headline would be that Europe wants to throw Gemini off Android. That is not what is happening. Gemini will remain there and will probably continue to be one of the best-integrated assistants. What Europe wants is for that integration not to be an exclusive privilege simply because Google makes the operating system. Google believes that forcing it to share certain capabilities may weaken protections built around Android. Europe believes those protections cannot be used as an excuse to keep competitors out.

The courts now have part of the answer, and while they decide, engineers will keep preparing Android for a rather curious future. In a few years, holding down a button may no longer summon Gemini automatically. Each person may choose who answers. That could be excellent, provided we are made absolutely clear about what we are allowing that assistant to do. Choosing who gets the keys is one thing. Handing out copies without knowing who can walk through the door afterwards is quite another.

Third main story: Apple looks back toward the home

After all those regulatory keys, we move to a different door. Apple has a curious habit: there are products it pampers like the youngest child in the family, and others it can leave sitting politely in a corner for four years waiting for somebody to remember them again. Apple TV has belonged to the second group for quite a while. HomePod mini cannot exactly complain about too much attention either, and the connected home in general has spent years appearing in Apple rumours with admirable punctuality and in stores with rather less enthusiasm.

That may change on October 13. If Mark Gurman's information is right again, Apple will introduce three home products that day: a new Apple TV 4K, a second-generation HomePod mini and, above all, a completely new device that has spent years appearing under provisional names such as HomePad, Home Hub or simply smart-home hub. Apple has not officially announced the presentation, so the conditional tense remains compulsory. This time, however, the pieces fit together better than in many earlier rumours. References to a new Apple TV have appeared in Apple documentation and code, we already know a great deal about the future home device, and several sources point to the same date. The three products would also have something in common: Siri AI. That is what could give this launch more weight than a simple refresh of devices that have been gathering dust in the catalogue for years.

Before the products, something changed at the top

This presentation would have another detail we should not ignore: Apple has a new CEO. Since September 1, 2026, John Ternus has occupied the job Tim Cook held for fifteen years. Cook has not disappeared off to grow tomatoes. He is now executive chair of the board and remains involved in major decisions, but the person running Apple is Ternus. He comes from Hardware Engineering and has worked across iPad, AirPods, iPhone, Mac and Apple Watch. His background is more product-oriented than operational, which does not mean Apple will wake up tomorrow as a manufacturer of intelligent toasters. Companies this size do not turn like bicycles.

It would be tempting to attribute every change we see in these first months to him, and I would be more cautious. A device such as the new home hub has been in development for years, and the Apple TV and HomePod mini were not invented over the new CEO's breakfast either. These launches are not “Ternus products” in the sense of having been conceived under his leadership. What we can begin to watch is something else: how Apple chooses to present them.

For years we became used to major events in which almost the entire conversation revolved around a stage, a keynote and several lines that seemed designed to end up printed on a T-shirt. That has not disappeared, but Apple has been mixing large presentations with press releases, shorter videos and launches spread across several weeks. September already brought a sizeable batch—iPhone 18 Pro, Apple Watch Series 12, Apple Watch Ultra 4 and AirPods 5—alongside a succession of announcements, videos, demonstrations and staggered arrivals in stores. October could continue in that direction.

We still do not know whether the 13th will bring a long video, a smaller presentation, press briefings or a mixture of all three. Perhaps that itself will be one of the first clues to the new period: an Apple that gives each category the size of presentation it needs instead of forcing every product to share one grand ceremony. I do not know whether we will be calling this “the Ternus style” in a year; saying so now would be premature. What we can already see is an Apple that spreads its cards around the calendar more than before and seems less determined to place everything on the table on the same day. For those of us who follow technology, this means more dates to remember, and we were doing quite well for dates already.

The star will not be Apple TV

Of the three expected products, the one attracting most curiosity is the new home hub. We have been hearing about it for years. At first it sounded like a HomePod with a screen. Then it became a small iPad attached to a speaker. Later the name HomePad began to circulate. Now the leaks describe something rather more concrete.

We are hearing about a square display of roughly six or seven inches. One version would be designed for a table or kitchen counter. Another could be mounted on a wall. The tabletop model would have a base containing speakers and microphones, with the display attached through a structure that recalls the old iMac G4—the one with the screen floating over a round base on an arm that could move freely. Apple has spent twenty years making things and then discovering that some of its old ideas were actually quite good. Reuse is welcome when the thing being reused was attractive.

The device would not have a battery, so it would need to stay connected to power. Traditional power and volume buttons are not expected either. That already tells us something about how Apple imagines it: not a tablet to carry around the house, but a fixed part of the home. A point we talk to. A place from which to control devices. A screen that changes depending on who approaches. And, probably, a permanent doorway to Siri AI.

A screen we cannot see. So what is in it for us?

This is the first question a blind person should ask. If Apple's big new product is a device with a screen, what do I gain? Possibly quite a lot. Possibly nothing. It depends on one thing Apple has not explained, because the product has not even been announced yet: the accessibility of its new system.

The device is expected to run a new operating system, possibly called homeOS. Leaks describe a mixture of tvOS, watchOS and iOS elements, with apps, widgets, clocks, home controls, FaceTime, Music, Podcasts, Calendar, Reminders, Notes and other Apple services. All excellent for somebody who can see the screen. Our question is different: will it have VoiceOver?

I would not assume that it will until Apple confirms it. The company has a strong history of bringing VoiceOver to its major platforms. Apple TV, for example, has had a screen reader for years and can be operated completely without sight. But this device would create a new product category and probably a new operating system as well, so VoiceOver will be one of the first things to check on presentation day. If homeOS includes VoiceOver from the beginning, the picture changes significantly. We would no longer be talking about a screen that is useless to us, but about a small accessible home computer that is always on, equipped with microphones, speakers, a camera and access to devices around the house.

Apple already has iOS, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS, tvOS and visionOS, because apparently six operating systems were not quite enough. In homeOS, the screen is also expected to change depending on the person's distance from it. Certain elements could grow larger as someone approaches, while the interface would show broader information at a distance. For low-vision users that could be useful if Apple lets people configure size, contrast and behaviour. For a totally blind person, what matters is whether all that visual adaptation has an equivalent through VoiceOver and whether apps maintain coherent navigation. A beautiful system is not much help if we have to guess where every button went. Apple has enough experience to avoid that. The question is whether it applies that experience from day one.

Siri AI: the difference between another speaker and something else

The current HomePod is an interesting product for a blind person. It does not need a screen. It is controlled by voice. It can play music, answer certain questions, control Home accessories and carry out some tasks with almost no need to touch anything. On paper, it sounds almost tailor-made for us. The problem is that Siri has spent years reminding us that a good idea and a good execution do not always arrive together.

Siri AI is meant to change that. The new generation of the assistant can understand more natural conversation, use personal context, draw information from messages, email and other content, and perform actions inside apps. If those capabilities arrive fully in the home, the new device could become much more than a HomePod with a screen attached.

Imagine walking into the kitchen and asking, “What have I got this afternoon?”, “What time is my next appointment?”, asking it to put on the station we were listening to in the living room, remind us in fifteen minutes, call Marta, turn off the hallway light, set the heating to twenty degrees, or tell us what ingredients we need for the recipe we saved yesterday. None of those things is revolutionary on its own. The novelty would be bringing them together in a home device that knows who is speaking and adapts its answers to that person.

The device would know who is standing in front of it

The new hub is expected to include a front-facing camera. It would be used for FaceTime, but would also have a role in recognising users. According to the information available, the system could identify who approaches by their face and adapt the information it displays to that person. It would also use voice to distinguish between users, and there would be a guest mode to avoid exposing private information when the person nearby does not belong to the household.

For a blind person, facial recognition in the interface is not the important part. The result is. If I walk into a room and the device knows it is me, it can offer my reminders, appointments, music, messages or automations without a sign-in, without selecting a profile and without hunting for a button. That is useful.

It also creates serious privacy questions, because a device that stays switched on in a room, has a camera and microphones and recognises people needs to explain very clearly when it is listening, when the camera is being used, what is processed locally and what leaves the device. Apple will almost certainly talk about privacy; it does that throughout its AI strategy. But here, saying the word several times during the presentation will not be enough. We will need to see how it works.

The camera may matter more to us than the display

There is another possibility. During 2026 Apple expanded image-description and recognition capabilities through Apple Intelligence. VoiceOver can provide richer descriptions, Real-Time Recognition allows questions about what the iPhone camera is seeing, and Magnifier includes related tools for people with low vision. There is no confirmation that the new home hub will use its camera in this way, but it would be an obvious opportunity.

A device permanently sitting in a kitchen could, in theory, help identify objects, interpret parts of the environment or answer questions about what the camera has in front of it. I am not saying it will. We do not know. But the hardware would be there, and Apple already has the technology. So one of my questions on presentation day will be simple: is that camera only for FaceTime and recognising faces, or will it also be able to look on our behalf? If the answer ends up being the second, the device earns quite a few more points in this house.

Table or wall

Apple is reportedly preparing two formats. The tabletop version appears intended for the kitchen, bedroom, living room or desk. The wall-mounted one would make sense as a central panel for lights, heating and cooling, cameras, locks and home scenes.

For a blind person, placement matters differently. If we are going to use it mainly by voice, we do not need to approach the screen constantly. We can place the device wherever the microphones work best and the sound reaches us properly. If it has VoiceOver and we want to use apps by touch, the tabletop version could be much more convenient.

We will also need to discover how it is controlled physically. Leaks suggest there may be no traditional power or volume buttons, leaving touch and voice as the main methods. Whenever a manufacturer removes buttons, screen-reader users tend to make the particular face we make when somebody says, “It is much simpler now, everything is done with gestures.” Sometimes it is. Other times, a very long afternoon is about to begin.

HomePod mini 2: six years waiting for its turn

The second expected product is much less mysterious. The current HomePod mini was introduced in 2020. Six years is a long time in technology—long enough, perhaps, for Siri to learn a new answer or two.

The second generation is expected to look much the same on the outside but change substantially within. A more modern processor should be able to run new Siri AI features. Sound quality should improve. Apple's N1 wireless chip, with Wi-Fi 7 support, is expected, along with the possibility of a new generation of Ultra Wideband for better proximity features with other devices. New colours are also likely. For a blind person, I can confirm that pink and green sit fairly low on the priority list.
