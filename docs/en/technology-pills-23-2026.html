Technology Pills No. 23 of 2026

Tony Acosta · Canal TifloAcosta

October 21, 2026

The small print is included too

Hello, hello, lovely people.

There is a word technology companies are remarkably fond of: “included”.

We buy a phone and they tell us some wonderful feature is included. Excellent. Until we discover that it works only in certain countries, on certain models and, preferably, when the moon is in its first quarter. We pay for a subscription and eventually discover that we have been paying for services we never knew we had. Then there are features announced as though everybody could use them, even though the software bringing them to us has not quite come out of the oven.

You have to give these companies credit: they have found an extraordinary number of meanings for one little word.

That, among other things, is what this edition is about.

Apple is preparing to release iOS 27.2, an update with far more substance than that modest “.2” might suggest. Its new Siri is adding several languages, including Spanish. Whether we get it on our own phones will depend on where we live, which iPhone we have and a few other conditions. I would rather explain those before anybody gets too excited. After all, a reader in Tenerife deserves the same clear information as somebody in Buenos Aires, Mexico City or London.

Then we will tackle iCloud, the cloud we pay for without always knowing what we receive. Some people have paid for extra storage for years without discovering all the features that come with it. Others are missing out on useful free tools. We will look at what iCloud stores, what it synchronises and why deleting photos from your phone because you think they will remain safely in the cloud can end in tears.

Over in the JAWS 2027 corner, Vispero is showing off an artificial-intelligence agent that tries to press the inaccessible button we have spent ten minutes hunting for. I will explain what has actually been demonstrated, what remains unknown and why nobody needs to rush after a suspicious installer just yet.

And there are ten shorter stories as well: WhatsApp calls for people who do not use WhatsApp, autonomous taxis, earthquake warnings, Android tablets, audiobooks and a new lock for ChatGPT conversations, among other developments. We will get to them in due course.

Not everything here is labelled “accessibility”. But almost any technological development can affect how we live and work. That is the point of these Pills: a story does not need the word “accessible” in its headline to matter to us.

If anyone is worried about an impenetrable technical lecture, there is no need for a dictionary, an engineering degree or an AI assistant to understand this edition. Just come along for the ride.

I will take care of the rest.

iOS 27.2: we all update, but we do not all get the same features

An update with borders

Apple released the release candidate, or RC, of iOS 27.2 on October 19. Barring a last-minute surprise, the public version is expected before the end of the month, with signs pointing towards the following week.

So far, so familiar: Apple prepares, tests and publishes, and millions of us download. This time, however, one detail deserves a little attention.

Imagine two identical iPhones, one in Mexico and the other in a European Union country. Both are updated. One gets a much cleverer Siri; the other keeps the familiar version. Neither download has failed. Apple is simply introducing certain features on different sides of a geographical border.

The model complicates matters further. An iPhone 15 Pro supports Apple Intelligence, while the standard iPhone 15 does not. And some of Siri's most advanced capabilities require newer hardware still.

That gives us three main factors: country, device and language. We need to keep them separate, particularly when distinguishing existing features from new ones that have yet to become available.

First stop: the European Union

Spain, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal and the other EU member states are in the same boat. The Canary Islands are included too. No one has given us a special Siri just because we happen to be surrounded by water, although I can think of people who would gladly accept one.

The headline feature of iOS 27.2 is Siri AI, Apple's next-generation assistant. It can hold more natural conversations, understand personal information distributed across applications, recognise what is happening on screen and carry out more ambitious tasks than setting alarms. This beta adds Spanish, French, Portuguese, Japanese and Korean to the English offered at launch.

Here is the first boundary: Siri AI is not currently available on iPhone or iPad in the European Union. An Apple Watch paired with an affected iPhone is restricted as well.

Apple cites the Digital Markets Act. The company says it proposed a solution that would meet the rules while preserving privacy, but that the European Commission did not accept it. According to Reuters, the Commission's position is that Apple itself chose not to release the feature.

We could spend hours debating who is right. Unfortunately, that debate will not change what turns up on our phones after an update.

In EU countries, installing iOS 27.2 on an otherwise compatible iPhone will not activate Siri AI or new features specifically dependent on it, such as Siri's camera mode.

That does not mean the European Union has lost Apple Intelligence altogether. Features introduced with iOS 18.4, including the earlier writing tools, Image Playground and certain Photos functions, remain available on supported devices, subject to their particular language and hardware requirements. The restriction concerns the newer generation built around Siri AI.

Here is the curious part: on a compatible Mac or Apple Vision Pro, Apple does allow Siri AI in the EU when the relevant language is supported. According to Applesfera, Spanish reaches the Mac with macOS 27.2. We could therefore have a Mac holding a conversation with the new Siri while the iPhone sitting right beside it has no such option. You have to smile at that.

Europe is not the same as the European Union

This distinction matters to readers in European countries outside the EU: the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Norway, for example. Apple's availability information identifies the European Union and mainland China as the main exclusions for Siri AI on the iPhone. Elsewhere, the new assistant can work on a compatible device if language and account requirements are met.

That brings us to a familiar temptation: changing the country associated with our Apple Account to obtain a feature early. I do not recommend it. Such a change can interfere with subscriptions, purchases and other services, and does not guarantee activation. Our phones have enough complicated menus without our inventing administrative problems for ourselves.

Second stop: outside the European Union

Let us travel across the Americas, Asia and Oceania, and beyond.

For someone with a compatible iPhone, support for Spanish and the other new languages is welcome news in much of the world. In Mexico, for example, specialist publications expect Siri AI in Spanish. Apple's general exclusions are the EU and mainland China, although actual roll-outs may happen in stages. Checking Apple's official feature-availability information is safer than assuming that all eligible countries receive every feature on precisely the same day.

What can the new Siri do? We can ask about information in our messages, emails, calendars, notes and reminders without remembering where it was saved. We might ask for the details of a meeting mentioned in an email or link an appointment to an address sent in a message. Siri can also understand on-screen material, search the internet for current questions, connect tasks across compatible apps and offer a dedicated app where conversations can be resumed on other devices.

For those of us who cannot see the screen, some of these possibilities are particularly interesting. Asking our phone what is open, where to find a detail or how to complete a task by speaking may be much more comfortable than memorising a long sequence of taps.

But remember that Siri AI remains a beta. Some capabilities depend on app support, server-based tools have daily usage limits, and updating does not automatically enrol us. Eligible users need to open Settings, Siri, and find “Try Siri AI (Beta)”. This places them on a waiting list, and Apple says the waiting time can vary. If the new Siri has not appeared, check the region, language and device before looking for the enrolment option. Restoring your iPhone three times is not on Apple's recommended list. At least, not for this.

The exception: mainland China

We cannot lump the whole world together. Siri AI is unavailable in mainland China, and other Apple Intelligence features face specific restrictions there. It would therefore be misleading to say that everyone outside the European Union will receive Siri AI.

Third stop: which iPhone is in your pocket?

We have dealt with countries. Now comes the question many readers are probably asking: what about my particular phone?

iPhone 15 Pro and 15 Pro Max

These were the first iPhone 15 models compatible with Apple Intelligence. Outside restricted regions, they can run Siri AI in an available language. What they do not offer are expressive Siri voices, which let users customise the pace and expressiveness of the assistant and require a more powerful on-device AI model. Siri AI can still speak on these phones; we simply cannot adjust it in the same way.

iPhone 15 and 15 Plus

Same year, different story. Neither supports Apple Intelligence. Both receive the general improvements in iOS 27.2 that do not depend on it, but they will not receive Siri AI. This is one of those occasions when the word “Pro” makes a substantial difference.

The iPhone 16 family

The iPhone 16, 16 Plus, 16 Pro, 16 Pro Max and 16e all support Apple Intelligence, and can use Siri AI wherever it is permitted. They lack expressive voices and some newer camera capabilities.

iPhone 17, 17e, Air, 18 Pro and Duo

All these models support Apple Intelligence, but a higher number does not guarantee every feature. Expressive voices are supported on the iPhone Air, the 17 Pro and 18 Pro models, and the new iPhone Duo, Apple's first folding iPhone. For now, expressive voices are available only in English. The standard iPhone 17 and the 17e do not have that particular option. Group FaceTime's dual capture also depends on the specific iPhone model, so it is worth checking Apple's feature requirements rather than relying on the marketing name alone.

iPhone 11, 12, 13, 14 and eligible SE models

There is news for you too. iOS 27 supports iPhone 11 onwards and the second-generation iPhone SE or later. These devices can install the update and enjoy improvements that do not require Apple Intelligence. They do not support Siri AI; that also applies to the iPhone 14 Pro models.

And this is worth saying plainly: an iPhone without Siri AI has not lost Siri. The familiar assistant continues to work with the capabilities that device supports. Receiving fewer new features is not the same thing as losing software updates.

The changes that are not Siri

So far, artificial intelligence has taken centre stage. But iOS 27.2 brings other changes, some of them particularly relevant when using an iPhone without looking at the screen.

Messages: suggested actions

A message may now offer a suggested action to create a calendar event, reminder or note, open Maps or consult flight and delivery details. If somebody sends the details of an appointment, the iPhone may offer to place it in the calendar. The relevant categories are controlled under Settings, Apps, Messages, Suggested Actions.

This feature appeared in early iOS 27 betas, disappeared before the public release and has now returned. Not all Apple tools reach every language simultaneously; anyone relying on it in Spanish should first check whether it is supported.

FaceTime: two cameras in group calls

Dual capture arrived with iOS 27, letting the front and rear cameras be used at the same time. With iOS 27.2, it extends to group calls. Participants can see our face and what is in front of us without a constant switch between cameras. It requires a supported recent iPhone; other people on the call need iOS 27, iPadOS 27 or macOS 27 to see both views. It could be particularly useful when asking somebody to look at an object during a conversation.

Apple TV: separate profiles

The Apple TV app now supports user profiles on iPhone and iPad, so recommendations and viewing histories no longer need to become mixed together. Not revolutionary, perhaps, but useful in a household where more than one person uses the same device or subscription.

Podcasts: new episodes and unfinished ones

The Up Next area on the home screen separates fresh episodes of followed shows from programmes we have started but not finished. Frequent podcast listeners should find it easier to return to something without searching one enormous queue. For VoiceOver users, the important test will be whether the new organisation is clearly navigable.

Home: smarter locks

The Home app adds two settings for compatible smart locks. One locks the door a specified time after the door has actually closed, rather than simply after it was unlocked. The other can warn us if a door has been left open too long. Both depend on the hardware. That second warning may be particularly useful when we cannot visually confirm whether a door was shut properly.

Phone, Music and Games

Call Context can show contacts' anniversaries wherever supported. Apple Music has a new black-and-white icon, which has attracted mixed opinions, while the Games app has a revised home screen. Not everything carries equal weight. A useful new function and a change of icon colour are hardly the same thing.

VoiceOver and accessibility

Here I want to linger a little.

Screen-reader users know that an update can bring thirty shiny features while the thing that truly matters is fixing the control we have to use twenty times every day.

Safari 27.2's release notes describe two accessibility fixes. One concerns websites that dynamically draw or update content, including many AI services: in certain circumstances VoiceOver read already-spoken text again. Apple says that has been corrected. The second concerns lists, tables and similar elements whose content was not properly included in an accessible name or description when another element referred to them.

Does that guarantee that Safari and VoiceOver will be flawless in iOS 27.2? Certainly not. It means Apple has documented these fixes during testing; we will still need to see how they behave in the final release. They are also separate from the broader iOS 27 accessibility changes we covered in an earlier edition.

One further point deserves caution. During testing, AppleVis users reported VoiceOver sometimes falling silent while a video played in Safari. I have not confirmed whether it still happens in the release candidate. If that use matters to you, test it rather than assuming the issue has disappeared.

Finally, Siri AI's expressive voices are not new VoiceOver voices. They are separate features.

Privacy also depends on location

In the European Union, Apple is introducing an alternative, expanded version of the permission notice through which apps ask to track us across services belonging to different companies. Apple's documentation sets out where it applies and which developers must use it; I would not assume it covers every app or every region.

Our choice should continue to count. If we have disabled the general setting allowing apps to request tracking permission, a redesigned notice should not override that. If an unfamiliar prompt appears after updating, read it before pressing the first button just to make it disappear.

What is not included

A redesigned Health app, with personalised information and other indicators, appeared during iOS 27.2 testing. Apple withdrew it from the release candidate and says it will come in a later update. iOS 27.3 has been mentioned for December, but Apple has not confirmed a date.

It is not part of this release. Likewise, a feature spotted inside beta code is not necessarily ready for public use.

What will each person actually receive?

A few examples should make the picture clearer.

In Spain, an iPhone 15 Pro gets general improvements and the Apple Intelligence features already available, but not Siri AI on the phone or expressive voices. A compatible Mac may be a different story.

In Mexico, an iPhone 15 Pro can gain Siri AI in Spanish, subject to the waiting list. It does not get expressive voices.

In Mexico, an ordinary iPhone 15 gets general improvements but not Apple Intelligence. Changing country does not magically replace the phone's processor.

In the United States, an iPhone 17 Pro can use Siri AI, supported dual-camera group FaceTime and expressive Siri voices, the last feature in English.

In the United Kingdom, the EU restriction does not apply; compatibility still depends on the device, account and language.

An iPhone 12 in any supported country receives the general update, but not Apple Intelligence.

Mainland China has its own restrictions even for technically compatible phones.

That is why asking simply whether a phone “gets iOS 27.2” no longer tells the whole story. We need to know which particular function we want, where we are and what language we need it in.

Before pressing Update

When the final version is released, it will appear in Settings, General, Software Update. Have a recent backup, sufficient battery and a stable connection. There is no need to install a beta. And if we are coming from a considerably older release, some changes we notice may predate October. Apple's final release notes will show whether anything changed after the RC.

The device model, chip, language and region all affect the experience. Two people can buy the same iPhone and end up with different capabilities. Those of us who rely on accessibility have especially good reasons to understand that before updating.

Not because we should give up on new features, but because we have a right to know what we are getting.

And for all its intelligence, the iPhone has yet to find a way of explaining these distinctions before we press Update. For now, that remains our job.

iCloud: the cloud that seems small until we discover everything it stores

There is a message most iPhone owners know only too well. It appears at the least convenient moment: “iCloud Storage Is Almost Full”.

Then the questions start. How can storage be full when my phone has 128 or 256 GB? What exactly is full? Must I pay? If I do not, will I lose my photos? Someone has probably wondered where that cloud lives and whether they could go and sweep it out with a broom.

Let us bring some order to the confusion. iCloud has been around for years, yet many people still think it does little more than store photographs or back up a phone. It does much more. Some genuinely useful features are free, while others come with subscriptions we may have paid for for years without knowing everything they include.

First, what is iCloud?

Imagine a filing cabinet for photographs, documents, addresses, phone numbers and other important things. Now move that cabinet out of your house and into huge data centres connected to the internet and operated by Apple. That, in very simplified terms, is iCloud.

It stores information outside our device so we can recover it later. More interestingly, it keeps certain information up to date across our devices. Add a contact on an iPhone, switch on a Mac later, and the new number is there, provided both use the same Apple Account and syncing is enabled. Start a note on the iPad and carry on with it on the phone. Save a file to iCloud Drive and find it on your computer. No emails or cables required. Apple handles the synchronisation.

We do not even need several devices. Someone with just one iPhone can benefit, particularly when changing phones, losing one or recovering information.

But one point is worth remembering: iCloud is not the physical storage inside an iPhone.

Two kinds of storage we must not confuse

Suppose we buy an iPhone with 128 GB. That is the capacity of the device itself, used for apps, the operating system, downloaded music and everything else stored locally.

Now suppose we subscribe to 200 GB of iCloud. Does our iPhone suddenly have 328 GB? No. It still has 128 GB. We have rented room on Apple's servers. Think of a wardrobe in the bedroom and a storage unit elsewhere: the unit can hold extra belongings, but it does not make the wardrobe any bigger.

It can, however, help us make better use of the wardrobe. With iCloud Photos, full-resolution originals can remain in the cloud while smaller, optimised versions sit on the iPhone. The phone downloads full-resolution content when needed. This requires the Optimise iPhone Storage setting, enough iCloud capacity and an internet connection when the full file is not on the device.

If our phone is crowded with games, downloaded videos and applications, buying more cloud storage will not make those things vanish. Some data takes up physical space no matter how many clouds we pay for.

Backup and synchronisation are different things

This is one of the most widespread sources of confusion. When somebody says their photos are “in iCloud”, they may mean two different mechanisms: synchronisation and backup.

Synchronisation keeps content consistent across devices using an account. Add a photo and it appears elsewhere. Edit it and the edit appears too. Delete it and the deletion is synchronised as well. iCloud Photos is not a separate archive that remains untouched regardless of what we do on the phone.

Imagine having five thousand pictures and deleting one thousand on the iPhone because we believe the cloud will keep them. With iCloud Photos enabled, they disappear from the synchronised library as well. Apple normally keeps them in Recently Deleted for thirty days, but after that they may be lost for good.

So please do not delete photographs indiscriminately in the hope of freeing storage while leaving the originals in the cloud. Use optimisation instead. If you want photographs outside your synchronised library, save an independent copy somewhere else first.

A device backup is different. It stores settings and other data needed to rebuild an iPhone after replacing or restoring it: preferences, app arrangement and information that is not independently synchronised through iCloud. The apps themselves are generally downloaded again during restoration, and banking or similar apps may require fresh authentication. When iCloud Photos is enabled, those photos are not duplicated inside the everyday device backup.

That is why I recommend keeping an independent copy of documents and photographs that truly matter. Synchronisation is convenient, but it is not the same as a separate backup.

What does Apple give us free?

Every account includes 5 GB of iCloud storage at no charge. That is 5 GB per account, not per device, shared across services and devices. Photos, videos, documents and backups can use it up remarkably quickly.

The free plan is not useless, though. It can synchronise contacts, calendars, reminders, notes, passwords, Safari data and information from supported apps. It also supports iCloud Drive documents, an @icloud.com email address, iCloud Photos and device backups, as long as everything fits.

That last condition is the catch. Apple is not charging us specifically to make a backup: if the backup is larger than our available storage, there is simply nowhere for it to go.

Find My also works without iCloud+. If we configured it beforehand, we can try to locate a lost device, play a sound, mark it as lost or erase it remotely. We can access much of our content on the iCloud website, and Apple provides an iCloud app for Windows. Owning a Mac is not a requirement.

There is another little-known free advantage, too.

A lifeline when changing iPhones

We buy a new phone and want to move everything from the old one. Our 5 GB allowance is already full, and the transfer backup needs much more.

Under certain conditions, Apple offers temporary free iCloud space for this move. Look in Settings, General, Transfer or Reset iPhone, within the steps for preparing a new device. Eligible users can create a temporary backup without taking out a monthly subscription for the occasion. There are normally twenty-one days to restore it on the new iPhone, sometimes extendable, and after a successful restoration the temporary copy remains available for a further seven days.

That is not permanent free storage. And do not erase the old iPhone until you have confirmed that everything important has arrived safely on the new one.

When we decide to pay: iCloud+

Eventually 5 GB may no longer be enough. iCloud+ offers extra capacity alongside privacy, email, family-sharing and home-security features.

The plans come in 50 GB, 200 GB, 2 TB, 6 TB and 12 TB sizes. As an example, in Spain in October 2026, the 50 GB and 200 GB plans cost €0.99 and €2.99 a month. Prices and currencies vary by country, so the amount shown on your own account is the one that matters.

A terabyte is approximately one thousand gigabytes. And remember that a 50 GB plan gives us 50 GB in total, not 50 GB on top of the free five.

Paid plans add privacy, mail and connected-home benefits, subject to regional availability. Let us see what they do.

Hide My Email: keeping our address to ourselves

A website or an app asks for our email address, and we are not yet sure we trust it. Hide My Email can create a random address that forwards messages to our usual inbox. The other party never needs to see the real address.

We can use a different alias for each service. If one starts attracting unwanted messages, switch that alias off without changing our personal email. Replies can also be handled without exposing the original address, subject to the feature's conditions.

It does not make us completely anonymous, but it helps avoid handing the same address to everyone. It works in Safari, Mail, Settings and compatible apps. And it is not quite the same as the email-hiding option in Sign in with Apple, which can work even without an iCloud+ subscription. They are relatives, not twins.

Private Relay: revealing less while browsing

Here comes another name that sounds as though it requires a course: iCloud Private Relay.

When we browse the internet, our service provider and the websites we visit can learn certain details, including an IP address that may reveal an approximate location. Private Relay protects Safari traffic through two separate relays, so no single operator can easily connect our identity with the sites we visit.

It does not make the iPhone invisible. If we sign in to a site with our name and password, the site still knows who we are. Nor is it a full substitute for a VPN or a promise of absolute anonymity: it focuses on Safari and certain related traffic. It is not available in every region and can interfere with some services. Apple also lets us show our IP address temporarily to certain sites. Where supported, find it under Settings, our name, iCloud, Private Relay.

An email address with our own domain

Those of us who run a website or a project can use a domain we own with iCloud Mail. Instead of contact@icloud.com, we could use something like contact@myproject.com, provided we own and configure that domain. Apple supports custom domains and several addresses, which can be useful to families, associations and small businesses.

The domain itself is not a free gift from iCloud+. We must obtain and maintain it separately. Someone who only needs a personal email address for friends and family probably does not need this at all. It is an option, not a duty.

Apple Invites: arranging gatherings without chasing everyone

Another iCloud+ benefit lets us create invitations for birthdays, meetings and celebrations. We enter the place, date and details, send a link, and guests can respond. Photos and other content can also be shared when the requirements are met.

What if a guest uses Android? They can access the invitation on the web. They do not need an iPhone or their own iCloud+ plan; it is the event organiser who needs the subscription. It sounds small, but it can spare us those endless conversations about who is coming, who is not and who still has to check at home.

Sharing a family plan without sharing our photographs

An iCloud+ plan can be shared with up to five other family members: six people in total. If the plan includes 200 GB, that is a shared pool of 200 GB, not 200 GB for each person.

Does that let everyone else see our photos and documents? No. Each family member keeps a separate account and private information. Sharing storage capacity does not mean sharing files unless we deliberately choose to share a library or an individual item.

If several people in one family are paying separately, check whether Family Sharing can save money without mixing anyone's personal information.

Cameras and an interesting possibility for blind users

HomeKit Secure Video lets compatible cameras keep encrypted recordings in iCloud. Events from the previous ten days can be viewed in the Home app, and these recordings do not count against the space used by our photos and documents.

Not every camera qualifies: appropriate hardware, a compatible home hub and the right subscription are needed. The 50 GB tier supports recordings from one camera, 200 GB supports up to five, and plans of 2 TB or more allow unlimited compatible recording cameras.

With iOS 27, Apple also introduced AI tools that can describe events in certain recordings and search using natural requests such as “when a package was left”. For a blind person, receiving a written description of what took place in front of a camera could be particularly useful. This analysis requires a compatible Apple Intelligence device, the necessary home setup and at least 2 TB of iCloud+.

Here is another distinction: the number of cameras that can record is not the number eligible for intelligent descriptions. At 2 TB, that analysis covers one camera; at 6 TB, two; and at 12 TB, five. Automatic descriptions can be wrong and should never be the only basis for a significant security decision.

Films and games included?

Here is our favourite word again: included. Apple has expanded iCloud+ to include Apple TV and Apple Arcade at no additional charge in more than one hundred countries and territories, even on the 50 GB plan. India, Pakistan and Mozambique are among the examples.

It is not universal. Apple's official list includes countries such as India, Pakistan, Mozambique, Saudi Arabia and Singapore, but does not include major markets such as the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom or European Union countries. Mexico is not on the published list either. A worldwide announcement is no guarantee that a particular account receives the bundle.

To check, open Settings, your name, Subscriptions, iCloud+. If Apple TV and Apple Arcade are included for your region, they should appear there. Apple One is a different bundle of services, with its own plan and country rules.

Before paying, it is worth checking which services we actually use and what appears on our bill. Companies are very good at adding subscriptions together. We need not help them unnecessarily.

The limits of artificial intelligence

Apple says most iCloud+ plans provide expanded access to some Apple Intelligence features subject to daily usage limits, including certain cloud-based image creation and editing tools. Apple has not published precise numbers of queries or generations per plan. The company has also said that greater paid access may be introduced later.

An iCloud+ subscription does not guarantee unlimited Siri AI access, bypass regional restrictions or turn an incompatible iPhone into a compatible one. Buying more cloud storage cannot upgrade the phone's processor, no matter how willing Apple might be.

How safe is our information?

Apple protects iCloud information with encryption and other safeguards. Encryption turns information into a form that requires the proper keys to read. Some categories, including Keychain passwords, already use end-to-end encryption. Other categories do not necessarily have that level of protection under standard settings.

Advanced Data Protection extends end-to-end encryption to additional categories such as device backups, photos and documents. Here is good news: it does not require a paid iCloud+ plan. The free plan qualifies too, provided the other requirements are met.

There is an important responsibility attached. If it is enabled, Apple may not hold the keys needed to restore certain data after we lose access. That is why a suitable recovery method must be configured. Without one, recovering that information may be impossible. Greater security sometimes asks a little more care of us.

If we stop paying, do we lose everything?

Suppose we subscribe to 200 GB and use 120 GB. We cancel and return to the free 5 GB. Apple does not necessarily erase everything immediately, but we cannot expect to keep synchronising 120 GB indefinitely with only 5 GB available. New backups, photo uploads and synchronisation may stop until we reduce usage or buy sufficient storage. Premium benefits also end when no longer included.

Before cancelling, download and independently back up anything important, then free enough space or choose a suitable plan. Cancelling a subscription and closing an Apple Account are entirely different operations.

Which plan do we really need?

Someone mainly using Contacts, Notes and a few documents may manage with the free 5 GB. A modest backup and photo collection might fit in 50 GB. Multiple devices, videos and large photo libraries may make 200 GB or more sensible. The 2, 6 and 12 TB plans are for much heavier needs, including families and advanced use. The most expensive plan is not automatically the right one.

Check actual usage in Settings, your name, iCloud. To see what occupies the iPhone's physical storage, open Settings, General, iPhone Storage. It is also worth verifying when the last device backup completed. Paying for capacity does not automatically mean backup is enabled and working: the relevant power, connection and configuration conditions must be met.

We can also make local backups using Windows or a Mac without buying iCloud storage. And iCloud Drive does not replace a complete Mac backup made with Time Machine.

In the end, the point is not to subscribe to everything a company offers. It is to know where we keep our information and how we would recover it if the phone disappeared tomorrow.

Synchronisation is not an independent backup. Cloud storage does not enlarge the memory inside a phone, and paid benefits differ across countries. Understanding those distinctions helps us make sensible choices.

Technology is far more useful when we understand what it actually does for us. And if we manage to stop that “iCloud storage is full” message appearing, I doubt anyone will complain.

JAWS 2027: a screen reader learning how to lend us a hand

We have been using JAWS to read computer screens for so many years that it is easy to forget how extraordinary it is to work with Windows, write documents, browse websites or use spreadsheets without seeing the display.

But we know the other side of that experience too.

We visit a website and JAWS cannot find a button. We try to complete a form and get trapped in its calendar. We open an application and discover that the developers have forgotten to label half a dozen controls. So out come the familiar manoeuvres: Tab, arrow keys, the JAWS cursor, the JAWS mouse and whatever other incantations we remember. Sometimes they get us through. Sometimes we close the page, wondering whether its designers ever tested it with a screen reader.

Vispero wants to improve this situation, or at least make a start. It is working on an artificial-intelligence agent that does more than describe a screen: it tries to take action when we cannot. The project is associated with JAWS 2027, the next major version of the screen reader.

Before anything else, let us answer the question on everybody's lips.

Can we install JAWS 2027 yet?

As of October 21, 2026, there is no public stable version of JAWS 2027. The latest listed release is JAWS 2026 build 2026.2609.84, from late September, and I have not found a general public 2027 beta on the official download page.

That does not mean Vispero has been sitting idle. It has already demonstrated features in presentations and podcasts. Freedom Scientific has also scheduled a webinar about JAWS, ZoomText and Fusion 2027 for Thursday, November 12, 2026. The event starts at noon US Eastern Standard Time, which is 17:00 UTC; 18:00 in mainland Spain, 17:00 in the Canary Islands, 14:00 in Argentina, noon in Colombia and 11:00 in Mexico City.

Major JAWS releases have often appeared in late October or early November, so a similar timetable would not be surprising. But precedent is not an official release date. In the meantime, be suspicious of any website claiming to offer a “final JAWS 2027” installer. If it is not provided by Freedom Scientific, leave it alone.

When JAWS does not just read, but acts

A conventional screen reader speaks or displays in braille the information the operating system and applications expose. On a properly built website, JAWS identifies headings, links, buttons, edit fields and other controls. But what if an application does not supply that information correctly? Everyone who can see the screen can spot the button, while we cannot reach it through the usual accessible interface.

That is where Vispero's new agent comes in. The word “agent” may sound grand, but the idea is fairly straightforward. Instead of merely answering our questions, it examines a situation and attempts specific actions on our computer. Based on the demonstrations, it can combine accessibility data with a visual interpretation of the screen and may even click the place where it finds a control. It then reports what it managed to do.

This is not a magic wand that makes everything accessible. The technology is still being developed and its limitations remain to be seen. But the demonstrations give us a clear idea of what Vispero is trying to achieve.

The button that is nowhere to be found

In a demonstration discussed on episode 268 of FSCast, a user was completing an online survey. They chose an answer and wanted to move to the next question. They explored the page, tried Tab and searched the controls. Nothing. As far as JAWS was concerned, the Continue button did not exist.

A sighted person would have recognised and pressed it immediately. But the site had not labelled or implemented it in a way a screen reader could understand. The user asked the agent why they could not proceed. The tool analysed the page, identified the inaccessible control, activated it and explained why JAWS had not been able to find it.

The interesting part is not only escaping the blockage but understanding what caused it. The website should have been accessible in the first place, of course. In the meantime, an alternative way forward could prevent us from abandoning a task.

And while we are talking about getting stuck, selecting a date in an online calendar can still be quite an expedition. Some let us type day, month and year. Others offer controls that are practically unreachable. A few seem designed specifically to test our patience. If the agent can deal with those, it will already have earned a good deal of goodwill.

Excel, Word and even the keyboard

Other demonstrations have involved desktop software. In one, the agent navigated an awkward Excel spreadsheet. In another, it helped format a Word document. A third involved Logitech's keyboard configuration software. The user wanted the F1 to F12 keys to return to their traditional functions instead of their multimedia functions. The agent explored the application's interface, found the setting and changed it while explaining what it was doing.

Until now, we could ask Vispero Companion how to do something and receive instructions. The new agent attempts to carry out those instructions, subject to its capabilities and our permission.

None of this means we should forget keyboard shortcuts. If a task takes two key combinations, we hardly need to hold a lengthy debate with an AI. Technology should help us work, not convince us that all our existing skills have become obsolete. Nor should we assume the agent will handle any obscure device and configuration utility we throw at it.

What we already have, and what is new

We must distinguish the different products and functions. JAWS already includes AI-powered tools: Picture Smart AI describes photographs and other images; Page Explorer, within Vispero Companion, summarises a website and helps explain its structure. Vispero Companion also answers questions about JAWS commands and selected applications.

Picture Smart AI, Page Explorer and Vispero Companion are not inventions of the 2027 release. Several were already available or improved during 2026. The new step is moving from explaining how we might do something to attempting it on request.

An AI that needs permission

There is a delicate issue here. When using JAWS conventionally, we issue the instructions: we press a key, activate a button and decide when to proceed. When an agent acts for us, we need clear boundaries. Changing a page's orientation is not the same as submitting a form containing personal information or approving a purchase.

In the demonstrations, the agent responds to a user request and explains its actions. Ideally it should be stoppable and request confirmation before significant operations, but that must be verified in real use rather than assumed from a presentation. AI systems can misunderstand requests or choose the wrong control. We should examine anything involving money, personal information or important documents before authorising it.

These tools also rely on cloud-based artificial intelligence. That means information needed to analyse the screen may be processed away from our computer. Vispero will need to explain clearly what information is transmitted and how it is protected. Having JAWS read a document locally is one thing; sending its contents to an external service to take action is another.

Will it replace the JAWS we know?

No. Vispero describes the agent as an additional aid, not a replacement for the screen reader. We will still navigate, write and use braille displays as before. When an inaccessible screen defeats us, we may have another kind of assistance available. The real scope will become clearer once the public product arrives.

Will we need an internet connection?

Ordinary JAWS reading does not depend on internet access, and many traditional functions work offline. The agent, on the other hand, appears to depend on online services and whatever account and licensing requirements Vispero establishes. I have seen no announcement of a fully offline version. Fortunately, losing Wi-Fi will not suddenly stop JAWS from reading Windows.

The United States, Spain and the rest of the world

In the United States, JAWS 2026 requires signing in with a Vispero account, and since May certain AI features have also required an account whose user is at least thirteen. The proposed agent fits into that broader arrangement.

When will it be available to those of us who use JAWS in Spanish? That remains unanswered. To my knowledge, Vispero has not announced a timetable for Spain or Latin America, or confirmed Spanish-language support from day one. We must not promise that every Spanish-speaking user will receive the agent as soon as JAWS 2027 launches. Region, language, account and licence may all be relevant.

If a feature is missing when the new installer eventually appears, it does not necessarily mean the installation failed. It may still be in testing or not yet enabled in that region.

Will it cost extra?

JAWS has different licensing arrangements depending on country and user: perpetual licences with maintenance, subscriptions, and agreements for businesses, schools and other organisations. Commercial terms, usage limits and the geographical availability of the agent are still to be confirmed.

Before renewing a licence solely for this feature, check its supported languages, roll-out, limitations and price. There is no reason to spend money blindly.

What about the ordinary JAWS improvements?

Somebody may quite reasonably be thinking that all this AI sounds interesting, but what they really want is to work with Word, Excel, Windows and web browsers without mishaps. We still need stability, reliable tables, focus that stays where it belongs, usable forms and good braille-display compatibility. Vispero continues to work on these things.

The precise improvements to navigation, Office, performance and braille will be clearer in the official release notes. ZoomText 2027 and Fusion 2027 are on the horizon too; we should not automatically attribute their particular features to JAWS.

How will we update when it appears?

The official download page is:

https://support.freedomscientific.com/Downloads/JAWS

Once JAWS 2027 appears there, we can read its release notes and check the installer. Before updating, verify the computer requirements and the upgrade rights attached to the licence. If the computer is essential for work or study, there is nothing wrong with reading the documentation before installing a brand-new version. The update will not run away if we leave it alone for a little while.

Freedom Scientific allows public versions to run in demonstration mode. JAWS 2026 permits forty-minute sessions, but the conditions for JAWS 2027 should be confirmed after release.

What we know, and what remains uncertain

So far, we have seen demonstrations of an agent that interprets a screen and attempts actions when the user asks. Examples include an inaccessible web button, Excel, Word and keyboard configuration software. The November 12 webinar should tell us more.

We do not yet have a confirmed public release date, a complete list of improvements or a timetable for the agent in Spanish. A well-controlled demonstration must not be confused with a feature anybody can download tomorrow.

Those of us who have used JAWS for years have learned plenty of ways out of a difficult situation: keyboard commands, cursor modes, settings and scripts. If AI gives us one more way through when those methods fail, so much the better.

But the companies building websites and applications remain responsible for accessibility. We should not need artificial intelligence to press a button that could have been programmed properly in the first place.

In the end, we do not need a computer that puts on impressive demonstrations. We need to finish the job we started. And if we can do that without arguing with an online calendar for half an hour, that will be a meaningful improvement.

Ten more technology stories worth our attention

After our three main reports, here is a quicker look at what October has brought us. We have calls involving people without WhatsApp, driverless taxis, earthquake alerts and even a lock that revives the old tradition of winding things up by hand. There are also developments in Windows, audiobooks, digital health, AI and privacy. None of these stories is here simply to fill a numbered space: each has something useful to tell us, even when a product is limited to certain countries or will not arrive until next year.

1. WhatsApp will let us call people who do not even have WhatsApp

October 14, 2026

Meta has announced a genuinely useful change. Until now, WhatsApp calls normally required both parties to have the app and an account. That is starting to change.

With guest calling, an account holder can create a link from the Calls tab and send it to someone who does not use WhatsApp. The guest opens it in a computer browser, enters a name and waits in a waiting room until the host admits them. Both individual and group calls are supported, with up to thirty-two participants and end-to-end encryption.

Guests cannot see other participants' phone numbers, send messages or invite additional people themselves. They can share their screen, raise a hand and use reactions. The host can remove them at any moment. A WhatsApp account is needed to create the link, but not to join as a guest.

Meta is also planning Google Calendar and Outlook integrations so that WhatsApp call links can be added to meeting invitations before the end of the year.

This may help families, associations, meetings and people who prefer other services. At least we might escape the familiar opening line: “Before we can talk, you'll have to install this.” Sometimes setting up the call takes longer than the conversation.

2. Waymo prepares to bring driverless taxis to Madrid

October 20, 2026

There was a time when getting into a car without a driver sounded like science fiction. Now Waymo, Alphabet's autonomous-driving company, has announced that it is coming to Spain, starting in the Madrid region.

The company hopes to welcome its first passengers in 2027, but that does not mean we can open an app tomorrow and summon an empty vehicle. Initially, electric Jaguar I-PACE cars will drive with a person on board while their systems are adapted to local streets and traffic. Further validation will follow, and commercial service will depend on meeting the necessary requirements. Business press reports place the early activities in Madrid itself, Alcobendas and Getafe. Waymo says it has already completed more than twenty million passenger trips in other markets.

For blind passengers, such services could offer enormous possibilities for independent mobility. But a vehicle that can drive itself is not enough. We must also be able to request it through an accessible app, find and identify it, get inside and obtain help if something goes wrong. A car that finds our address perfectly is not much use if we cannot find its door.

For now, what is confirmed is the preparation work. Passenger service will have to wait for the 2027 timetable.

3. Android expands earthquake warnings to more than two hundred countries and regions

October 15, 2026

Some technology stories entertain us; others help us work; a few might save lives. This one belongs in the third group.

Google says its Android Earthquake Alerts System is expanding from ninety-eight countries and regions to more than two hundred. Panama and Guatemala are among the newly supported places. The system uses sensors in large numbers of Android phones to detect vibrations consistent with an earthquake. When the necessary conditions are met, it sends alerts to devices in the affected area. Sometimes a warning may arrive seconds before the strongest shaking reaches the user's location.

This is not earthquake prediction. Google cannot tell us that an earthquake will happen tomorrow. It detects one that has already begun and tries to warn people who have not yet felt it. The company says more than a billion warnings have been issued since 2021. In the coming months, its “Be Ready” notification is expected to gain a map of the detected shaking area and an estimated magnitude.

Coverage, region and other factors affect the service, and not every earthquake will trigger an alert. Still, a few seconds can matter. Accessibility is not a luxury here: a safety warning must be communicated clearly to someone who cannot look at the screen, rather than being lost among ordinary notifications.

4. Amazon launches Android tablets with full Google Play access

October 8, 2026

Amazon has sold Fire tablets for years: inexpensive devices designed mainly for consuming content through Amazon's ecosystem. Their Fire OS software was based on Android but used Amazon's own app store, making certain familiar apps harder to install.

Now the company has announced the Alexa Tablet family: Alexa Tablet 8, Tablet 11 and Tablet 12 Pro. These run Android with full access to Google Play, making it possible to download Gmail, YouTube, Netflix and many other apps directly. Alexa+, Amazon's newer voice assistant, is integrated, alongside Kindle, Audible, Prime Video and Amazon Music. Of course, having access to a service does not make every subscription or piece of content free.

The tablets are available in multiple markets. They reached the United States, Mexico and Canada on October 14, with shipments beginning on October 19 in the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy and Spain. For comparison, US starting prices are $229.99, $329.99 and $499.99; in Spain, they start at €229.99, €329.99 and €499.99. Local prices should be checked in the relevant national store.

For screen-reader users, the real questions involve the menus, home screen, TalkBack and Amazon's own apps. Access to Google Play does not automatically give a device exactly the same accessibility experience as a Google Pixel. Still, this is a major change: the new family is much closer to conventional Android than the older Fire tablets.

5. Spotify wants to become a vast audiobook library too

October 7, 2026

When we hear Spotify, we think of music and podcasts. The company would also like us to think of books.

It has announced plans to expand audiobooks from twenty-two markets to more than one hundred and eighty before the end of 2026, adding catalogues in Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Polish and other languages. In Spain, for example, the Spanish-language catalogue is expected to include more than 45,000 titles. The roll-out is gradual, so readers may not yet see the new service on their accounts.

Access will differ by region. In countries including Spain, Mexico, Chile, Colombia, Peru and Portugal, eligible Premium subscribers will receive twelve audiobook listening hours a month; additional time will be sold through an Audiobooks+ add-on. In places such as Argentina, Audiobooks+ will be the primary way in. Individual books can also be purchased. Finding a title in Spotify therefore does not mean we can listen to it without limits under our existing subscription.

The company is also improving Page Match, a feature for switching from a printed or electronic book to its audio version. Users will be able to scan a book's ISBN barcode and enter a page number to find the corresponding spot in the audiobook.

For those of us who enjoy listening to books, the growing catalogue is only part of the story. Search, chapter navigation and reliable playback position matter just as much, particularly with a screen reader. Thousands of titles are wonderful, but hunting down the chapter where we stopped should not require a police investigation.

6. Microsoft plans a Windows where AI also works inside the computer

October 7, 2026

Microsoft has described its next stage of Windows as “hybrid intelligence”. Do not let the name frighten you.

Until recently, many AI tools sent our questions to remote servers. Modern computers increasingly have processors capable of carrying out some AI tasks locally. Microsoft's idea is to combine both approaches: handle suitable tasks on the computer and call on remote processing when more power is needed.

With our permission, Copilot could consult information on the computer itself and help with actions involving files and apps, such as moving items or changing settings. Microsoft describes supervised containers in which AI agents have limited permissions. Some components are already available to developers, with additional Copilot features due over the coming months.

That does not mean any old Windows 11 PC can run all those models without a connection. The announcements particularly concern Copilot+ PCs and depend on the hardware and the requirements of each feature.

JAWS and NVDA users will need to see how these agents cooperate with their screen readers. If an assistant is going to work on our documents, we need to know what it is doing and how to stop it. A computer working for us can be marvellous. One deciding to do things we never requested would be another story altogether.

7. Ring introduces a smart lock you can recharge by turning a wheel

October 7, 2026

A battery-powered smart lock is wonderfully convenient until we reach our front door, the battery is flat and we are stuck on the wrong side.

Ring, Amazon's home-security brand, has announced the Ring Smart Lock, with a small wheel that can generate power manually when the battery is depleted. A few turns are designed to produce enough energy to unlock the door. It offers fingerprint access, a keypad, the Ring app and Alexa, along with remote monitoring and temporary guest permissions.

It is not on sale yet. Ring expects an early-2027 US launch, with reports suggesting a price around $249, subject to necessary approvals. There is no announced release date for Europe or Latin America.

Voice and phone control may offer accessibility benefits, but the controls and the emergency procedure during connection failures need testing. Opening a door with AI is one thing; explaining to a locksmith that our lock needs winding up is quite another.

8. Samsung wants to improve our sleep with less data

October 14, 2026

Smartwatches have been telling us for years how long we slept, whether we woke up during the night and other things we probably suspected before looking at the numbers.

Samsung has announced an update to Samsung Health. Its sleep coaching programme can now start with three days of data from the previous two weeks, instead of the former requirement of seven days within a month. After a short questionnaire, the app suggests a programme lasting four to eight weeks and introduces a Sleep Balance chart summarising five measures of duration and regularity.

For runners, the running coach expands from ten to twelve levels and integrates imported Strava routes. The update is rolling out from October, but individual features may require particular Galaxy Watch models, Android versions or Samsung devices. The information is intended as guidance, not a medical diagnosis.

The data may help us understand habits. What no smartwatch has managed yet, however clever, is persuading someone who goes to bed at three in the morning to put their phone down several hours earlier.

9. Gemini gets personal in the car

October 20, 2026

Google has announced Personal Intelligence for Gemini in Android Auto. With our consent, the assistant can consult Gmail, Calendar, Photos, Maps, YouTube and Search to answer questions about our own lives. If we have an appointment but cannot remember the address, for example, we could ask it to find the location and guide us there. The feature is optional: it must be enabled in Gemini settings, with permission granted to the chosen apps.

The roll-out began on October 20 in Android Auto, with cars featuring Google built-in expected to follow over the coming months. For now, it is limited to personal adult accounts and is unavailable in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Nigeria.

Gemini may still operate as a voice assistant in regions where this access to personal Google information is restricted. It is the personal-data layer that is missing there, not necessarily the entire assistant.

Asking a car to take us to an address is not the same as allowing it to enter our accounts to find that address. It pays to know which kind of permission we are giving.

10. ChatGPT lets us protect conversations with an extra lock

October 20, 2026

OpenAI has introduced Locked Chats. We can choose a conversation and protect it with a four-digit account PIN or, on supported devices, device authentication. Locked conversations are hidden from the ordinary history, pinned chats and search, and appear in a separate area. The lock does not create a completely isolated memory: depending on settings, locked chats can use relevant information from ordinary Memory. New information remembered while a chat is locked, however, is not automatically shared with regular chats.

The feature is being rolled out to Free, Go, Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu accounts across the web, desktop and iOS and Android apps. Once it reaches our account, a Lock chat option should appear in the conversation's menu.

It may be useful for personal documents, private projects and information we would rather hide from someone else using our device. But locking is not deleting: OpenAI says it does not add end-to-end encryption, alter retention periods or change our setting for model improvement. And there is one serious warning: resetting a forgotten account PIN permanently deletes the locked chats, memories stored only within them and content protected only by that PIN. Make sure you keep the PIN safe.

Not everybody needs to know what we ask an AI. Some conversations, frankly, are best kept between ourselves and the assistant.
