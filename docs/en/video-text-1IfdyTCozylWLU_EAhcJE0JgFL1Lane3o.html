The biggest iPhone confusion is called iCloud

Accessible written version of a Canal TifloAcosta video. Watch the original video on YouTube.

Three concepts that are not the same thing

Tony starts with a very common source of confusion: iPhone storage, iCloud syncing, and iCloud Backup are often treated as though they were the same thing.

The purpose of the video is to separate them clearly. iPhone storage is the physical storage inside the device. iCloud syncing keeps selected data up to date between iCloud and devices signed in to the same Apple Account. iCloud Backup preserves information and settings that can be used when restoring or replacing a device.

The iPhone's physical storage

Tony opens Settings > General > iPhone Storage.

He compares internal storage to a cupboard with a fixed capacity. Everything stored locally—apps, videos, audio, files, and other data—uses part of that space.

On the iPhone used in the demonstration, VoiceOver reports 109.41 GB used out of 128 GB, leaving 18.56 GB available.

The breakdown includes:

  • Apps: 53.16 GB.
  • Mail: 8.54 GB.
  • Photos: 209.1 MB.
  • iOS: 25.45 GB.
  • System Data: 21.85 GB.

Tony remarks that apps take up a large amount of space because he installs many of them for testing and research.

What System Data means

In the video, Tony describes System Data as space that builds up through normal use, app installations, and iOS updates, and says that a full restore can significantly reduce it.

The practical idea he wants to convey is that not everything using storage belongs neatly to one visible app and that, when this category becomes large, it can be difficult to identify what can be removed directly.

An update note later in the document clarifies that System Data is broader than simply accumulated leftovers.

Offloading unused apps

On the storage screen, Tony shows the recommendation Offload Unused Apps.

He explains that this removes an app when storage is needed while keeping its documents and data. If the app is needed again, it can be downloaded without losing the preserved local information.

The aim is to recover the space used by the app itself without necessarily deleting its associated data.

The difference between app size and Documents & Data

Tony moves through the list of apps sorted by storage usage. Items shown during the demonstration include:

  • On My iPhone: 8.96 GB.
  • Mail: 8.54 GB.
  • WhatsApp: 6.72 GB.
  • Audio Converter: 2.31 GB.

He opens Audio Converter to demonstrate an important distinction. VoiceOver reports an App Size of 42.1 MB, while Documents & Data occupies 2.27 GB.

Most of the storage used in this example therefore comes from accumulated data rather than the app itself.

Offload App versus Delete App

Tony shows two different actions:

  • Offload App: removes the app from the device while preserving its documents and data.
  • Delete App: removes the app and the local data that iOS treats as associated with it.

In the example, Tony uses this difference to show why completely deleting an app with a large amount of Documents & Data can recover far more space than simply offloading it.

He also notes that apps connected to cloud-storage services such as Google Drive, MEGA, or Dropbox can accumulate a significant amount of local content or cache even though the original files live online.

Moving from phone storage to iCloud

After reviewing physical storage, Tony returns to the main Settings screen, opens his Apple Account, and selects iCloud.

This begins the second part of the explanation: iCloud storage is not the same space as the physical storage inside the iPhone.

An iPhone may have plenty of free local space while iCloud is full, or the reverse.

What iCloud syncing means

Tony describes syncing as the mechanism that keeps selected information up to date between iCloud and devices signed in to the same Apple Account.

Examples include Photos, Contacts, Calendars, Notes, Reminders, and Mail.

The essential point is that changes propagate. Creating or editing an item on one device can update it elsewhere, and deleting data that is syncing with iCloud can also remove it from the other connected devices.

This is why Tony stresses that syncing is not the same as keeping an independent backup copy.

The iCloud storage plan

Inside iCloud, Tony reviews his storage plan. In the demonstration he has a 200 GB paid plan.

This space can be shared by different kinds of iCloud content, including files, photos, synced data, and backups, depending on the user's configuration.

He also reminds viewers that Apple provides a basic amount of free iCloud storage and offers larger paid plans.

Accessing iCloud data on the web

Tony shows the Access iCloud Data on the Web setting.

When web access is allowed, he explains that a user can sign in at www.icloud.com with their Apple Account and access various types of iCloud content through a browser.

This can be especially useful when working from a computer that is not a Mac.

Hide My Email

Tony briefly opens Hide My Email. His account shows 93 addresses.

He explains that the feature creates random addresses that forward messages to the user's personal inbox, allowing people to sign up for services without giving out their real address directly.

The goal is greater privacy and less exposure of the primary email address.

Private Relay

Tony also mentions Private Relay as an iCloud privacy feature connected with web browsing.

He does not explore it in depth because the video's main purpose remains the difference between storage, syncing, and backup.

Custom Email Domain and plan management

The iCloud settings also include a Custom Email Domain option and controls for managing the current storage plan.

Tony explains that the storage level can be changed from this area and also mentions Apple One, which bundles several Apple services into a single subscription.

iCloud Backup

Tony then reaches the third key concept.

He uses the analogy of a frozen photograph of the device to distinguish a backup from continuous syncing. The comparison is intended to show that a backup is a recovery point rather than information that changes immediately every time something is edited.

A backup can help recover information and settings when the device is lost, damaged, or replaced with another iPhone.

Syncing and backup work together without duplicating everything

There is an important detail: information that already syncs regularly with iCloud does not need to be stored again in the same way inside the device backup.

For example, when iCloud Photos is enabled, photos are maintained through syncing. When Messages in iCloud is enabled, messages follow that system as well. iCloud Backup mainly covers device information and settings that are not already being regularly synced with iCloud.

Syncing and backup are therefore separate but complementary mechanisms.

What a backup needs

Tony explains that enough available iCloud storage is required for a backup to complete. If iCloud is full, the backup can fail even when the iPhone still has plenty of free physical storage.

He also explains that automatic backups happen when the required conditions are met, normally while the device is locked, connected to power, and connected to a suitable network.

Update note — September 8, 2026

Current iPhone capacity. The video mentions entry-level models starting at 128 GB. That no longer applies to the current generation: the iPhone 17 starts at 256 GB.

System Data. It should not be understood simply as “leftovers” from apps and software updates. Apple includes items such as Siri voices, fonts, dictionaries, logs, indexes, caches, and other system resources in this category. iOS automatically manages some temporary and cached data when space is needed. A restore may change the size of System Data, but it is not accurate to describe restoring as the only general way to clear it.

Offloading an app. Apple still provides an option that removes an app while preserving its documents and data. The wording can vary by iOS version and region.

Deleting an app. Removing an app deletes the app and its associated local data from the device, but this does not necessarily erase information held in the app's own account or cloud service. If the app is reinstalled and the user signs in again, some information may return.

iCloud Backup. Apple currently defines iCloud Backup as a copy of device information and settings that are not already regularly syncing with iCloud. It should therefore not be interpreted as a literal clone of every item on the iPhone.

Backup connectivity. Automatic iCloud backups normally use Wi-Fi while the iPhone is locked and connected to power. On supported 5G models and carriers, backing up over cellular data may also be available.

iCloud.com. Web access remains available. On a computer, supported services include Photos, iCloud Drive, Notes, Mail, Calendar, Contacts, Reminders, and Find My, among others. Availability can vary depending on the device, account, and security configuration.

iCloud+. Apple still provides 5 GB of free iCloud storage and currently offers iCloud+ plans of 50 GB, 200 GB, 2 TB, 6 TB, and 12 TB. iCloud+ includes features such as Private Relay, Hide My Email, and Custom Email Domain.

Conclusion

Tony's central point is straightforward: the iPhone has its own physical storage; iCloud has separate cloud storage; syncing keeps selected information current across devices; and backup provides a recovery mechanism for information and settings that are not already covered by that syncing.

Understanding that distinction makes storage warnings, missing data, and backup problems much easier to interpret without mixing up systems that perform different jobs.
