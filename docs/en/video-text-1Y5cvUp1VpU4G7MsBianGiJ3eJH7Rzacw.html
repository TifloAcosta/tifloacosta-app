iPhone Battery: Everything You Need to Know

Accessible written version of a Canal TifloAcosta video.

Introduction

Tony Acosta presents a broad overview on Canal TifloAcosta of how to use, manage, and optimise the iPhone battery. The aim is to offer practical guidance for improving both daily battery life and long-term battery health without sacrificing functions that are necessary for accessibility or normal use of the device.

First recommendations about battery use

Before opening Settings, Tony shares several general ideas.

He explains that using VoiceOver continuously consumes more battery than using the iPhone without a screen reader. However, he makes it clear that this extra consumption is unavoidable for someone who needs VoiceOver, and that essential functions should not be disabled simply to save power.

He also stresses the importance of using suitable, certified, good-quality chargers. He warns that poor-quality chargers can harm both the battery and the device itself.

Another important point is heat. Tony explains that high temperatures damage the battery and the phone, so excessive heat exposure should be avoided whenever possible.

Regarding fast charging, he says that it produces more heat and places greater strain on the battery. For that reason, unless a quick charge is genuinely needed, he prefers normal charging.

Connectivity and power consumption

Tony opens Settings and goes through several functions that can affect battery use.

With iCloud and synchronisation, he explains that background activity consumes energy. As an example, he mentions synchronising large numbers of photos or videos, in addition to the battery used by the Camera app itself when taking pictures or recording video.

On Wi-Fi, Tony challenges the idea that simply leaving Wi-Fi enabled away from home necessarily causes major battery drain. He explains that the iPhone is designed to work with Wi-Fi and that Wi-Fi also contributes to location accuracy.

For Bluetooth, Tony points out that it may be essential for accessories such as Apple Watch, AirPods, Braille displays, speakers, or keyboards. He adds that Bluetooth power consumption is much lower than it was in earlier generations.

With mobile data and 5G, he explains that continuously searching for a 5G signal in areas with unstable coverage can increase battery consumption. In those situations, he recommends using an automatic network setting or 4G/LTE, depending on local coverage.

He also discusses Personal Hotspot. Sharing the iPhone’s data connection with other devices uses a significant amount of power, so he recommends using it with the iPhone connected to power when possible and turning it off when it is no longer needed.

The Battery section in Settings

Tony opens the Battery section in Settings. During the demonstration, VoiceOver announces that the battery level is 90%.

The charging information shows that the most recent charge reached 90% and took place 10 minutes earlier.

Tony uses this section to explain that charging to 100% is not always necessary, depending on the iPhone model and the available charging-management options.

He also warns that some battery-management features, charge-limit options, or related functions can vary between iPhone models. He mentions differences between an iPhone 15 Pro or later and earlier models, as well as possible regional differences.

Haptics and vibration

Tony explains that vibration and haptic feedback require energy to operate the relevant motors.

If a user does not need certain vibrations in everyday use, disabling some of them may provide a small battery saving. He does not present this as mandatory, but as an option each person can assess according to their own needs.

Location Services

Tony opens Privacy & Security and then Location Services.

He argues in favour of keeping location services enabled, but recommends reviewing permissions app by app. In particular, he suggests avoiding permanent “Always” access for apps that do not genuinely need it.

Depending on the app, he recommends options such as “While Using the App” or “Never”, based on what the app actually requires.

App Store and automatic updates

Tony explains that automatically checking for and downloading app updates in the background also uses energy.

As an alternative, he suggests disabling automatic App Store downloads and carrying out updates manually when convenient.

Email synchronisation

Tony also discusses email retrieval.

He explains that Push keeps a more constant connection with mail servers and can therefore use more battery than checking periodically.

As alternatives, he mentions checking every 15 or 30 minutes, or checking manually, except in situations where immediate email delivery is important.

Conclusion

Tony finishes by stressing that battery management should not become a limitation on using the iPhone.

Necessary features should continue to be used, especially when they are part of accessibility. If someone spends a long time away from home, he suggests carrying a power bank rather than giving up important functions.
