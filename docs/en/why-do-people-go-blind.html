Why Do People Go Blind?

An analysis without a white coat

By Tony Acosta · TifloAcosta

Let me say this from the start: I am not a doctor. I am not an ophthalmologist or an optometrist, and I am not even that neighbour who has a confident opinion about absolutely everything. So if an ophthalmologist has made it this far, relax: I am not planning to compete for your patients or open a clinic on Monday.

My thing is curiosity, and when it comes to eyes I have quite a lot of it. One day I asked myself a very simple question, one you may have asked too: why do people go blind around the world? What are the real causes?

I already had my little list in my head: cataracts, glaucoma, something to do with the retina, and not much else. That was about the limit of my medical science. The trouble started when I stopped looking only at the names and began looking at numbers, countries, ages and, above all, how much of all this vision loss could have been avoided.

That last part kept me thinking for quite a while. We will get there.

First, a word about the numbers

If you have read that more than 2 billion people worldwide have some form of vision impairment, do not panic: that does not mean 2 billion people are blind. That figure covers a very wide range, from relatively mild problems to people who need glasses and do not have them, and then much more serious loss of vision. Putting everything in the same bag makes for a frightening headline, but it does not explain very much.

International estimates put the number of blind people worldwide in 2020 at about 43.3 million. It is an estimate, of course. Nobody can go country by country and house by house counting eyes. Researchers work with uncertainty intervals and update their figures as better evidence appears, which is rather more honest than pretending every number is exact.

Of those 43.3 million people, about 23.9 million were women, and almost eight in ten were aged 50 or older. By 2050, the number of blind people is projected to reach about 61 million if demographic trends continue.

Put like that, it sounds as though everything is getting worse, but there is a catch. When people of the same age are compared, the prevalence of blindness has fallen over recent decades. The problem is that there are many more of us and we live longer. More older people means more people reaching the ages at which many eye diseases become common. We improve on one side and end up with more cases on the other. You came looking for a simple answer and statistics, being statistics, have decided to complicate your afternoon.

Cataracts, still right at the top

When we talk about blindness, cataracts have been near the top of the list for a very long time.

Inside the eye is the lens, which works as a natural focusing lens. With age it can become cloudy, making the image increasingly blurred, rather like looking through a bathroom window after a long hot shower.

So far, nothing surprising. What I find harder to understand is how something that can be operated on, and has been operated on for decades, can still leave so many people blind.

And the answer is not hidden in some mysterious corner of the eye. It is much more external: money, distance, specialists, hospitals and time.

WHO estimates that only one in two people worldwide who need cataract surgery have access to it. You can have an excellent operation waiting at the end of the road; if getting there means crossing half a country and having a bank account in excellent health, we have a problem.

The disease is the same. Everything around it changes the outcome.

Glaucoma: the nasty one

With glaucoma we are no longer talking about a lens becoming cloudy. The problem is the optic nerve, the cable that carries what the eye sees to the brain.

There are several types, and some have the bad manners to progress for years without introducing themselves. You can carry on with your life while losing parts of your visual field and may not notice for a long time. The worst part comes later: once the optic nerve has been damaged, that lost vision cannot be restored. Treatment can slow progression, but what has been lost is lost. That is why eye checks are mentioned so often, and in this case the repetition is justified.

There is an interesting detail. In places where cataracts are detected and operated on easily, glaucoma accounts for a larger share of blindness. Not necessarily because there is more glaucoma, but because other treatable causes lose ground and glaucoma is left closer to the front of the queue.

Age-related macular degeneration: the passing-years problem

The macula is a small area of the retina responsible for central vision. It is the part you use for reading, recognising a face or looking straight at something in detail.

When it fails, you may keep some peripheral vision and still have enormous difficulty reading or recognising faces. And age-related macular degeneration, as the name hints with very little subtlety, has quite a lot to do with getting older. As populations age, its importance is likely to grow.

Diabetic retinopathy: when the problem does not start in the eye

Over time, diabetes can damage the tiny blood vessels in the retina. At first there may be no obvious symptoms. Later can come bleeding, other retinal damage and loss of vision.

What struck me here is that the eye depends on a disease that does not even begin in the eye. Good diabetes control, regular checks and timely treatment can make a major difference. Another reminder that talking about blindness is not only talking about eyes.

Glasses, which look trivial until you do not have them

If we widen the lens and talk about vision impairment in general, one cause appears that is almost embarrassingly simple: uncorrected refractive error. Myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and company. In a great many cases, a pair of glasses solves the problem.

WHO estimates that two out of three people in low-income countries who need eyeglasses do not have access to them. I admit I found that hard to digest. We talk about gene therapy, implants, artificial intelligence and every other futuristic wonder, while millions of people could benefit from something as ordinary as a pair of glasses.

Sometimes we become so busy looking towards the future that we leave the glasses sitting on the table.

Not all of those cases lead to blindness, of course. But a very large part of avoidable vision impairment sits right there.

Where are the most people with vision loss?

This sounds like an easy question. It is not.

If we simply count people, Asia wins by a landslide, and the reason is not mysterious: a huge share of the world's population lives there. International estimates count hundreds of millions of people with vision loss across the major Asian regions.

But if instead of counting heads we look at the proportion of the population affected, parts of Africa fare much worse. Saying 'there are more cases here' is not the same as saying 'it is more common here'. A very large region may contain far more affected people and still have a lower prevalence than a much smaller one.

One figure sums up much of the inequality: WHO states that 90% of people with unaddressed vision impairment or blindness live in low- and middle-income countries. By this point it is becoming difficult to claim that this is only about disease.

Trachoma is still with us

There are causes of blindness that are barely mentioned in some countries and remain serious public-health problems in others. Trachoma is one of them.

It is caused by infection with the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis. Repeated infections over many years can scar the inside of the eyelid until the eyelashes turn inward. Imagine eyelashes rubbing against your eye all day, every day. Eventually the cornea can be damaged. Calling it unpleasant does not quite cover it.

Today, trachoma causes blindness or vision impairment in about 1.9 million people and remains a public-health problem in parts of 29 countries. Africa is still the most affected continent.

There is good news too. As of 4 September 2026, 33 countries had been validated by WHO as having eliminated trachoma as a public-health problem. The most recent was Timor-Leste.

River blindness

And just when you think you have learnt enough ways to go blind for one afternoon, along comes onchocerciasis.

It is also known as river blindness. It is caused by a parasite transmitted through repeated bites from infected blackflies that breed in fast-flowing rivers and streams. Hence the name.

It can cause severe skin problems and eye lesions that may end in permanent blindness. It mainly affects rural populations in sub-Saharan Africa and Yemen. The remaining endemic focus in the Americas is concentrated along the border between Brazil and Venezuela.

Mass treatment with ivermectin has dramatically reduced transmission in many places and remains the central strategy in elimination programmes.

Another example of how the map of blindness changes depending on where you put your finger.

Vitamin A and children

Severe vitamin A deficiency can still damage children's eyes, particularly where undernutrition and repeated infections are common. In its most serious forms it can damage the cornea and lead to blindness.

At this point the explanation stops being mainly about ophthalmology. We are talking about food, poverty, infection, vaccination and living conditions. And childhood blindness does not have the same causes as blindness in adults.

WHO notes that congenital cataract is a leading cause of vision impairment in low-income countries, while retinopathy of prematurity is more likely to be a leading cause in middle-income countries.

Retinopathy of prematurity affects babies born too early, before the blood vessels in the retina have finished developing. And there is a curious paradox here: it has become more important in some places because medicine has improved. Many more extremely premature babies now survive. We save more children, and that means we also have to become better at looking after what comes next.

Women and men

It is not the same for everyone here either. Of the estimated 43.3 million blind people in 2020, about 23.9 million were women.

Part of the difference is explained by women living longer and by many eye diseases becoming more common with age. But that is not the whole story. In some places economic, social and cultural inequalities can make it harder for women to reach an examination, treatment or surgery.

Trachoma shows the difference particularly starkly: WHO reports that the number of women blinded by trachoma is four times that of men, probably because women have closer and more frequent contact with infected children.

How much of this could be avoided?

And this is the figure that stopped me in my tracks.

WHO estimates that at least 1 billion people live with a vision problem that could have been prevented or has yet to be addressed. Again: that does not mean 1 billion blind people. The figure includes very different degrees and types of vision impairment. It is still an extraordinary number.

Unoperated cataracts, uncorrected refractive error, diseases that could have been detected earlier, and treatments that exist but are still out of reach.

I had always thought that the big challenge in blindness was finding cures for conditions that do not yet have one. That is part of it, obviously. But another very large part is much more down to earth: making sure that what we already know how to do actually reaches more people.

But not everything can be prevented

We should not swing too far in the other direction either. Not all blindness can be prevented and not all lost vision can be restored. There are inherited diseases, retinal degenerations, optic-nerve damage, injuries and other conditions for which there is no cure today. And some treatable diseases are diagnosed too late.

That is why another field sometimes seems to be pushed into the background when eye health is discussed: rehabilitation. When somebody has already lost their sight, medicine may have reached its limit, but life continues. Orientation and mobility, braille, screen readers, recognition apps, training and accessibility. None of that repairs a retina, nor does it pretend to. It helps a person continue living with as much independence as possible.

Anyone who has ever relied on a screen reader knows that is no small matter.

So what do I take away from all this?

I started by wanting to know which diseases cause the most blindness in the world. It sounded like the sort of question you could answer with a list, four diseases and move on. Apparently not.

There is an answer, and it is not especially complicated. Cataracts still account for a huge amount of blindness worldwide. Glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy are also major causes. And uncorrected refractive error weighs very heavily when we look at vision impairment more broadly.

But after all the numbers and maps, what stayed with me most was something else: the same disease does not have the same consequences for everybody. It matters whether a specialist is nearby, whether treatment exists and is affordable, whether you get there in time, whether you live in a rural area or a city and, sometimes, whether you can get hold of a simple pair of glasses.

I have not reached a medical conclusion, which was never the idea and would be well beyond me. What I have done is understand a little better why so much vision loss still exists despite everything we have achieved.

Sometimes the cure is missing. And sometimes the cure is there, but much too far away.
